## 1. A local finite-birational flatness criterion

**Claim.** Let A be a reduced Noetherian analytic local ring, and let B be its finite normalization inside its total ring of fractions Q(A). Then B is flat as an A-module **if and only if** B=A. Consequently, at a point x of a reduced complex analytic space, flatness of the finite normalization along the fiber over x forces X to be normal at x. The converse is immediate.

**Proof.** A finite flat module over the local Noetherian ring A is finite free. Say B is free of rank r. As B is birational, its localization at all nonzerodivisors is Q(A), so
\[
  B\otimes_A Q(A)\simeq Q(A).
\]
Because Q(A) is the finite product of the fraction fields of the minimal components of A, localization of a free module gives Q(A)^r. Hence r=1. Write B=Ab for some b in Q(A). The element 1 belongs to B, so 1=ab for an a in A. In Q(A), b=a^{-1} and a is a nonzerodivisor.

As b is integral over A, there is a monic equation
\[
 b^m+c_1b^{m-1}+\cdots+c_m=0,\qquad c_j\in A.
\]
Substitute b=a^{-1} and multiply by a^{m-1}:
\[
 a^{-1}=-(c_1+c_2a+\cdots+c_ma^{m-1})\in A.
\]
Thus b belongs to A and B=Ab=A. Conversely if B=A, it is free of rank one. This proof uses only the finite-birational description of normalization, not any result about smooth approximation. \(\square\)

For a reduced complex analytic germ, the standard local criterion for flatness of a finite morphism applies to this finite A-algebra B. The claim concerns **flatness of the normalization**; it does not say that arbitrary finite maps are nonflat. For instance a degree-two finite flat branched cover is usually not birational.

## 2. The analytic cusp: exact minimal free resolution

Let S=C\{x,y\}, f=y^2-x^3 and
\[
 A=S/(f)=\mathbb C\{t^2,t^3\}\subset B=\mathbb C\{t\},
 \qquad x=t^2,\quad y=t^3.
\]
As in integrated Example 9.4.10, B is the normalization, finite over A and of generic rank one. Every convergent power series in t has a unique expression as a sum of an even power series in x and t times another even power series in x; in particular B=A+At. Therefore the surjective A-linear map
\[
  \pi:A^2\longrightarrow B,\qquad (u,v)\longmapsto u+tv
\]
is well-defined. Its kernel is generated by the columns of
\[
  M=\begin{pmatrix}-y&-x^2\\ x&y\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Indeed, if u+tv=0 with u,v in A, then tv belongs to A. But A consists exactly of convergent t-series with vanishing coefficient of t. Accordingly tv belongs to A if and only if v has zero constant term, equivalently v belongs to the maximal ideal (x,y)A. Write v=xp+yq with p,q in A. Then
\[
  u=-tv=-yp-x^2q,
\]
so (u,v)=p(-y,x)+q(-x^2,y); these two vectors are precisely the columns of M. Thus \(\mathrm{coker}_A(M)=B\).

Now the **same** matrix satisfies the identity over the regular ambient ring S,
\[
 M^2=(y^2-x^3)I_2=fI_2,\qquad \det M=-f.
\]
It follows that over A, \(M^2=0\). Conversely, if v in A^2 is killed by M, lift it to \(\widetilde v\in S^2\). Then \(M\widetilde v=f w=M^2w\) for some w in S^2. Therefore \(M(\widetilde v-Mw)=0\) in S^2. Since S is a domain and det(M)=-f is nonzero, the S-linear matrix M is injective. Hence \(\widetilde v=Mw\), proving \(\ker_A M=\mathrm{im}_A M\).

This establishes the **complete exact 2-periodic free resolution**
\[
 \cdots\xrightarrow{M}A^2\xrightarrow{M}A^2
 \xrightarrow{M} A^2\xrightarrow{\pi} B\longrightarrow0.
\]
It is minimal: all four entries of M lie in the maximal ideal \(\mathfrak m=(x,y)\). Upon tensoring with the residue field \(k=A/\mathfrak m=\mathbb C\), all matrices vanish. Thus we obtain the **precise infinite Tor obstruction**
\[
 \boxed{\operatorname{Tor}^{A}_i(B,k)\simeq k^2
 \quad\text{for every }i\geq 0.}
\]
At i=0 this also follows directly from
\[
 B/\mathfrak m B=\mathbb C\{t\}/t^2\mathbb C\{t\}
 \simeq k\{1,t\}.
\]
So the special fiber of the normalization algebra has vector-space length two whereas the generic rank is one. In particular B is not flat or projective, and has infinite projective dimension over A. These are precise algebraic facts, not a statement about a topological fiber: the normalization of the cusp has only **one geometric point** over the cusp, as printed in Example 9.4.10.

## 3. A distinct two-branch crosscheck

For the node A'=C\{x,y\}/(xy), the normalization in the reading edition is B'=C\{x\}\oplus C\{y\}. Its generic rank on **each** irreducible branch is one, but
\[
 B'/\mathfrak m_{A'}B'\simeq k\oplus k.
\]
It is again nonflat. Unlike the cusp, its normalization has **two geometric points** above the singular point. Both examples therefore confirm that failure of flatness must not be confused with the number of geometric branches or with failure of the normalization to be a homeomorphism.
