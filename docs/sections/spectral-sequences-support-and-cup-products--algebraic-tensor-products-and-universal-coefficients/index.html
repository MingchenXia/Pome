10.67 Algebraic tensor products and universal coefficientsBC4B9432012E

BC4B9432012E

Until a principal ideal domain is explicitly assumed, $R$ is a commutative ring with identity. Modules are unital. All tensor products in this section are algebraic tensor products over $R$. An arbitrary direct-sum total complex is used:

\[ (K\otimes L)^ n=\bigoplus _{p+q=n}K^ p\otimes L^ q, \qquad D(k\otimes l)=d_ Kk\otimes l+(-1)^ p k\otimes d_ Ll. \]

Thus every element has finite support in the bidegrees, even when the complexes are unbounded. There is no topology or completion. A principal ideal domain is, in particular, an integral domain; the zero ring and principal ideal rings with zero divisors are not included in that later hypothesis.

10.67.1 Resolutions, torsion products and principal ideal domains331B46253D62

Lemma 10.67.2.BB113EE3ABCF Tensor product is right exact and commutes with direct sums and filtered direct limits in either variable. Filtered direct limits of modules are exact. A module is projective if and only if it is a direct summand of a free module. Projective modules are flat, and every module has a free resolution in nonnegative homological degrees.

Proof. For a submodule $A\subset B$, a balanced bilinear map on $(B/A)\times M$ is precisely a balanced bilinear map on $B\times M$ vanishing on $A\times M$. Consequently

\[ (B/A)\otimes M=(B\otimes M)/\operatorname {im}(A\otimes M). \]

The same universal property identifies tensoring a direct sum or a filtered direct limit with the direct sum or direct limit of the tensors. In a filtered limit, an element represented at one stage is zero if it becomes zero at a subsequent stage. More generally, any equality of finitely many representatives holds at a common subsequent stage: the filteredness conditions combine the finitely many indices and equalize the finitely many arrows. Applying this observation to a kernel element in a compatible system of exact sequences proves exactness at the middle term; lifting a representative proves surjectivity at the last term. It also proves that cohomology commutes with filtered limits. Direct sums are exact by the same finite-support argument.

Here projective means that every map from the module to the target of a surjection lifts through that surjection. For a free module, lift each basis vector; for a direct summand, restrict such a lift after projecting the free module to its summand. Conversely, a free surjection onto a projective module splits by lifting its identity. Tensoring a free module is a direct sum of copies of the original sequence and therefore preserves injections. The same is true for a direct summand, proving flatness. Finally, take a free surjection $P_0\to A$, then a free surjection onto its kernel, and repeat. The resulting augmented complex $P_\bullet \to A$ is exact, with $P_ q=0$ for $q<0$. $\square$

Proposition 10.67.3.E5490FB46564 Let $P_\bullet \to A$ and $Q_\bullet \to B$ be projective resolutions. The modules

\[ \operatorname {Tor}^ R_ q(A,B)=H_ q(P_\bullet \otimes B) \simeq H_ q\operatorname {Tot}(P_\bullet \otimes Q_\bullet ) \simeq H_ q(A\otimes Q_\bullet ) \]

are independent of the resolutions, naturally in both variables. They are also computed by any flat resolution in the resolved variable. They are symmetric, commute with filtered direct limits in either variable, and satisfy $\operatorname {Tor}_0^ R(A,B)=A\otimes B$. Each short exact sequence in either variable gives its natural long exact Tor sequence. A module $B$ is flat if and only if $\operatorname {Tor}_1^ R(A,B)=0$ for every $A$; in this case all positive Tor groups with $B$ vanish.

Proof. For a map $a:A\to A'$, a projective resolution $P$ of $A$, and any exact resolution $P'$ of $A'$, lift $a\epsilon :P_0\to A'$ to $P'_0$. If $f_0,\ldots ,f_{q-1}$ have been constructed, $f_{q-1}\partial $ has image in $\ker \partial '_{q-1}=\operatorname {im}\partial '_ q$. Projectivity of $P_ q$ gives $f_ q$. Two such lifts satisfy

\[ f_ q-g_ q=\partial '_{q+1}h_ q+h_{q-1}\partial _ q, \qquad h_{-1}=0. \]

Indeed, in degree zero their difference has image in $\ker \epsilon '$, so one chooses $h_0$. Inductively the difference $f_ q-g_ q-h_{q-1}\partial _ q$ has zero differential, by the previous-degree identity, and lifts to $P'_{q+1}$ by projectivity. After tensoring, this remains a homotopy. Lifts of identities and compositions are therefore unique on homology. This proves resolution independence and functoriality for projective resolutions.

The differential on the homological double complex is $\partial _ P\otimes 1+(-1)^ p1\otimes \partial _ Q$ on $P_ p\otimes Q_ q$. For fixed $p$, flatness of $P_ p$ gives the augmented exact column $P_ p\otimes Q_\bullet \to P_ p\otimes B$; the analogous augmented rows are exact by flatness of $Q_ q$. The homological version of the finite elimination in lemma 10.43.15 makes both augmentations quasi-isomorphisms. Explicitly, lift a degree-$n$ cycle of $P\otimes B$ to $P_ n\otimes Q_0$. Its horizontal differential has zero augmentation, so exactness of the next column supplies a correction in $P_{n-1}\otimes Q_1$. Continue through $P_{n-2}\otimes Q_2$ down to $P_0\otimes Q_ n$; the vertical sign $(-1)^ p$ is absorbed in each correction. There is no next horizontal term, so the result is a total cycle. For injectivity, subtract a lift of a boundary to make the augmentation of a total cycle zero. Kill its component in $P_ n\otimes Q_0$ by a vertical preimage in $P_ n\otimes Q_1$. The next component is vertically closed by the total cycle equation, so it too has a preimage. Continue down to horizontal degree zero. The sum of these finitely many preimages bounds the original cycle. These constructions are the augmentation maps' surjectivity and injectivity on homology, not choices defining those maps. Only $p,q\geq 0$ occur, so no unbounded convergence is used. The same argument works with an arbitrary flat resolution in place of $P$ or $Q$; it does not require comparison lifts from a flat module. The interchange

\[ P_ p\otimes Q_ q\longrightarrow Q_ q\otimes P_ p, \qquad x\otimes y\longmapsto (-1)^{pq}y\otimes x \]

commutes with the differentials and gives the natural symmetry. This fixes the symmetry convention also in positive Tor degree. Right exactness gives the assertion in degree zero.

For $0\to A\to A'\to A''\to 0$, the free resolution $Q$ of $B$ gives a short exact sequence

\[ 0\longrightarrow A\otimes Q_\bullet \longrightarrow A'\otimes Q_\bullet \longrightarrow A''\otimes Q_\bullet \longrightarrow 0. \]

The long exact sequence of lemma 10.43.3, reindexed homologically, applies. Its connecting map lifts a cycle to the middle complex and takes its differential in the first complex. This description proves naturality and fixes its sign. Symmetry gives the sequence in the other variable. If $B$ is flat, tensoring the augmented resolution $P\to A$ stays exact, giving positive-degree vanishing. Conversely, the part

\[ \operatorname {Tor}_1^ R(A'',B)\longrightarrow A\otimes B \longrightarrow A'\otimes B \]

shows that the stated vanishing implies preservation of every injection, hence flatness. Finally compute a filtered limit in the first variable using the fixed free resolution $Q$ of $B$. Tensor and homology commute with that limit by lemma 10.67.2. Symmetry treats the other variable. No assertion about arbitrary, unfiltered colimits is being made. $\square$

Lemma 10.67.4.3F097EB89283 Over a principal ideal domain every submodule of a free module is free, with no finite-generation assumption. Every finitely generated module is a finite direct sum of a free module and cyclic modules $R/aR$ with $a\ne 0$. A module is flat if and only if it is torsion free. A projective module is free, but a flat module need not be free.

Proof. Well order a basis $(e_\alpha )_{\alpha <\kappa }$ of a free module $F$, and set

\[ F_\alpha =\bigoplus _{\beta <\alpha }Re_\beta , \qquad N_\alpha =N\cap F_\alpha \]

for a submodule $N\subset F$. The $e_\alpha $ coordinate identifies $N_{\alpha +1}/N_\alpha $ with an ideal $I_\alpha $ of $R$. If this ideal is nonzero, choose its generator $a_\alpha $ and a vector $v_\alpha \in N_{\alpha +1}$ with that coordinate. Every element of $N_{\alpha +1}$ differs by a multiple of $v_\alpha $ from one in $N_\alpha $, and the sum is direct because $a_\alpha \ne 0$ in a domain. If $I_\alpha =0$, the two successive submodules agree. At a limit ordinal, $N_\lambda =\bigcup _{\alpha <\lambda }N_\alpha $, because each vector has finite support. Transfinite induction thus gives a basis consisting of the selected $v_\alpha $. For a submodule of $R^ m$ the same proof gives at most $m$ basis vectors.

A finitely generated module has a presentation $0\to N\to R^ m\to A\to 0$, and the preceding finite-rank case makes its relation matrix finite. We give the required diagonal reduction over a PID without assuming a Euclidean algorithm. Move a nonzero entry $a$ to the upper-left corner. For another entry $b$ in its column, let $g$ generate $(a,b)$, write $a=ga'$, $b=gb'$, and choose $u,v$ with $ua'+vb'=1$. The invertible row operation with matrix

\[ \begin{pmatrix} u & v \\ -b' & a' \end{pmatrix} \]

replaces the two entries by $g,0$. Column operations do the same for the first row. Whenever an entry was not divisible by the old pivot, its principal ideal strictly increases. An increasing chain of ideals in a PID stabilizes: its union is an ideal $(c)$, and its generator already belongs to one stage. Thus only finitely many such strict increases occur. When all entries in the first row and column are divisible by the pivot, ordinary row and column subtractions clear them. If a remaining entry $b$ is not divisible by the pivot, add its row to the first row and repeat the Bezout operation; this again strictly increases the pivot ideal. Eventually the pivot divides every entry and the first row and column are cleared. Repeat on the remaining smaller matrix. All of its entries remain divisible by the first pivot, so the successive nonzero diagonal entries can be taken to satisfy $a_1\mid a_2\mid \cdots $. The cokernel is the asserted direct sum; unit entries give zero summands. The inclusion matrix has independent columns over the fraction field, so its diagonal has no zero relation column.

If a finitely generated module is torsion free, all its nonzero cyclic torsion summands vanish, and it is free. Every torsion-free module is the filtered union of its finitely generated submodules, which are consequently free. Exactness of filtered limits and tensor shows that it is flat. Conversely, tensor the injection $R\xrightarrow {a}R$ for every $a\ne 0$ with a flat module: multiplication by $a$ remains injective, proving torsion-freeness. A projective module is a submodule of a free module and hence free. For the distinction from flatness, $\mathbb Q$ is a flat $\mathbb Z$-module, but is not free: every nonzero free abelian group has a basis vector not divisible by $2$, whereas every element of $\mathbb Q$ is divisible by $2$. $\square$

Corollary 10.67.5.9E1649673CCE Over a principal ideal domain, $\operatorname {Tor}_ q^ R(A,B)=0$ for $q\geq 2$. Write $A\star B=\operatorname {Tor}_1^ R(A,B)$ and $B[a]=\{ b\in B:ab=0\} $. For $a\ne 0$ one has

\[ (R/aR)\star B=B[a]. \]

If $a,b\ne 0$ and $g$ generates $(a,b)$, then

\[ (R/aR)\star (R/bR)\simeq R/gR. \]

If either $a=0$ or $b=0$, the latter torsion product is zero, since that factor is free. In contrast, $(R/aR)\otimes (R/bR)=R/(a,b)$ holds also with zero parameters. Every short exact sequence gives the six-term sequence

\[ \begin{aligned} 0\longrightarrow A\star B\longrightarrow A'\star B & \longrightarrow A''\star B\longrightarrow A\otimes B\\ & \longrightarrow A'\otimes B\longrightarrow A''\otimes B \longrightarrow 0. \end{aligned} \]

Proof. The kernel of a free surjection onto $A$ is free by lemma 10.67.4, so $A$ has a free resolution of length one. This proves the vanishing and truncates the long exact sequence. For $a\ne 0$ use the resolution $0\to R\xrightarrow {a}R\to R/aR\to 0$. If $a=ga'$, $b=gb'$ with $(a',b')=R$, the condition $ax\in bR$ is equivalent to $x\in b'R$: Bezout's identity for $a',b'$ proves the nontrivial implication. The map $R\to (R/bR)[a]$, $r\mapsto b'r\bmod bR$, is onto with kernel $gR$. This proves the cyclic formula and specifies its dependence on chosen generators. The tensor formula is right exactness applied to the same quotient presentation. The case $a=0$ uses the free module $R$, not the displayed length-one resolution with a zero, noninjective first map. $\square$

10.67.6 The natural tensor sequence and its splitting34E46E5EB84D

Theorem 10.67.7.669128A0733C Let $R$ be a principal ideal domain and let $K,L$ be complexes of $R$-modules. Suppose that every term of at least one complex is torsion free. There is a natural short exact sequence

ADD4029A2513 10.67.8
\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} 0\longrightarrow \bigoplus _{p+q=n}H^ p(K)\otimes H^ q(L) & \xrightarrow {\ \mu \ } H^ n(K\otimes L)\\ & \xrightarrow {\ \nu \ } \bigoplus _{p+q=n+1}H^ p(K)\star H^ q(L)\longrightarrow 0, \end{aligned} \end{equation}

where $\mu ([k]\otimes [l])=[k\otimes l]$. This assertion uses direct-sum totalization and needs no boundedness or finite-type assumption. The exact sequence splits as a sequence of modules; a natural choice of splitting is not part of the assertion.

Proof. We first prove exactness when the terms of $K$ are flat, leaving the splitting and full functorial normalization to the end of the proof. Write $Z^ p=\ker d_ K^ p$ and $B^ p=\operatorname {im}d_ K^{p-1}$. These submodules of torsion-free modules are torsion free, hence flat. Give $Z$ and $Q=K/Z$ zero differentials. The differential of $K$ identifies $Q^ p$ with $B^{p+1}$, so $Q^ p$ is flat. Consequently

\[ 0\longrightarrow Z\otimes L\longrightarrow K\otimes L \longrightarrow Q\otimes L\longrightarrow 0 \]

is an exact sequence of total complexes. Indeed, flatness of $Q^ p$ makes the original short exact sequence stay exact after tensoring by any $L^ q$, and direct sums preserve exactness. Flatness also identifies

\[ \begin{aligned} H^ n(Z\otimes L)& =\bigoplus _{p+q=n}Z^ p\otimes H^ q(L),\\ H^{n-1}(Q\otimes L)& =\bigoplus _{p+q=n}B^ p\otimes H^ q(L). \end{aligned} \]

For this computation, each $Z^ p\otimes L$ is simply a shifted complex with differential $(-1)^ p d_ L$; its cycles and boundaries are the corresponding tensors, by exactness of $Z^ p\otimes -$. Cohomology commutes with the direct sum.

Under these identifications, the connecting map from the second displayed group to the first is induced by $B^ p\hookrightarrow Z^ p$, with sign $+1$. To check it, a product class represented by $b\otimes l$ lifts to $k\otimes l$ with $d_ Kk=b$ and $d_ Ll=0$, and $D(k\otimes l)=b\otimes l$. Such classes generate the entire group, because $Q^ p$ is flat. The long exact sequence therefore identifies the kernel and cokernel needed for $H^ n(K\otimes L)$ with the kernel and cokernel of these inclusion maps in degrees $n+1$ and $n$, respectively. The flat presentation $0\to B^ p\to Z^ p\to H^ p(K)\to 0$ gives

\[ \begin{aligned} 0\longrightarrow H^ p(K)\star H^ q(L) \longrightarrow B^ p\otimes H^ q(L) \longrightarrow Z^ p\otimes H^ q(L)\\ \longrightarrow H^ p(K)\otimes H^ q(L)\longrightarrow 0. \end{aligned} \]

This proves the exact sequence and its asserted product map when $K$ is termwise flat. Changing the representative $k$ by $d_ Ka$ changes the product by $D(a\otimes l)$; changing $l$ by $d_ Lb$ changes it by $(-1)^ pD(k\otimes b)$. Thus the map is precisely the canonical cross product. If $L$ is termwise flat, the chain isomorphism $k\otimes l\mapsto (-1)^{pq}l\otimes k$ gives exactness with the roles reversed.

Two consequences of this exactness will be used before any splitting is claimed. If a termwise flat complex is acyclic, its tensor with every complex is acyclic. Also, tensoring an arbitrary acyclic complex with a termwise flat complex is acyclic. Both follow immediately because the two outer terms in the already proved sequence vanish. Hence tensoring a quasi-isomorphism between termwise flat complexes with any complex preserves quasi-isomorphisms; likewise tensoring any quasi-isomorphism with a termwise flat complex preserves it. For the first assertion use its acyclic, termwise flat cone. For the second use its acyclic cone and the other flat factor. With the convention

\[ \operatorname {Cone}(f)^ j=D^ j\oplus C^{j+1},\qquad d(y,x)=(d_ Dy+fx,-d_ Cx), \]

the identity of the two displayed summands identifies $\operatorname {Cone}(f)\otimes L$ with $\operatorname {Cone}(f\otimes 1)$; the vertical differential on the shifted component has exactly the required minus sign. The long exact cone sequence gives the usual acyclicity criterion. This argument covers unbounded direct sums and uses no spectral sequence.

For precision about naturality, there is a functorial, termwise free replacement $\epsilon _ C:P(C)\to C$ over a PID. For every $x\in C^ j$ take a copy of the disk complex $R\xrightarrow {1}R$ in degrees $j,j+1$, mapping its generators to $x,d_ Cx$. Their direct sum is a termwise free complex $F(C)$ with a degreewise surjection $F(C)\to C$. The kernel $N(C)$ is termwise free by lemma 10.67.4. Put

\[ P(C)=\operatorname {Cone}\bigl (N(C)\hookrightarrow F(C)\bigr ), \qquad \epsilon _ C(f,n)=\epsilon (f). \]

Its kernel is $\operatorname {Cone}(1_{N(C)})$, which is contractible: $h(a,b)=(0,a)$ satisfies $dh+hd=1$. Thus $\epsilon _ C$ is a quasi-isomorphism and a degreewise surjection. A map $C\to C'$ sends the disk indexed by $x$ to that indexed by its image, giving functorial maps on $F,N,P$ and a natural augmentation. No basis of $N(C)$ is chosen to define these maps; only its freeness is used.

Set $P=P(K)$ and $T=P(L)$. The natural map $P\otimes T\to K\otimes L$ is a quasi-isomorphism under the theorem's hypotheses. For example, when $K$ is termwise flat, factor it as $P\otimes T\to P\otimes L\to K\otimes L$. The first arrow uses flatness of $P$ and the acyclic cone of $T\to L$; the second uses that $P\to K$ is a quasi-isomorphism between termwise flat complexes. If $L$ is flat, use the factorization through $K\otimes T$ instead. Transport the exact sequence already constructed for $P,T$ through this natural isomorphism, identifying their cohomologies with those of $K,L$. This specifies $\nu $ naturally for all pairs in the theorem. When $K$ is flat it agrees with the preceding cycle–boundary construction, by its naturality under the two comparison maps. In particular it never requires natural choices of cycle complements.

Finally, in every free complex $P$ the sequence $0\to Z^ p(P)\to P^ p\to B^{p+1}(P)\to 0$ splits since its last term is free. Choose such complements and define

\[ e_ P:P\longrightarrow H(P),\qquad e_ P(z+s)=[z], \]

where the target has zero differential. This is a chain map: $d_ P(z+s)=d_ Ps$ is a boundary and is sent to zero. Define $e_ T$ in the same way. The actual chain map $e_ P\otimes e_ T:P\otimes T\to H(P)\otimes H(T)$ induces a retraction of its cross-product map, since it sends a product of cycle classes to that same tensor. Transport this retraction through the natural comparison just proved. An exact sequence $0\to A\xrightarrow {\mu }E\xrightarrow {\nu }B \to 0$ with a retraction $r:E\to A$ splits: for $b\in B$ choose any lift $x$ and set $s(b)=x-\mu r(x)$. This is independent of the lift and is an $R$-linear section. Applying this observation finishes the splitting proof. $\square$

Corollary 10.67.9.738A1519FEEF Over a principal ideal domain, if either every term of $K$ or the module $M$ is torsion free, there is a natural, noncanonically split exact sequence

\[ 0\longrightarrow H^ n(K)\otimes M \longrightarrow H^ n(K\otimes M) \longrightarrow H^{n+1}(K)\star M\longrightarrow 0. \]

Under the termwise flatness hypothesis of theorem 10.67.7, if either $H(K)$ or $H(L)$ is torsion free in every degree, the cross product is an isomorphism. Over a field the cross product is an isomorphism for all complexes.

Proof. Take $L^0=M$ and $L^ q=0$ for $q\ne 0$ in the theorem. For the second assertion, a torsion-free cohomology factor is flat, so every Tor term vanishes. Over a field all modules are free, supplying both the termwise and cohomological flatness hypotheses. $\square$

Remark 10.67.10.34C9EFAC359B The termwise hypothesis cannot be replaced by torsion-free cohomology alone. Over $\mathbb Z$ take the exact cochain complex $K^0=\mathbb Z\xrightarrow {2}K^1=\mathbb Z \to K^2=\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$ and tensor with $L^0=\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$. Its degree-zero cohomology becomes $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$, although $H(K)=0$.

Nor is the splitting generally natural in the complexes. For an integer $m\geq 2$ take $K^0=\mathbb Z^2$, $K^1=\mathbb Z$, $d(u,v)=mv$, and $M=\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z$. The degree-zero coefficient sequence is

\[ 0\longrightarrow M\xrightarrow {u\mapsto (u,0)}M^2 \xrightarrow {(u,v)\mapsto v}M\longrightarrow 0. \]

The chain automorphism $(u,v)\mapsto (u+v,v)$, with the identity in degree one, induces the identity on $H(K)$ but the displayed shear on $H^0(K\otimes M)$. A section would have to send $1$ to $(a,1)$; this vector is never fixed by the shear. Hence no section natural under all chain maps can exist.

A projection of graded modules $P\to Z(P)$ is not itself a chain map to the zero-differential cycle complex. For $P^0\xrightarrow {1}P^1$ with both terms $R$, the cycles are $Z^0=0$, $Z^1=R$, and the projection $\pi $ satisfies $\pi ^1d^0=1$ but $d_ Z^0\pi ^0=0$. It therefore gives no cohomology arrow $H(P\otimes L)\to H(Z(P)\otimes L)$. The map $e_ P:P\to H(P)$ used in the theorem is valid because it additionally kills boundaries; the retraction uses this map for both free replacements.

Proposition 10.67.11.64AD0CD558E6 Let $R$ be a principal ideal domain, let $K$ be termwise torsion free, and filter $K\otimes L$ by the $K$ degree. The spectral sequence obtained by first taking $L$ cohomology has

\[ E_1^{p,q}=K^ p\otimes H^ q(L),\qquad E_2^{p,q}=H^ p\bigl (K\otimes H^ q(L)\bigr ), \]

and all differentials $d_ r$ for $r\geq 2$ vanish. Whenever the filtration is finite in each relevant total degree, for example when $K$ and $L$ are bounded below, it converges to the associated graded of total cohomology. No general convergence conclusion for an unbounded filtration is included in this statement.

Proof. Use the filtered map $P(K)\otimes L\to K\otimes L$ from the theorem. On $E_2$ it is the map $H^ p(P(K)\otimes H^ q(L))\to H^ p(K\otimes H^ q(L))$, which is an isomorphism by the already proved preservation of quasi-isomorphisms between termwise flat complexes. Choose complements $P^ p=Z^ p\oplus S^ p$. As a complex, $P$ is the direct sum, over $a$, of the two-term complexes

\[ S^{a-1}\xrightarrow {\ d\ }Z^ a \]

in degrees $a-1,a$. This is an actual decomposition of complexes; the displayed differential has image $B^ a$ and is injective on $S^{a-1}$. After tensoring with $L$, each summand has just these two adjacent $K$ columns. Every $d_ r$ with bidegree $(r,1-r)$ and $r\geq 2$ is therefore zero on that summand. Kernels, images and quotients commute with direct sums of module complexes, so every page and differential of the full filtered complex is the direct sum of those of these summands. The filtered comparison is an isomorphism from $E_2$ onward, giving the asserted vanishing for $K\otimes L$. The finite-filtration convergence statement is exactly the filtered-complex construction already established in theorem 10.54.3; it is not inferred from an abstract, unfiltered direct-sum isomorphism. $\square$
