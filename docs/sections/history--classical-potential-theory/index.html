25.1 Classical potentials, boundary problems, and capacity65E16F3944C7

65E16F3944C7

Pluripotential theory grew out of the study of harmonic functions, but its development repeatedly required a change in what counts as a solution, a negligible set, or a boundary value. This chapter follows those changes through the subjects of Pome. Classical potentials lead to capacity and fine topology; plurisubharmonic functions lead to nonlinear Monge–Ampère measures; singular metrics connect these analytic constructions to complex geometry. The later sections follow this connection through equations, envelopes, energy, sections, and non-Archimedean spaces. The order follows the mathematical dependencies of the reference rather than the tables of contents of its contributing texts.

The Dirichlet problem supplied one of the original organizing questions: how can boundary data determine a harmonic function inside a domain? Green functions express a solution in terms of sources, while Perron's method constructs one by taking an envelope of subsolutions. Boundary regularity then becomes a geometric question. Wiener's criterion answers it through capacity, measuring the thickness of the complement near a boundary point rather than merely its Euclidean size. The chapters on harmonic and subharmonic functions and energy, capacity, and fine topology develop these related viewpoints. Helms's account provides a classical reference for Green potentials, sweeping, boundary limits, and the Wiener test; the supplementary contributions in Pome also pursue energy and Martin boundaries.

Riesz's theory of subharmonic functions made positive measures central to this picture. A subharmonic function can be studied through its distributional Laplacian and its potential representation, even where ordinary second derivatives do not exist. This explains the position of measures, distributions, and convergence near the beginning of Pome. Distributional equations, local integrability, weak convergence, and the choice of testing class are part of the analytic language needed later. Vague convergence on an open domain, for example, permits mass to escape to its boundary; convergence against all bounded continuous functions is stronger.

Capacity records exceptional sets which volume alone cannot detect. Polar sets arise as pole sets of subharmonic potentials, and quasi-continuity replaces ordinary continuity by continuity outside open sets of arbitrarily small capacity. Cartan's fine topology makes superharmonic functions continuous and provides the natural setting for thinness and sweeping. Fuglede's work develops fine potential theory beyond the Euclidean framework. These ideas precede their complex analogues: the plurifine topology and nonlinear capacity inherit the same concern with exceptional sets, while using a different family of test functions. Brelot and Fuglede provide complementary classical perspectives.

The classical material remains active throughout the later theory. Perron envelopes reappear in homogeneous Monge–Ampère equations and weak geodesics; capacity controls nonlinear limits; integration by parts becomes the source of energy inequalities. Pome includes Fourier analysis and complex-line incidence where these supply analytic tools, but these tools should be distinguished from the potential-theoretic structures they help establish. The historical continuity lies in the questions about positivity, envelopes, and exceptional sets.

Separate harmonicity and geometric boundary tests illustrate how the classical theory interacts with several variables. Lelong and Avanissian developed the passage from separate to joint harmonicity; Avanissian's account uses harmonic expansions and complexification. Klimek also surveys the integrable-majorant theorem of Arsove and Wiegerinck's example for separate subharmonicity. The planar boundary-distance criteria are due to Armitage–Kuran and Parker. Their higher-dimensional failures require separate examples, as developed in the classical chapter; an account of the negative-distance criterion also appears in Kuznetsov.
