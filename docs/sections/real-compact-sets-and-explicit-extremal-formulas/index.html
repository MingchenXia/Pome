16.33 Real compact sets and explicit extremal formulasED27A3294909

ED27A3294909

Proposition 16.33.1.C39B105D9256 Every compact $K\subset \mathbb R^ n\subset \mathbb C^ n$ is polynomially convex. At a point $a\in K$, global L-regularity is equivalent to regularity at $a$ of $K\cap \overline B(a,r)$ for every $r>0$.

Proof. For real $a\notin K$, put $q(z)=\sum _ j(z_ j-a_ j)^2$ and choose $R>\sup _ Kq$. The polynomial $1-q/R$ is one at $a$ and has absolute value strictly less than one on $K$. For nonreal $a=x+iy$, instead put $q(z)=\sum _ j(z_ j-x_ j)^2$ and take $R>\sup _ Kq$ positive. The same polynomial has absolute value at most one on $K$ and value $1+|y|^2/R>1$ at $a$. These degree-two separators prove the hull assertion directly, without polynomial approximation as a prerequisite.

Local regularity implies global regularity by obstacle monotonicity. Conversely suppose $V_ K^*(a)=0$ and set $F=K\cap \overline B(a,r)$. If $F$ were not regular at $a$, choose a finite PSH function $\phi $ on $\mathbb C^ n$ with $\phi (a)>0$ and $\phi =0$ on $F\setminus P$ for some pluripolar $P\subset F$. If $F$ is non-pluripolar, take $\phi =V_ F^*$ and its discrepancy set. If $F$ is pluripolar, take $\phi =1$ and $P=F$. Put $M=\sup _ K\phi >0$ and

\[ w(z)=\phi (z)-\frac M{r^2}\operatorname {Re}\sum _ j(z_ j-a_ j)^2. \]

Then $w(a)>0$ and $w\leq 0$ on $K\setminus P$, because the quadratic is $|x-a|^2$ on real points. On a large bounded ball $\Omega $ containing $K$, normalize $w$ to a negative relative competitor $v=w/C-1$ with $v(a)>-1$ for $K\setminus P$.

Relative regularized envelopes are unchanged by deleting a pluripolar subset of an obstacle: choose a negative PSH pole $b$ on $P$ in $\Omega $. For every bounded competitor $u$ of $K\setminus P$, $u+\epsilon b$ is a competitor of $K$, tends to $u$ almost everywhere, and canonical representatives give $u\leq h_{K,\Omega }^*$. Monotonicity gives the reverse inequality after taking suprema. Thus $h_{K,\Omega }^*(a)>-1$. The global-relative lower comparison gives $V_ K^*(a)>0$, a contradiction. The raw/star sandwich identifies regularity at $a$ with zero-star contact throughout this argument. $\square$

Example 16.33.2.2B1312D361AC For $K=\{ |z|=1\} \cup \{ 0\} \subset \mathbb C$, the global extremal is $\log ^+|z|$, so $K$ is regular at zero. But its intersection with a sufficiently small closed ball about zero is the polar singleton, whose regularized extremal is infinite. Hence the preceding local equivalence uses the real-subspace hypothesis.

Write $J_\pm (\zeta )=(\zeta \pm \zeta ^{-1})/2$ and $J=J_+$. The map $J$ sends $|\zeta |>1$ biholomorphically to $\mathbb C\setminus [-1,1]$. Indeed its quadratic inverse roots have product one, and a root on the unit circle gives a point in $[-1,1]$. Off that segment exactly one root has modulus greater than one; $J'$ has no zero there, giving a holomorphic inverse $h(z)=z+\sqrt{z^2-1}$ with $h(t)>1$ for $t>1$. Define

\[ g(z)=\begin{cases} \log |h(z)|,& z\notin [-1,1],\\ 0,& z\in [-1,1],\end{cases} \qquad H(t)=t+\sqrt{t^2-1}\quad (t\geq 1). \]

The function $g$ is continuous, nonnegative and harmonic off the cut, with $g(z)=\log |z|+\log 2+o(1)$ at infinity. It is SH on the whole plane: at a cut point its value zero is below every local mean. The ellipses

\[ J(re^{it})=J_+(r)\cos t+iJ_-(r)\sin t,\qquad r>1, \]

give both continuity at the cut and $|z+1|+|z-1|=2J_+(r)$ when $g(z)=\log r$.

Proposition 16.33.3.227A1367C31C For scalar $z$ put $A_ z(\zeta )=(\overline\zeta z-\zeta ^{-1}\overline z)/2$. Then

\[ |A_ z(\zeta )|=|A_ z(|\zeta |)|,\qquad 2|A_{J(\zeta )}(|\zeta |)|=|J_-(|\zeta |^2)|. \]

For every nonzero $c\in \mathbb C^ n$, the vector map $J_ c(\zeta )=(\zeta c+\zeta ^{-1}\overline c)/2$ is injective for $|\zeta |>1$ and has continuous inverse on its image. If image points approach a point of $J_ c(\{ |\zeta |=1\} )$, their parameter moduli tend to one.

Proof. For $r=|\zeta |$ direct expansion gives $4|A_ z(\zeta )|^2=|z|^2(r^2+r^{-2})-2\operatorname {Re}z^2$. If $\zeta =re^{it}$, then $A_{J(\zeta )}(r)=(r^2-r^{-2})e^{it}/4$, proving the absolute-value identity, including $r<1$. Also

\[ 2(J_ c(\zeta _1)-J_ c(\zeta _2)) =(\zeta _1-\zeta _2)(c-(\zeta _1\zeta _2)^{-1}\overline c). \]

Distinct parameters giving the same image would have product of modulus one, impossible when both are outside the unit disc. Image convergence bounds the parameters, since $|J_ c(\zeta )|\to \infty $ as $|\zeta |\to \infty $. The same identity excludes a unit-circle cluster parameter for an exterior image point and excludes an exterior cluster parameter for a boundary image point. This proves the claims. $\square$

Proposition 16.33.4.D47F294B7F7D If a holomorphic $f:\mathbb C^*\to \mathbb C$ satisfies

\[ 2f(|\zeta |)=|f(\zeta )+1|+|f(\zeta )-1|, \]

then it is either constant with value in $[1,\infty )$, or $f(\zeta )=J((a\zeta )^ p)$ for $a>0$ and an integer $p\geq 1$. Every listed function satisfies the identity.

Proof. The identity on the positive axis forces $f(r)\geq 1$ and real. The ellipse identity shows that $g(f(\zeta ))=\log H(f(|\zeta |))$. For a nonconstant $f$, $g\circ f$ is continuous radial SH on $\mathbb C^*$, harmonic on each circle where $f(r)>1$. Its profile in $\log r$ is convex, by harmonic annular comparison. If it never vanished, it would be harmonic on all of $\mathbb C^*$ and equal to $A\log r+B$. Nonnegativity forces $A=0$, so the image of $f$ would lie in one ellipse, contradicting the local inverse theorem at a point with $f'\ne 0$. Thus it has a zero radius $r_0$. There cannot be two: convexity would make the whole intervening annulus map into $[-1,1]$, again forcing constancy.

For $r>r_0$ radial harmonicity now gives $g(f(\zeta ))=p\log (r/r_0)$ with $p>0$. On this annulus the nonvanishing holomorphic function $q=h\circ f$ has modulus $(r/r_0)^ p$. Differentiating its logarithmic modulus shows $\zeta q'/q=p$; integration around a circle makes $p$ an integer. Thus $q=C\zeta ^ p$, and its positive real values on the positive axis give $C=r_0^{-p}$. Hence $f=J((\zeta /r_0)^ p)$ on the annulus and, by the identity theorem, on $\mathbb C^*$. The constants and the converse follow directly from the ellipse identity. $\square$

Proposition 16.33.5.F36E879B8976 Let $D\subset \mathbb C$ be bounded, let $E\subset \mathbb C^ n$ be compact, and let $f:\overline D\to \mathbb C^ n$ be continuous and holomorphic on $D$, with $f(\partial D)\subset E$. Suppose that $v$ is continuous and nonnegative on $f(\overline D)$, vanishes on $f(\partial D)$, and $-v\circ f$ is SH on $D$. Then $V_ E\leq v$ on $f(D)$.

Proof. For every global competitor $u$, the function $(u-v)\circ f$ is SH or identically $-\infty $ on each component and has nonpositive boundary upper limits. The bounded-domain maximum principle makes it nonpositive. Take the supremum over all competitors. $\square$

Theorem 16.33.6.A1B73D6E87D0 Let $E\subset \mathbb R^ n$ be compact, convex, symmetric about zero, with zero in its real interior, and let $E^\circ =\{ y\in \mathbb R^ n:x\cdot y\leq 1\text{ for all }x\in E\} $. Extend real linear functionals complex linearly. Then

\[ V_ E(z)=\max _{y\in E^\circ }g(y\cdot z) =\sup _{y\in \operatorname {Extr}(E^\circ )}g(y\cdot z). \]

Both equal the maximum over the closure of the extreme points.

Proof. Finite-dimensional separation gives the bipolar identity and compactness of $E^\circ $. Every point of a finite-dimensional compact convex set is a finite convex combination of its extreme points: boundary points lie in lower-dimensional exposed faces; use induction on their affine dimension, and express an interior point on a segment between boundary points. An extreme point of an exposed face is extreme in the whole set. The sublevel sets of $g$ are the filled ellipses just described, including the degenerate segment at level zero, so they are convex. Applied to $y\mapsto y\cdot z$, this shows that its maximum over the polar equals its supremum over extreme points. Closure does not change the supremum, and supplies a compact set for attainment.

Each $g(y\cdot z)$ is PSH of logarithmic growth, nonpositive on $E$; the zero functional gives the admissible zero function. Thus $V_ E$ is at least the displayed maximum. For $z_0\notin E$ this maximum is $\log r>0$: real separation detects a real point outside $E$, and the polar's real span detects any nonzero imaginary part. Choose $y_0$ attaining it and $\zeta $ with $|\zeta |=r$ and $y_0\cdot z_0=J(\zeta )$. Set

\[ c=\frac{2(\overline\zeta z_0-\zeta ^{-1}\overline z_0)}{r^2-r^{-2}}. \]

Then $J_ c(\zeta )=z_0$ and $y_0\cdot c=1$. For every $y\in E^\circ $, $g(y\cdot z_0)\leq \log r$. The real-linear map $w\mapsto (\zeta w+\zeta ^{-1}\overline w)/2$ takes the closed unit disc bijectively onto this filled ellipse. Its inverse formula is exactly the displayed formula for $y\cdot c$, so $|y\cdot c|\leq 1$. Consequently $J_ c(e^{it})=\operatorname {Re}(e^{it}c)$ lies in $E$ by bipolarity and symmetry.

For every global competitor $u$, the function $u(J_ c(\xi ))-\log |\xi |$ is SH, or identically $-\infty $, on $|\xi |>1$. Its growth is bounded above at infinity and its boundary upper limits are nonpositive. Inversion gives a bounded-above removable puncture in the unit disc; the maximum principle therefore bounds it by zero. Thus $u(z_0)\leq \log r$. Take the supremum over $u$. On $E$ both sides are zero. This exterior argument supplies the growth condition missing from an invocation of the bounded-disc lemma. $\square$

Proposition 16.33.7.E41A4DC7736D Let $B\subset \mathbb R^ n$ be the closed unit ball, let $S=\{ x_ j\geq 0,\ \sum _ jx_ j\leq 1\} $ and let $C=[-1,1]\cup [-i,i]\subset \mathbb C$. Then

\[ \begin{aligned} V_{[-1,1]^ n}(z)& =\max _ j g(z_ j),\\ V_ B(z)& =\tfrac 12\log H\left(|z|^2+\left|\sum _ jz_ j^2-1\right|\right),\\ V_ S(z)& =\log H\left(\sum _ j|z_ j|+\left|\sum _ jz_ j-1\right|\right),\\ V_ C(z)& =\tfrac 14\log H\left(|z|^4+|z^4-1|\right). \end{aligned} \]

Proof. The cube formula is the already proved Cartesian maximum theorem applied to the one-dimensional case of the body formula. Equivalently the extreme points of the cube's polar, the unit l1 ball, are $\pm e_ j$.

For the real ball use the preceding construction and take a maximizing unit real $y_0$. Such a maximizer exists on the sphere by the extreme- point formula. Write $c=a+ib$. We have $a\cdot y_0=1$, $b\cdot y_0=0$, and $|y\cdot c|\leq 1$ for every unit real $y$. Testing $y=a/|a|$ and Cauchy–Schwarz give $|a|=1$ and $a=y_0$; testing $y=b/|b|$ when $b\ne 0$ gives $|b|\leq 1$. Hence $a\perp b$ and

\[ z_0=aJ_+(\zeta )+ibJ_-(\zeta ),\quad |z_0|^2=|J_+(\zeta )|^2+|b|^2|J_-(\zeta )|^2, \]

while $\sum _ jz_{0,j}^2-1=(1-|b|^2)J_-(\zeta )^2$. Their indicated sum is $J_+(r^2)$, since $|J_+(\zeta )|^2+|J_-(\zeta )|^2=(r^2+r^{-2})/2$. Thus $r^2=H(|z_0|^2+|\sum _ jz_{0,j}^2-1|)$, proving the ball formula outside $B$; on $B$ the argument equals one and the value is zero.

The degree-two regular polynomial map $f(z)=(z_1^2,\ldots ,z_ n^2)$ has $f^{-1}S=B$, including the requirement that every coordinate be real. The polynomial covariance theorem gives $V_ S(f(z))=2V_ B(z)$. Choose square roots of arbitrary coordinates to obtain the simplex formula. Likewise $z\mapsto z^2$ has inverse image $C$ of $[-1,1]$; use covariance and the one-dimensional ball formula for the last identity. All displayed arguments of $H$ are at least one by the triangle inequality; $H(1)=1$ includes the cut/boundary values continuously. $\square$
