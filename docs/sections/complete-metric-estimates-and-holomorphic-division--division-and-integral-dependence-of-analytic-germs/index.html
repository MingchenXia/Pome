6.30 Division and integral dependence of analytic germs98636F849B94

98636F849B94

Let $R=\mathcal O_{\mathbb C^ n,0}$, $n\geq 1$, and let $0\ne \mathfrak a=(g_1,\ldots ,g_ r)\subset R$. Use the existing bounded-growth and multiplier ideals the bounded-growth ideal, the multiplier ideal, and write

\[ \mathfrak a_\infty (t)=\mathcal I_\infty (t\log |g|^2)_0, \qquad \mathfrak a_+(t)=\bigcup _{\eta >0} \mathcal I((t+\eta )\log |g|^2)_0 \quad (t\geq 0). \]

At $t=0$ the zero weight means zero, including at the zeros of $g$. These are ideals: the union is directed, and holomorphic coefficients are locally bounded. Replacing generators of the same ideal changes $|g|$ by bounded positive factors, so this notation depends only on $\mathfrak a$. Rescale the generator tuple by a nonzero constant and shrink representatives so that $|g|\leq 1$. The unit ideal has both families equal to $R$; the assertions below then have their immediate unit-ideal meaning.

We will use the already elementary relations

9CDE16182344 6.30.1
\begin{equation} \begin{gathered} \mathfrak a_\infty (t)\subset \mathfrak a_+(t),\qquad \mathfrak a^\ell \subset \mathfrak a_\infty (\ell ),\quad \mathfrak a_\infty (s)\mathfrak a_+(t)\subset \mathfrak a_+(s+t),\\ \mathfrak a_\infty (s)\mathfrak a_\infty (t) \subset \mathfrak a_\infty (s+t). \end{gathered} \end{equation}

Here $s,t\geq 0$ and $\ell $ is a nonnegative integer. For clarity, the only integrability step is the first inclusion. Choose $g_ j\not\equiv 0$ and $\eta >0$ small enough that $|g_ j|^{-2\eta }$ is locally integrable. This follows from calculus and semicontinuity of potential Lelong numbers, multiplier-ideal order bounds from Lelong numbers: $\eta \operatorname {ord}_0g_ j<1$ makes the multiplier ideal have order zero, hence contain a unit. If $|u|\leq C|g|^ t$, then $|u|^2|g|^{-2(t+\eta )}\leq C^2|g_ j|^{-2\eta }$. The remaining relations follow pointwise, with the minimum positive integrability margin used in finite sums. In particular both families at $t=0$ equal $R$.

Lemma 6.30.2.A350950F4102 If $r>n$, then outside a proper analytic subset of $P=(\mathbb P^{r-1})^ n$, the coefficient tuple $([a_1],\ldots ,[a_ n])$ gives generators $\widetilde g_ j=a_ j\cdot g$ satisfying

6FC33A438D15 6.30.3
\begin{equation} C^{-1}|g|\leq |\widetilde g|\leq C|g| \end{equation}

near zero, with a constant depending on the chosen representatives. Consequently the ideal $\mathfrak b=(\widetilde g_1,\ldots ,\widetilde g_ n)$ lies in $\mathfrak a$ and has the same two ideal families for all $t\geq 0$.

Proof. If $g(0)\ne 0$, discard the proper analytic subset where every $a_ j\cdot g(0)$ is zero; continuity gives the lower bound and the upper bound is immediate. Assume $g(0)=0$, and represent $g$ on a connected neighborhood $\Omega $ of zero. Put $Z=\{ g=0\} $ and

\[ A=\{ (z,[w_1],\ldots ,[w_ n]):w_ j\cdot g(z)=0\ (1\leq j\leq n)\} , \qquad A^*=\overline{A\setminus (Z\times P)}\subset \Omega \times P. \]

The closure is analytic and its local branches are exactly the branches not contained in $Z\times P$, by analytic closure after removing an analytic subset. Off $Z$, the equations give a locally trivial bundle with fiber $(\mathbb P^{r-2})^ n$: where one component of $g$ is nonzero, solve for the corresponding linear coordinate in each hyperplane. It is smooth of dimension $n+n(r-2)=n(r-1)=\dim P$. Every branch of $A^*$ has points off $Z\times P$, so purity and purity and dimension drop for analytic germs show that all its branches have this dimension.

Its zero fiber $B=A^*\cap (\{ 0\} \times P)$ is a proper analytic subset of $\{ 0\} \times P$. Indeed, if it contained that whole fiber, its irreducible germ of dimension $\dim P$ at any point would be contained in one local branch of $A^*$, by irreducible analytic branches and prime ideals. The equal dimensions force equality of germs. That branch would be contained in $Z\times P$, contrary to the closure construction. Identify $B$ with its proper analytic subset of $P$ and choose $([a_1],\ldots ,[a_ n])\notin B$.

Since $A^*$ is closed, some product neighborhood of this point and zero is disjoint from $A^*$. Choose nonzero vector representatives $a_ j$ and $\delta >0$ so small that all perturbations $a_ j-h_ j$, $|h_ j|<\delta $, are nonzero and their projective classes stay in that product neighborhood. If $z\notin Z$ is sufficiently near zero and every $|a_ j\cdot g(z)|<\delta |g(z)|$, put

\[ h_ j=\frac{a_ j\cdot g(z)}{|g(z)|^2}\, \overline{g(z)}. \]

Then $|h_ j|<\delta $ and $(a_ j-h_ j)\cdot g(z)=0$ for all $j$. The resulting point lies in $A\setminus (Z\times P)\subset A^*$, a contradiction. Thus $\max _ j|a_ j\cdot g|\geq \delta |g|$ near zero; it holds on $Z$ as well. The upper bound follows from Cauchy–Schwarz. Norm equivalence gives equality of both families, including every real exponent. $\square$

Theorem 6.30.4.D191522951EC Put $p=\min (n-1,r-1)$. For every real $t\geq p$,

FE0312A8F3A0 6.30.5
\begin{equation} \mathfrak a_+(t+1)=\mathfrak a\, \mathfrak a_+(t) =\mathfrak a_\infty (1)\mathfrak a_+(t). \end{equation}

For every integer $k\geq 0$,

74FDC8170F34 6.30.6
\begin{equation} \mathfrak a_\infty (k+p)\subset \mathfrak a_+(k+p) =\mathfrak a^ k\mathfrak a_+(p)\subset \mathfrak a^ k. \end{equation}

Proof. The unit case is immediate. Suppose first $r\leq n$, so $p=r-1$. For $f\in \mathfrak a_+(t+1)$ choose a small ball and $\eta >0$ with $\int |f|^2|g|^{-2(t+1+\eta )}<\infty $. Use scalar division corollary 6.29.2 on that ball with $m=r-1$ and $\phi =(t-m)\log |g|^2$. Its coefficient $t-m$ is nonnegative, so this is PSH by plurisubharmonicity of logarithmic holomorphic norms, with zero understood when $t=m$. The ball is complete Kähler by lemma 6.26.8, using its smooth PSH exhaustion $(R^2-|z|^2)^{-1}$. The data exponent is exactly $t+1+\eta $. Division gives $f=\sum g_ jh_ j$ with $\int |h|^2|g|^{-2(t+\eta )}<\infty $, so every $h_ j\in \mathfrak a_+(t)$. If $r>n$, use the preceding $n$-generator ideal $\mathfrak b$. Its two families agree with those of $\mathfrak a$, and its threshold is $n-1=p$. The case just proved gives $\mathfrak a_+(t+1)=\mathfrak b_+(t+1) \subset \mathfrak b\mathfrak b_+(t) \subset \mathfrak a\mathfrak a_+(t)$. The reverse inclusions and the factor $\mathfrak a_\infty (1)$ follow from (6.30.1) and $\mathfrak a\subset \mathfrak a_\infty (1)$. Iterating the recurrence from $t=p$ proves (6.30.6); its first inclusion is (6.30.1). $\square$

Theorem 6.30.7.D091ECF916A1 For every real $t\geq 0$, a germ $u$ belongs to $\mathfrak a_\infty (t)$ if and only if it satisfies a monic equation

AFD88AFE39D9 6.30.8
\begin{equation} u^ d+c_1u^{d-1}+\cdots +c_ d=0, \qquad c_ s\in \mathfrak a^{\lceil ts\rceil }\ (1\leq s\leq d). \end{equation}

In particular $\mathfrak a_\infty (1)$ is the integral closure of $\mathfrak a$. There is always a rational $l\geq t$ such that $\mathfrak a_\infty (l)=\mathfrak a_\infty (t)$.

Proof. If a monic equation holds, put $M=\max _ s|c_ s|^{1/s}$ at each point. The elementary root bound $|u|\leq 2M$ follows because $|u|>2M$ would give $\sum _ s|c_ s/u^ s|<\sum _{s\geq 1}2^{-s}=1$. Since $|g|\leq 1$, membership of each coefficient in the indicated ideal power gives $|c_ s|\leq C_ s|g|^{\lceil ts\rceil } \leq C_ s|g|^{ts}$. Hence $|u|\leq C|g|^ t$.

For the converse, let $J=\mathfrak a_+(p)$. It is finitely generated by Noetherianity of convergent power-series modules and nonzero: for a nonzero $g_ j$, choose an integer $N>p$ and $0<\eta <N-p$; the integrand $|g_ j|^{2N}|g|^{-2(p+\eta )}$ is bounded near zero. Choose nonzero generators $v_1,\ldots ,v_ q$ of $J$. Their finitely many integrability margins give $\delta >0$ such that

ACAC37891CB2 6.30.9
\begin{equation} J=\mathfrak a_+(p+\eta )\qquad (0\leq \eta <\delta ). \end{equation}

Indeed take $2\delta $ below all margins in the defining integrals; each generator then belongs to the right side, and the reverse inclusion follows from the stronger exponent.

For any real $t\geq 0$ and any $\delta >0$ there are an integer $m\geq 1$ and $0\leq \eta <\delta $ with $N=mt+\eta $ an integer. For rational $t$ take a denominator and $\eta =0$. For irrational $t$, the pigeonhole principle applied to fractional parts $0,t,\ldots ,Lt$ gives $q_0\geq 1$ with the fractional part of $q_0t$ within $1/L$ of an integer. If it is $1-\beta $, take $m=q_0$ and $\eta =\beta $. If it is $\beta >0$, take $m=q_0\lfloor 1/\beta \rfloor $, whose fractional part lies in $(1-\beta ,1)$, and take $\eta =1-\lfloor 1/\beta \rfloor \beta $. Here $L$ is chosen with $1/L<\delta $; irrationality excludes equality at an integer. This proves the assertion.

For $u\in \mathfrak a_\infty (t)$ choose such $m,\eta ,N$ and use (6.30.9), the product inclusion and (6.30.6) to get

\[ u^ mJ\subset \mathfrak a_\infty (mt)\mathfrak a_+(p+\eta ) \subset \mathfrak a_+(p+N)=\mathfrak a^ N J. \]

Thus $u^ mv_ j=\sum _ l b_{jl}v_ l$ with $b_{jl}\in \mathfrak a^ N$. Multiplying $(u^ mI-B)v=0$ by its adjugate gives $\det (u^ mI-B)v=0$. Since $R$ is a domain and $v\ne 0$, its determinant is zero. The coefficient of $u^{m(q-s)}$ lies in $\mathfrak a^{Ns}\subset \mathfrak a^{\lceil mts\rceil }$. This is (6.30.8), with zero coefficients at degrees whose indices are not multiples of $m$.

Finally choose finitely many generators of $\mathfrak a_\infty (t)$ by the same Noetherian theorem and equations of the proved type for them. If $t$ is rational use $l=t$. Otherwise the finitely many nonzero coefficient indices $s$ have $\lceil ts\rceil /s>t$. Choose rational $l\geq t$ below all these upper bounds. Then $\lceil ls\rceil =\lceil ts\rceil $ at every such index, and the same equations put all generators in $\mathfrak a_\infty (l)$. The reverse inclusion follows from $l\geq t$ and $|g|\leq 1$. If the index family is empty the corresponding generators are zero, and any rational $l\geq t$ suffices. $\square$

Lemma 6.30.10.FC08C236D5C5 Let $f,g_1,\ldots ,g_ r\in R$ vanish at zero. Then $|f|\leq C|g|$ near zero if and only if for every holomorphic arc $\gamma :(\mathbb C,0)\to (\mathbb C^ n,0)$ there is $C_\gamma $ with $|f\circ \gamma |\leq C_\gamma |g\circ \gamma |$ near zero.

Proof. Necessity is immediate. If the growth bound fails, choose $z_\nu \to 0$ with $f(z_\nu )\ne 0$ and $|g(z_\nu )|/|f(z_\nu )|\to 0$. The analytic germ

\[ A=\{ (z,w):g_ j(z)=f(z)w_ j\ (1\leq j\leq r)\} \]

contains $(z_\nu ,g(z_\nu )/f(z_\nu ))\to (0,0)$. Its finite branch decomposition has a branch on which $f$ is not identically zero. This branch has positive dimension since $f(0)=0$. Apply analytic curve selection to that branch and its proper subgerm $f=0$. The resulting arc is $(\gamma (t),w(t))$, with $f\circ \gamma (t)\ne 0$ for $t\ne 0$ and $w(t)=O(t)$. Its defining equations give $|g\circ \gamma (t)|\leq C|t|\, |f\circ \gamma (t)|$, contradicting the asserted arc bound. Its first component is nonconstant, since otherwise $f\circ \gamma =0$. $\square$

Corollary 6.30.11.069E4D5B96EF Let $f\in R$ satisfy $f(0)=0$, and put $\mathfrak a_ f=(z_1\partial f/\partial z_1,\ldots , z_ n\partial f/\partial z_ n)$. Then $f$ belongs to the integral closure of $\mathfrak a_ f$, and for every integer $k\geq 0$,

\[ f^{k+n-1}\in \mathfrak a_ f^ k. \]

Proof. If $\mathfrak a_ f=0$, the domain property forces all derivatives of $f$ to vanish, so $f=0$ and the assertions are immediate. For an arc $\gamma $, a nonzero $q=f\circ \gamma $ has positive vanishing order, hence $|q|\leq C|tq'|$ near zero; if $q=0$ the desired bound is automatic. Each nonzero coordinate $\gamma _ j$ has $t\gamma '_ j/\gamma _ j$ holomorphic and bounded near zero, since $\gamma _ j=t^{d_ j}u_ j$ with $u_ j(0)\ne 0$. The chain rule thus gives

\[ |f(\gamma (t))|\leq C_\gamma \sum _ j|\gamma _ j(t)(\partial _ jf)(\gamma (t))| \leq C'_\gamma |(z_ j\partial _ jf)_ j(\gamma (t))|. \]

The preceding curve test proves $f\in (\mathfrak a_ f)_\infty (1)$. The integral-dependence theorem and (6.30.6), with $r=n$, prove both assertions. The vanishing condition $f(0)=0$ is essential. $\square$

Lemma 6.30.12.58C58F25C58C Let $d\geq 1$ be an integer. If $F_ j=z_ j^ d+$ terms of degree $>d$, $1\leq j\leq n$, then the lowest homogeneous part of every nonzero member of $(F_1,\ldots ,F_ n)$ belongs to the monomial ideal $(z_1^ d,\ldots ,z_ n^ d)$.

Proof. First every homogeneous relation $\sum _ j a_ jz_ j^ d=0$ is generated by the pair relations $z_ l^ de_ j-z_ j^ de_ l$. Indeed group terms by a monomial $z^\alpha $. Only indices with $\alpha _ j\geq d$ occur, and their coefficients sum to zero. Choose one such index as anchor; each other coefficient is represented by the corresponding pair relation times $z^{\alpha -de_ j-de_ l}$. All exponents are nonnegative. If the $a_ j$ have degree $b$, the multipliers have degree $b-d$.

Now let $H=\sum a_ jF_ j$ have order $N$, and put $b=\min _ j\operatorname {ord}a_ j$. If $b+d<N$, the degree-b parts of the $a_ j$ form such a relation. Subtract its pair expressions with $F_ l e_ j-F_ j e_ l$ in place of $z_ l^ de_ j-z_ j^ de_ l$. This preserves $H$ exactly and raises the minimum order of the coefficient tuple by at least one. After finitely many repetitions all coefficient orders are at least $N-d$. Consequently the degree-N part of $H$ is $\sum _ j(a_ j)_{N-d}z_ j^ d$, as asserted. If $N<d$, no nonzero member can have order $N$ in the first place. All operations use finite Taylor pieces and preserve convergent germs. $\square$

Example 6.30.13.898990855F7E The first power bounds above are sharp in the following examples. For $2\leq r\leq n$, let $\mathfrak a=(z_1^ r,\ldots ,z_ r^ r)$ and $u=z_1\cdots z_ r$. Then $u\in \mathfrak a_\infty (1)$ and $u^ r\in \mathfrak a$, but $u^{r-1}\notin \mathfrak a$. For $n\geq 1$ the germ

\[ f=(z_1\cdots z_ n)^3+\sum _{j=1}^ n z_ j^{3n-1} \]

satisfies $f^{n-1}\notin \mathfrak a_ f$.

Proof. The arithmetic–geometric mean inequality gives $|u|^2\leq r^{-1}\sum _ j|z_ j|^{2r}$. The monomial $u^ r$ is in the ideal, whereas every monomial of an element of $(z_1^ r,\ldots ,z_ r^ r)$ is divisible by some $z_ j^ r$; the monomial $u^{r-1}$ is not. For the second example, $n=1$ says $1\notin (zf')$, since this is a proper ideal. Assume $n\geq 2$ and put $d=3n-1$ and $P=(z_1\cdots z_ n)^3$. Then $\mathfrak a_ f=(d z_ j^ d+3P)_ j$, and modulo this ideal, $f=P+\sum z_ j^ d=-P/d$. If $f^{n-1}$ belonged to the ideal, so would $P^{n-1}$. The preceding lemma forbids this, since each exponent $3(n-1)=d-2$ of that monomial is smaller than $d$. $\square$
