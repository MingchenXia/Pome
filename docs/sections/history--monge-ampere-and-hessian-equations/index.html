25.5 Monge–Ampère and complex Hessian equationsE1BC130DDD5C

E1BC130DDD5C

The complex Monge–Ampère equation links the local nonlinear operator to global geometry. Yau's solution of the Calabi conjecture in the Kähler case established the smooth theory for prescribed volume forms with the required total mass. In the weak theory, the background geometry, the regularity of the measure, and the permitted singularities of the solution become separate parts of the problem. The chapter on Monge–Ampère equations and stability follows this progression from a priori estimates to existence, uniqueness, and stability.

The local Dirichlet theory of Bedford–Taylor makes the regularity of the density and the boundary geometry central to estimates. Degenerate equations require additional care: the examples after Pliś, and the regularity qualifications discussed by Dinew–Pliś–Zhang, show why smoothness of the density alone does not supply all the expected second-derivative bounds. Pome keeps these counterexamples beside the precise density-root hypotheses of the positive results.

Kołodziej's bounded-solution theory for measures with $L^p$ density, $p>1$, changed the range of accessible right-hand sides. Capacity estimates replace estimates that require a smooth density and make it possible to pass to weak limits. In big classes, Boucksom–Eyssidieux–Guedj–Zeriahi constructed solutions with controlled singularities, while Berman–Boucksom–Guedj–Zeriahi developed the variational approach through energy functionals. Local energy classes originating with Cegrell provide another route from bounded functions to unbounded solutions. These approaches share nonlinear comparison tools but have different admissible classes.

The Hermitian equation has a further normalization issue. Since the total mass need not be fixed by cohomology, prescribing a measure can also require determining a multiplicative constant. Cherrier's work and the smooth existence theory of Tosatti–Weinkove are basic stages of this development. The weak theory developed by Kołodziej–Nguyen and others must control the torsion errors in comparison and stability arguments. Uniqueness for a normalized solution, uniqueness of the normalization constant, and a selection principle for limits are distinct assertions; the book treats them with the corresponding assumptions.

The chapter on equations with prescribed singularities asks for more than a solution in an ambient big class. Fixing an ordinary singularity type constrains how the solution may diverge, and the mass of that type must match the equation. The relative theory developed by Darvas–Di Nezza–Lu uses model potentials and relative full-mass classes to formulate this compatibility. In the Hermitian setting, the same question interacts with upper and lower mass bounds. Results for semipositive backgrounds, big closed backgrounds, and general nonclosed backgrounds therefore occupy different places in the theory.

Complex Hessian equations replace the top exterior power by intermediate symmetric functions of the complex Hessian. Their admissible cones, mixed inequalities, and comparison principles extend part of the Monge–Ampère picture, but require their own ellipticity and background assumptions. Work of Dinew–Kołodziej and the Hermitian developments of Kołodziej–Nguyen are among the reference points for this subject. The parallel with Monge–Ampère theory helps organize the questions; it does not eliminate the changes in degree, positivity, or torsion.
