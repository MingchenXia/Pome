25.2 Plurisubharmonicity, currents, and singularitiesE49C1A0D4682

E49C1A0D4682

The passage to several complex variables begins with plurisubharmonic functions, introduced independently in the work of Oka and Lelong. Subharmonicity along complex lines provides a function-theoretic definition; positivity of the distributional complex Hessian provides its current-theoretic form. These two descriptions explain the joint treatment of plurisubharmonic functions and positive currents. Unlike real subharmonicity alone, plurisubharmonicity is adapted to holomorphic maps and complex analytic sets. Bremermann's historical account discusses the early development.

Currents allow differential forms with singular coefficients to participate in geometric operations. The Poincaré–Lelong formula identifies the complex Hessian of a logarithm with the current of a divisor, linking zeros of holomorphic functions to positivity. Lelong numbers then quantify concentration at a point. Siu's analyticity theorem shows that suitable upper level sets of these numbers are analytic, making a numerical measure of singularity detect complex subvarieties. The notions of positivity for currents were developed by Lelong and Harvey–Knapp; they have a history distinct from later regularization techniques.

Another way to measure a singularity is to test which holomorphic germs remain square integrable against its exponential weight. Nadel's multiplier ideal sheaves turn this integrability condition into coherent algebraic data. The weighted estimates of Hörmander and the extension theorem of Ohsawa–Takegoshi supply the analytic mechanisms relating holomorphic functions to singular weights. The chapter on Lelong numbers, multiplier ideals, and regularization brings these descriptions together. Our convention is $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm c}=\mathrm i\partial\bar\partial/(2\pi)$ and the multiplier weight is $\exp(-\varphi)$; changing either convention changes numerical thresholds.

The openness problem asks how stable such integrability is under a small increase in the weight. Demailly–Kollár formulated and studied the openness conjecture; Berndtsson established an openness theorem, and Guan–Zhou proved strong openness. These results are distinct from approximation statements. Bergman kernels and regularization provide controlled models of singular weights, whereas openness concerns the ideal of a fixed weight. Historical attribution to these methods does not by itself supply the hypotheses or estimates required in a particular approximation argument.

The exceptional sets also have a specifically complex history. Josefson's local-to-global theorem identifies locally pluripolar sets in complex Euclidean space with global pole sets; global variants on compact manifolds require their own hypotheses. The plurifine topology is associated with Fuglede's work in the Lelong seminar. Bedford–Taylor developed its relation to nonlinear products, and El Marzguioui–Wiegerinck proved the local-connectivity result used in the theory. Keeping these contributions separate clarifies why pluripolarity, plurifine locality, and ordinary almost-everywhere identities have different roles.
