7.33 Separated maximum Monge–Ampère products0480FCC6AA86

0480FCC6AA86

We use $dd^ c=\frac{i}{2\pi }\partial \bar\partial $ and the classical Bedford–Taylor products. In particular, the products at an isolated pole include their mass at that pole.

Lemma 7.33.1.11B8D65C2D19 Let $f,g$ be locally bounded plurisubharmonic functions on an open subset of $\mathbb C^ N$, put $h=\max \{ f,g\} $, and write $A=dd^ cf$, $B=dd^ cg$, and $C=dd^ ch$. For $1\leq k\leq N$,

32CB78FC0E95 7.33.2
\begin{equation} C^ k=C\wedge \sum _{j=0}^{k-1}A^ j\wedge B^{k-1-j} -\sum _{j=1}^{k-1}A^ j\wedge B^{k-j}. \end{equation}

The second sum is zero when $k=1$.

Proof. All products and scalar weights below are the bounded recursive products, with their symmetry. By maximum locality, $C^ k=B^ k$ on the Borel set $\{ g>f\} $ in the sense of restriction of coefficient measures. The locally bounded nonnegative function $h-f$ vanishes on its complement. Hence

\[ (h-f)C^ k=(h-f)B^ k. \]

Apply $dd^ c$. The recursive definition and symmetry give, for $1\leq k<N$,

\[ C^{k+1}-A\wedge C^ k=C\wedge B^ k-A\wedge B^ k. \]

Starting with $C^1=C$, this recurrence proves (7.33.2) by induction: multiplication of the $k$th identity by $A$, followed by addition of $C\wedge B^ k-A\wedge B^ k$, gives exactly the $(k+1)$st identity. Every scalar multiplication used here is multiplication of the coefficient measures of a bounded product; no multiplication of distributions is required. $\square$

Theorem 7.33.3.F8604F30C7C6 Let $D\subset \mathbb C^ p$ and $G\subset \mathbb C^ q$ be open, where $p,q\geq 1$. Suppose that $f\geq 0$ and $g\geq 0$ are plurisubharmonic on $D$ and $G$, respectively, and

\[ \mathbf1_{\{ f>0\} }(dd^ cf)^ p=0, \qquad \mathbf1_{\{ g>0\} }(dd^ cg)^ q=0. \]

Then, on $D\times G$,

BD9883DFED0B 7.33.4
\begin{equation} \bigl (dd^ c\max \{ f(z),g(w)\} \bigr )^{p+q} =(dd^ cf)^ p\otimes (dd^ cg)^ q. \end{equation}

The right-hand side is the product measure, with no binomial factor.

Proof. The nonnegative plurisubharmonic functions are locally bounded: upper semicontinuity bounds them above on compact neighborhoods, and zero is a lower bound. Put $h(z,w)=\max \{ f(z),g(w)\} $, and let $A,B,C$ denote the pulled-back Hessians of $f,g$ and the Hessian of $h$, as in the preceding lemma.

For $0\leq a\leq p$ and $0\leq b\leq q$, products of these separate-variable Hessians satisfy

\[ A^ a\wedge B^ b=(dd^ cf)^ a\otimes (dd^ cg)^ b. \]

Indeed, regularize $f$ and $g$ decreasingly on relatively compact coordinate domains in their own variables. For smooth potentials the formula is ordinary exterior algebra and Fubini. Bounded decreasing continuity passes the left-hand side to its limit. On the right, the coefficient measures have locally uniformly bounded total variations, by positivity and their trace masses. Their tensor products converge: for a smooth compact test coefficient $F(z,w)$, a finite smooth partition in the $z$ variables approximates it uniformly on a containing compact rectangle by finite sums $\sum _ i\rho _ i(z)F(z_ i,w)$, with fixed compact cutoffs in both factors. Each separated term converges by the two weak convergences; the uniform total-variation bound controls the approximation error. This proves the displayed tensor identity. The same smooth approximation proves $A^{p+1}=B^{q+1}=0$ by degree.

Apply (7.33.2) with $k=p+q$. Only two terms in the first sum and one in the second can survive:

5E25FA94CA28 7.33.5
\begin{equation} C^{p+q} =C\wedge \bigl (A^{p-1}\wedge B^ q+A^ p\wedge B^{q-1}\bigr ) -A^ p\wedge B^ q. \end{equation}

The coefficient measures of $S=A^{p-1}\wedge B^ q$ give zero mass to $D\times \{ g>0\} $, by the tensor identity and the hypothesis on $(dd^ cg)^ q$. On the complementary set $g=0$, nonnegativity of $f$ gives $h=f$. Thus $hS=fS$ as weighted currents, and bounded recursion gives

\[ C\wedge A^{p-1}\wedge B^ q =dd^ c(hS)=dd^ c(fS)=A^ p\wedge B^ q. \]

Interchanging the factors similarly gives $C\wedge A^ p\wedge B^{q-1}=A^ p\wedge B^ q$. Substitution in (7.33.5) leaves one copy of $A^ p\wedge B^ q$, proving the assertion. $\square$
