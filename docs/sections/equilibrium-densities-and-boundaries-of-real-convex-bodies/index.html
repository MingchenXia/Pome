16.42 Equilibrium densities and boundaries of real convex bodiesBDAC528E041B

BDAC528E041B

Write $\mu _ K=(dd^ cV_ K^*)^ n$, with the normalization fixed above. Volumes and distances in this section are real Euclidean ones.

Lemma 16.42.1.CBCC93967447 Let $H\subset \mathbb R^ n$ be a convex body containing zero in its interior, and let $h(y)=\max _{\eta \in H}y\cdot \eta $ be its support function. As a locally bounded PSH function of $z=x+iy$, it satisfies

\[ (dd^ ch(\operatorname {Im}z))^ n =\frac{n!}{(4\pi )^ n}\operatorname {vol}(H)\, dx\otimes \delta _0(dy). \]

Proof. For a smooth convex function $f(y)$, the determinant formula gives

\[ (dd^ cf)^ n=\frac{n!}{(4\pi )^ n}\det D^2f(y)\, dx\, dy =(4\pi )^{-n}dx\otimes \operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(f). \]

Here the canonical real Monge–Ampère measure includes the factor $n!$. Convolve a finite convex $f$ with smooth kernels. The convolutions converge locally uniformly, so bounded Bedford–Taylor continuity and real Monge–Ampère continuity give the same identity for $f$. There is no assertion of convergence against noncompactly supported tests.

For the support function, $\partial h(0)=H$, while every supporting slope at $y\ne 0$ belongs to the exposed face $\{ \eta \in H:y\cdot \eta =h(y)\} $, hence to $\partial H$. The boundary of a convex body has zero Lebesgue measure. The canonical subgradient-image definition therefore gives $\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(h)=n!\operatorname {vol}(H)\delta _0$. Substitution proves the formula. $\square$

Proposition 16.42.2.B7B8F93CC817 Let $K\subset \mathbb R^ n$ be compact with nonempty real interior, $d(x)=\operatorname {dist}(x,\partial K)$ and $D=\operatorname {diam}K$. Then on $\operatorname {int}K$ the measure $\mu _ K$ has a density $f_ K$, locally bounded above and bounded below by a positive constant. More precisely, almost everywhere there,

\[ (2\pi D)^{-n}\leq f_ K(x) \leq \left(\frac{\sqrt n}{2\pi d(x)}\right)^ n. \]

No conclusion about $\mu _ K(\partial K)$ is included in this assertion.

Proof. The cube formula of proposition 16.36.1, followed by a real translation, dilation and orthogonal change of coordinates, gives the density of every real cube. A real cube is nonpluripolar, so the presence of an interior cube in $K$ also makes $V_ K^*$ finite and locally bounded. It is zero on the real interior of $K$.

Enclose $K$ in a cube of half-side $D$ centered at a point of $K$. Obstacle monotonicity gives $V_ Q\leq V_ K^*$. On $\operatorname {int}K$ both functions vanish. The bounded ordered-contact inequality gives $\mu _ Q\leq \mu _ K$ there. The cube density is at least $(2\pi D)^{-n}$ at every interior point of $Q$; its real boundary is pluripolar, so the possible contact with that boundary causes no exceptional positive measure.

Conversely, around any $x\in \operatorname {int}K$ and for $0<r<d(x)/\sqrt n$, the centered cube $Q_ r$ of half-side $r$ lies in $\operatorname {int}K$. Thus $V_ K^*\leq V_{Q_ r}$ and contact gives $\mu _ K\leq \mu _{Q_ r}$ on its interior. A countable covering by smaller cubes proves local domination by Lebesgue measure; the canonical dominated-measure density lemma supplies $f_ K$. At every Lebesgue point of this density, compare averages over small balls centered at $x$ with the continuous cube density. This yields $f_ K(x)\leq (2\pi r)^{-n}$. Let $r\uparrow d(x)/\sqrt n$. The enclosing cube similarly gives the lower bound. $\square$

Proposition 16.42.3.86440B2CBCE4 Let $K\subset \mathbb R^ n$ be a convex body symmetric about zero, and let $K^\circ $ be its polar body. For $x\in \operatorname {int}K$ put

\[ S_ x(\eta )=\frac{\eta }{\sqrt{1-(x\cdot \eta )^2}},\qquad H_ x=\operatorname {conv}S_ x(K^\circ ). \]

Then the interior density has the continuous representative

\[ f_ K(x)=\frac{n!}{(4\pi )^ n}\operatorname {vol}(H_ x). \]

If $S_ x(K^\circ )$ is convex, this is equivalently

\[ f_ K(x)=\frac{(n-1)!}{(4\pi )^ n} \int _{|\theta |=1} \frac{d\sigma (\theta )}{[p_ K(\theta )^2-(x\cdot \theta )^2]^{n/2}}, \qquad p_ K(\theta )=\max _{t\in K}t\cdot \theta . \]

The volume formula is an interior assertion without any boundary smoothness assumption.

Proof. The real-body formula of theorem 16.33.6 gives

\[ V_ K(z)=\max _{\eta \in K^\circ }\psi (\eta \cdot z), \qquad \psi (w)=\log |w+\sqrt{w^2-1}|. \]

On compact real subintervals of $(-1,1)$ choose the branch of $\arccos w$ which is real there. The Joukowski formula says $\psi (w)=|\operatorname {Im}\arccos w|$. Taylor's formula consequently gives, uniformly for such real $s$ and small real $t$,

\[ \psi (s+it)=\frac{|t|}{\sqrt{1-s^2}}+O(t^2). \]

If $x$ ranges over a compact subset of $\operatorname {int}K$, $|x\cdot \eta |$ is uniformly less than one for $\eta \in K^\circ $. Taking the maximum in the last estimate therefore gives

\[ V_ K(x+iy)=h_ x(y)+O(|y|^2),\qquad h_ x(y)=\max _{\eta \in K^\circ } \frac{y\cdot \eta }{\sqrt{1-(x\cdot \eta )^2}}. \]

Symmetry removes the absolute value. The function $h_ x$ is the support function of $H_ x$. On each compact set of real base points, it is bounded below by $c|y|$, with $c>0$, and depends continuously on $x$, uniformly for $|y|=1$. Thus, for fixed $x_0$ and any $0<\epsilon <1$, on a sufficiently small product neighborhood of $x_0+i0$ one has

\[ (1-\epsilon )h_{x_0}(y)\leq V_ K(x+iy) \leq (1+\epsilon )h_{x_0}(y). \]

All three functions are PSH and have the common zero set $y=0$ there. Apply the ordered-contact inequality twice and use lemma 16.42.1. On the small real neighborhood the measure $\mu _ K$ is bounded below and above by

\[ (1\mp \epsilon )^ n\frac{n!}{(4\pi )^ n} \operatorname {vol}(H_{x_0})\, dx. \]

At every Lebesgue point of $f_ K$, let $\epsilon \downarrow 0$. This proves the formula almost everywhere. The bodies $H_ x$ depend continuously on $x$ in Hausdorff distance, and their volumes are continuous by the canonical convex-body volume theorem. The displayed formula is therefore a continuous representative.

On the ray $r\theta $, the polar body has endpoint $\theta /p_ K(\theta )$. Its image under $S_ x$ has endpoint $\theta /[p_ K(\theta )^2-(x\cdot \theta )^2]^{1/2}$. When this image is convex its volume is the polar-coordinate integral of the $n$th power of that radial function divided by $n$. This gives the asserted integral and its factor $(n-1)!$. $\square$

Lemma 16.42.4.16C56B7B3FDA Every real convex body with $C^2$ boundary admits convex bodies with real-analytic boundary converging to it in $C^2$. The approximants have a common interior rolling-ball radius, uniformly bounded boundary area and a common tubular neighborhood with uniformly bounded normal coordinate Jacobians. Symmetry can be preserved if present, but is not required.

Proof. Translate zero into the interior and let $p$ be the Minkowski functional. Each ray meets the boundary transversely, since its outward supporting normal has positive pairing with the radial vector. The implicit-function theorem in boundary charts gives $p\in C^2(\mathbb R^ n\setminus \{ 0\} )$. The function $p^2$ is convex and has quadratic growth. Convolve it with a centered Gaussian of variance tending to zero, and add $\epsilon |x|^2$. The resulting $q_\epsilon $ is real analytic, coercive and strictly convex. Analyticity follows by extending the Gaussian kernel to a complex neighborhood and differentiating its absolutely convergent integral; quadratic growth gives the required domination. Near the original boundary $q_\epsilon \to p^2$ in $C^2$. For this local assertion split the integral into a small neighborhood, where $p^2$ is $C^2$, and its complement, where the differentiated Gaussian tails tend to zero even after multiplication by a polynomial. The level sets $q_\epsilon =1$ therefore converge in $C^2$ by the implicit-function theorem. They bound the desired convex bodies. An even Gaussian and the even quadratic preserve symmetry.

Here are the uniform geometric consequences. A finite collection of boundary graph charts has uniformly bounded second derivatives and fixed chart sizes, also for these $C^2$-close boundaries. A ball centered a distance $r$ inward along the normal is contained in the interior if $r$ is sufficiently small uniformly in the charts: its upper spherical cap lies below the quadratic lower bound for the boundary graph when $1/(2r)$ exceeds the common second-derivative bound. Choose $2r$ smaller than the chart heights and widths, so the entire ball stays in that chart. This proves a common interior rolling radius. The finite graph charts give a uniform area bound. Finally the normal map $(p,t)\mapsto p-t\nu (p)$ has invertible derivative for uniformly small $|t|$, since the derivatives of $\nu $ are uniformly bounded. Local injectivity follows in the same fixed charts; points outside one chart are separated by a uniform positive distance, so decreasing the radius makes the map globally injective. Its Jacobian is the product of $1-t\kappa _ j(p)$ and is uniformly bounded. These statements also hold in dimension one, where the boundary consists of endpoints. $\square$

Theorem 16.42.5.1E10E926515B Let $K\subset \mathbb R^ n$ be a convex body with $C^2$ boundary. Then $\mu _ K$ gives no mass to $\partial K$ and is absolutely continuous on all of $K$. There are constants $0<c\leq C<\infty $ such that

\[ \frac{c}{\sqrt{\operatorname {dist}(x,\partial K)}} \leq f_ K(x)\leq \frac{C}{\sqrt{\operatorname {dist}(x,\partial K)}} \quad \text{for almost every }x\in \operatorname {int}K. \]

No symmetry or positive lower bound for the principal curvatures is assumed. If $K$ is symmetric, its density is the polar-volume formula of proposition 16.42.3.

Proof. First compute the measure of a real ball from the preceding interior formula. For $K=\overline{B_{\mathbb R^ n}(0,R)}$, the image $S_ x(K^\circ )$ is the ellipsoid

\[ R^2|\xi |^2-(x\cdot \xi )^2\leq 1. \]

Its volume is $R^{-n}\operatorname {vol}(B_{\mathbb R^ n}(0,1)) (1-|x|^2/R^2)^{-1/2}$. Thus the ball density is this expression multiplied by $n!/(4\pi )^ n$. Its real sphere is contained in the holomorphic hypersurface $\sum z_ j^2=R^2$, hence is pluripolar and receives no mass.

Fix a boundary point $p$, write $\nu (p)$ for its outward unit normal and $x=p-d\nu (p)$ for an interior point sufficiently near it. A ball of fixed radius $r$ centered at $p-r\nu (p)$ lies inside $K$. Its distance from $x$ to its boundary is $d$. Since $V_ K\leq V_ B$, contact on the ball interior bounds $f_ K(x)$ above by the ball density, which is at most $C d^{-1/2}$ for $0<d<r$.

For the lower bound take an enclosing cube with one face in the supporting hyperplane at $p$, that face centered at $p$, and side length $4\operatorname {diam}K$. It contains $K$. At $x$ the distance to that face is $d$, while the remaining face distances have a fixed positive lower bound. The cube formula and $V_ Q\leq V_ K$ give $f_ K(x)\geq c d^{-1/2}$. The comparisons are justified at every Lebesgue point of $f_ K$ by averaging on a small neighborhood of $x$, where the comparison density is continuous. Interior compact sets are handled by proposition 16.42.2. This proves the asserted two-sided bounds on the interior, with constants controlled by the diameter, rolling radius and an interior ball.

If the boundary is real analytic it is locally contained in a holomorphic hypersurface: complexify its nonzero real-analytic defining function in the real coordinates. A finite covering of the compact boundary shows pluripolarity. The locally bounded product $\mu _ K$ therefore gives it zero mass.

For general $C^2$ boundary take the analytic convex approximants $K_ j$ of lemma 16.42.4. Their interior bounds have common constants, and their boundaries have zero mass. Normal coordinates and the uniform area/Jacobian bounds give

\[ \mu _{K_ j}\{ x\in K_ j: \operatorname {dist}(x,\partial K_ j)<s\} \leq C'\sqrt s \]

for all sufficiently small $s$, uniformly in $j$.

All these bodies contain a fixed ball. Their radial $C^2$ convergence gives $(1-\delta _ j)K\subset K_ j\subset (1+\delta _ j)K$, with $\delta _ j\to 0$. The continuous real-body extremal and obstacle monotonicity squeeze $V_{K_ j}$ between $V_ K(z/(1+\delta _ j))$ and $V_ K(z/(1-\delta _ j))$. It follows that $V_{K_ j}\to V_ K$ locally uniformly, and hence $\mu _{K_ j}\to \mu _ K$ weakly on compact sets. For the open real-space tube $U_ s=\{ x:\operatorname {dist}(x,\partial K)<s\} $, boundary convergence gives, for large $j$, $K_ j\cap U_ s\subset \{ x\in K_ j:d(x,\partial K_ j)<2s\} $. The measures are supported in one fixed compact subset of $\mathbb R^ n$; their ambient weak convergence is also weak convergence on that real space, by extending real test functions with a cutoff in $y$. The open-set inequality for weak convergence yields

\[ \mu _ K(U_ s)\leq \liminf _ j\mu _{K_ j}(U_ s)\leq C'\sqrt{2s}. \]

Let $s\downarrow 0$. This proves $\mu _ K(\partial K)=0$. Together with the interior density and support on $K$, it proves absolute continuity and completes the theorem. $\square$

Theorem 16.42.6.69C6DC7FA336 Let $K\subset \mathbb R^ n$ be a convex body symmetric about zero, with nonempty interior. No boundary regularity is assumed. Then

\[ \mu _ K(\partial K)=0,\qquad \mu _ K=f_ K\, dx\quad \hbox{on }K,\qquad \int _{\operatorname {int}K}f_ K\, dx=2^{-n}, \]

where $f_ K$ is the continuous interior density in proposition 16.42.3. In fact, writing $M=2^{-n}$, for $0<r<1$ one has

\[ \mu _ K\{ x\in K:m_ K(x)\geq r\} \leq \frac{2(n+1)M\log (1/r)}{\operatorname {arcosh}(1/r)}. \]

Here $m_ K$ denotes the Minkowski functional of $K$, with $m_ K(x)=\inf \{ s>0:x\in sK\} $.

Proof. Put $U=V_ K$ and $\gamma (z)=\tfrac 12\log (1+|z|^2)$. The affine/projective dictionary extends $q=U-\gamma $ to a bounded $\omega $-PSH function on $X=\mathbb P^ n$, where $\omega =dd^ c\gamma $ on the affine chart and $\int _ X\omega ^ n=M$. We use the existing bounded-type energy cocycle:

\[ E_\omega (b)-E_\omega (a) =\frac1{n+1}\sum _{j=0}^ n\int _ X(b-a) (\omega +dd^ cb)^ j\wedge (\omega +dd^ ca)^{n-j}, \]

with $E_\omega (0)=0$. This formula applies to bounded potentials; it does not require differentiating the possibly nonsmooth extremal. Its reference potential is zero, which is a model potential for the Kähler form $\omega $ since the envelope below zero is zero.

For $t>1$ let $F_ t[Z_0:Z']=[Z_0:tZ']$ and put

\[ a_ t=\frac12\log \frac{|Z_0|^2+t^2|Z'|^2}{|Z_0|^2+|Z'|^2}, \qquad b_ t=q\circ F_ t+a_ t. \]

Thus $0\leq a_ t\leq \log t$, $\omega +dd^ ca_ t=F_ t^*\omega $, and $\omega +dd^ cb_ t=F_ t^*(\omega +dd^ cq)$. Apply the cocycle to $a_ t,b_ t$ and change variables by the projective automorphism $F_ t$. Each of its $n+1$ mixed integrals becomes the corresponding integral defining $E_\omega (q)$, because $b_ t-a_ t=q\circ F_ t$. Consequently

\[ E_\omega (b_ t)-E_\omega (q)=E_\omega (a_ t) \leq M\log t. \]

The inequality follows either from monotonicity and constant shifts of the same cocycle, or from $0\leq a_ t\leq \log t$ and the common mixed mass $M$. Pullback commutes with the bounded products by their local definition.

On the affine chart $b_ t-q=U(tz)-U(z)\geq 0$: indeed $U(tz)=V_{t^{-1}K}(z)$ and $t^{-1}K\subset K$. This ordering holds on the hyperplane at infinity as well, since $F_ t$ is its identity and $a_ t=\log t$ there. Apply the cocycle now to $q,b_ t$. All its terms are nonnegative, and retaining its $j=0$ term gives

\[ \int _ X(b_ t-q)(\omega +dd^ cq)^ n \leq (n+1)M\log t. \]

The bounded measure gives no mass to the hyperplane at infinity. By proposition 16.34.2, its affine measure is supported on $K$, where $U=0$. We have therefore proved

\[ \int _ K U(tx)\, d\mu _ K(x)\leq (n+1)M\log t. \]

For every $x\in K$ the bipolar identity gives $m_ K(x)=\max _{\eta \in K^\circ }x\cdot \eta $. If $t m_ K(x)>1$, a maximizing functional and theorem 16.33.6 give $U(tx)\geq \operatorname {arcosh}(t m_ K(x))$. Thus for $tr>1$ the last integral bounds $\operatorname {arcosh}(tr)\, \mu _ K\{ m_ K\geq r\} $. Take $t=r^{-2}$ to obtain the displayed layer estimate. Its right side tends to zero as $r\uparrow 1$, since $\log (1/r)/\operatorname {arcosh}(1/r)\to 0$. The decreasing layers have intersection $\partial K$, so finite-measure continuity proves zero boundary mass. The preceding interior-density formula, support on $K$ and total mass $M$ prove the remaining assertions. $\square$

Sources. Bedford–Taylor, The complex equilibrium measure of a symmetric convex set in $\mathbb R^ n$ [BedfordTaylor1986], Theorems 1.1–1.3 and 3.2; Klimek, Pluripotential Theory, p. 219. The canonical ordered-contact theorem replaces the cited Levenberg input. Real Monge–Ampère construction/continuity and convex-volume continuity are reused. The approximation is proved for arbitrary smooth convex bodies. Source-normalized equilibrium measures are divided by $(4\pi )^ n$. The rough symmetric-body boundary assertion is proved here by the canonical bounded energy cocycle and a projective dilation, independently of a rolling-ball approximation or an unverified limit of densities.
