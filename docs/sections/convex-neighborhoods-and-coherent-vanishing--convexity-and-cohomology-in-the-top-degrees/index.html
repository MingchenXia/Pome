9.52 Convexity and cohomology in the top degreesC1F06AA2AAF1

C1F06AA2AAF1

Complex spaces are Hausdorff and have a countable basis. Their dimension is that of their reduction. Smooth functions and strong $q$-convexity, $q\geq 1$, have the local ambient-extension meaning of lemma 9.49.1; thus the geometric assertions also apply to nonreduced spaces. An exhaustion has compact closed sublevels. On a Hermitian manifold put $L_\omega f=\operatorname {Tr}_\omega (\mathrm i\partial \bar\partial f)$. This is a real elliptic operator with no zeroth-order term. In holomorphic real coordinates it has the form $\sum a^{ij}(x)\partial _ i\partial _ j$ with symmetric positive definite real matrix $a$. In particular $L_\omega f>0$ implies strong $n$-convexity in complex dimension $n$.

Lemma 9.52.1.88C2BC468CBC Let $U,W$ be open in a Hermitian manifold. Suppose every component $U_ s$ of $U$ meets a component $W_{t(s)}$ of $W$ for which

2A3AB1116C31 9.52.2
\begin{equation} W_{t(s)}\setminus \overline U\ne \varnothing . \end{equation}

There is a smooth nonnegative $v$, zero outside $U\cup W$, such that $v>0$ and $L_\omega v>0$ on $U$, and $L_\omega v\geq 0$ off $W$. If $U$ has finitely many components, the set where $L_\omega v<0$ has closure in a compact subset of $W\setminus \overline U$. In all cases $\operatorname {supp}v\subset \overline U\cup \overline W$.

Proof. The case $U=\varnothing $ has the solution $v=0$. Here is a local construction allowing arbitrarily thin cylinders. Normalize the metric at the center of a holomorphic chart and use a unitary rotation to put the chosen real direction on its first axis. On a sufficiently small chart there are constants $c_0,C_0>0$ such that $a^{11}\geq c_0$, the transverse quadratic form is at least $c_0|\xi '|^2$, and $\sum _{j>1}a^{jj}\leq C_0$. We may also require $|(a^{12},\ldots ,a^{1,2n})|\leq c_0/12$: these mixed coefficients vanish at the center and are continuous.

Choose a longitudinal half-length $\ell $ and a transverse radius $\rho $ inside this chart, put $u=x_1+\ell $ and $T=\rho ^2-|x'|^2$, and on $|x_1|<\ell $, $|x'|<\rho $ set $v=\chi _ A(u)e^{-1/T}$, where $\chi _ A(u)=u e^{-A/u^2}$ for $u>0$ and is zero for $u\leq 0$. Multiply its continuation in $x_1$ by a nonnegative smooth cutoff equal to one for $x_1\leq \ell $ and zero before $x_1=\ell +\epsilon $. Extend by zero across the transverse boundary. The flat exponential factors make all these extensions smooth, and any negative Laplacian outside the main cylinder is confined to the exit cap $\ell <x_1<\ell +\epsilon $, $|x'|<\rho $. Direct differentiation on the main cylinder gives

\[ \frac{\chi _ A'}{\chi _ A}=u^{-1}+2Au^{-3},\quad \frac{\chi _ A''}{\chi _ A}=4A^2u^{-6}-2Au^{-4},\quad \frac{\partial _ j e^{-1/T}}{e^{-1/T}}=-2x_ jT^{-2}, \]

and, for $j,k>1$,

\[ \frac{\partial _{jk}e^{-1/T}}{e^{-1/T}} =(4-8T)x_ jx_ kT^{-4}-2\delta _{jk}T^{-2}. \]

The mixed sum in $L_\omega v/v$ is $-4\sum _{j>1}a^{1j}(u^{-1}+2Au^{-3})x_ jT^{-2}$. Take $A\geq (2\ell )^2$ and $\rho ^2\leq 1/4$. Then $\chi _ A'/\chi _ A\leq 3Au^{-3}$, $\chi _ A''/\chi _ A\geq 2A^2u^{-6}$ and $4-8T\geq 2$. The absolute value of the mixed sum is at most $c_0Au^{-3}|x'|T^{-2}$, hence at most $\tfrac 12c_0(A^2u^{-6}+|x'|^2T^{-4})$. We obtain, with $c=c_0$ and $C=2C_0$,

EE28C5815630 9.52.3
\begin{equation} L_\omega v/v\geq c A^2u^{-6}+c|x'|^2T^{-4}-C T^{-2}. \end{equation}

Where $|x'|^2\geq 2CT^2/c$, the last two terms are nonnegative. Else $|x'|^2\leq (2C/c)\rho ^4$, so $T\geq \rho ^2/2$ if $\rho ^2\leq c/(4C)$. The negative part is then at most $4C\rho ^{-4}$. Choose $A$ still larger so that $cA^2(2\ell )^{-6}>4C\rho ^{-4}$. This proves $L_\omega v>0$ on the cylinder, for arbitrarily small $\rho $ relative to the fixed short longitudinal segment and with any prescribed small exit cap. Setting $A=1$ in the differentiation formulas also gives the corrected original longitudinal factors.

Fix a small ball $B\Subset W_{t(s)}\setminus \overline U$ and a point $p\in U_ s$. A path in $U_ s\cup W_{t(s)}$ joins $p$ to $B$. It can be taken piecewise straight in finitely many holomorphic charts, with all exit junctions different from $p$. Indeed join successive points in overlapping coordinate balls and, after leaving a small ball about $p$, use that a connected real manifold of dimension at least two remains path connected after one point is removed. This last fact follows by replacing passages through that point by arcs in a punctured coordinate ball. Subdivide the compact path into sufficiently short chart segments. Working backwards, the thin-cylinder construction gives finitely many bumps $b_0,\ldots ,b_ N$ with main cylinders $D_ j$ and exit caps $E_ j$ such that

\[ p\in D_0,\qquad \overline E_ j\subset D_{j+1},\qquad \overline E_ N\subset B. \]

All cylinder closures lie in $U_ s\cup W_{t(s)}$; their closed exit caps avoid $p$. Backwards choice is possible since the next main cylinder contains its junction as an interior point, and the preceding exit cap can have arbitrarily small transverse radius. Each $b_ j$ has positive Laplacian on $D_ j$, and its negative Laplacian is confined to $E_ j$. Inductively choose positive coefficients so that $w_ p=b_0+C_1b_1+\cdots +C_ Nb_ N$ has nonnegative Laplacian off $E_ N$ and strictly positive Laplacian near $p$. At each step the previous possible negative part lies on the compact $\overline E_{j-1}$, where $L_\omega b_ j$ has a positive minimum, so a finite coefficient suffices. Previously nonnegative points outside the new exit cap stay nonnegative; near $p$ every new Laplacian is nonnegative. Also $w_ p>0$ near $p$, and it has compact support in $U_ s\cup W_{t(s)}$.

For each $s$ use one fixed sink ball $B_ s$ as above. Choose countably many of these positive neighborhoods covering $U_ s$. Take a positive linear combination of their $w_ p$'s converging with every derivative on compact subsets of the entire manifold. Explicitly, for a compact exhaustion $K_ l$, make the $j$th coefficient at most $2^{-j}/(1+\max _{l,k\leq j}\| w_{p_ j}\| _{C^ k(K_ l)})$. The sum is globally smooth, nonnegative, positive and strictly subharmonic on $U_ s$, and its negative Laplacian is confined to $\overline B_ s$. Sum in the same way over components. All sinks lie outside $\overline U$, so terms from other components have nonnegative Laplacian on $U$; this proves the assertion on the entire $U$. If there are finitely many components the finite union of sink closures is compact inside $W\setminus \overline U$. $\square$

Example 9.52.4.E0D7BF24D793 In the preceding lemma one cannot replace $\overline U$ in (9.52.2) by $\overline{U_ s}$. On $M=\mathbb P^1$ with its Fubini–Study metric, take $U=\mathbb P^1\setminus \{ |z|=1\} $ and $W=M$. Its two components both meet $W$ and each has proper closure. Yet a smooth function with $L_\omega v>0$ on the dense open set $U$ would have $L_\omega v\geq 0$ everywhere and a strictly positive integral. Stokes gives $\int _ M L_\omega v\, dV_\omega =0$, a contradiction.

Lemma 9.52.5.4A19B00897C5 Let $Z$ be connected, locally connected, locally compact and Hausdorff. If $O\Subset Z$ is open, adjoining all compact connected components of $Z\setminus O$ gives an open relatively compact set whose complement has finitely many components, all noncompact. If $K\subset Z$ is compact, adjoining all relatively compact components of the open set $Z\setminus K$ gives a compact set $\widehat K$; every component of $Z\setminus \widehat K$ has noncompact closure. If $K$ is connected, so is $\widehat K$. If in addition $Z$ has a countable basis and is noncompact, there are connected compact sets $K_\nu \subset \operatorname {int}K_{\nu +1}$ exhausting $Z$, with all complementary open components having noncompact closure.

Proof. Every compact set is contained in a connected relatively compact open set $V$. Cover it by finitely many connected relatively compact neighborhoods and connect them by finite chains of such neighborhoods. The set of points accessible by these chains from one neighborhood is both open and closed, hence all of the connected space. The finite union used for the compact set is the desired $V$.

Choose such a $V$ containing $\overline O$. Every component $T$ of the closed set $Z\setminus O$, when $O$ is nonempty, meets $\partial O$: otherwise its points have connected open neighborhoods disjoint from $\overline O$, so $T$ would be open and closed in $Z$. Every $T$ not contained in $\overline V$ consequently meets $\partial V$. A finite cover of the compact $\partial V$ by connected neighborhoods disjoint from $\overline O$ shows that there are only finitely many such $T$. Every noncompact $T$ is among them. The compact components not among them lie in $\overline V$; adding the finitely many other compact components still gives a relatively compact set. Its complement is a finite union of closed noncompact components, hence is closed. This proves the first assertion. The empty $O$ case is immediate.

For compact $K$ the components $G$ of $Z\setminus K$ are open, and $\partial G\subset K$. If $K$ is nonempty each $\overline G$ meets $K$, or $G$ would be open and closed in $Z$. A connected relatively compact neighborhood $V$ of $K$ and the same finite boundary cover show that only finitely many $G$ extend outside $\overline V$. Thus all the relatively compact $G$ together with $K$ lie in a compact set. Their union with $K$ is closed, since its complement is the union of the remaining open components, and is therefore compact. Adjoining connected sets whose closures touch a connected $K$ preserves connectedness. Start with larger and larger connected compact closures of relatively compact open neighborhoods of a countable compact exhaustion, including the preceding $K_\nu $ in their interiors, and fill them by this operation. This proves the last assertion. Empty $K$ causes no exception. $\square$

Lemma 9.52.6.1B57CC3FFF8A Let $Z$ be a connected noncompact normal complex space, let $A$ be a nowhere dense analytic subset, and suppose $N=Z\setminus A$ is smooth. For an open neighborhood $V$ of $A$ there are open $U_\nu \Subset N$, each with finitely many components, locally finite in $Z$, covering $Z\setminus V$, such that each component of $U_\nu $ meets a component of $U_{\nu +1}$ containing a point outside $\overline{U_\nu }$. Thus the whole-open sink condition holds. There are also compact sets $C_\nu \subset U_\nu $ whose union is $Z\setminus V$. The same conclusion holds when $A=V=\varnothing $ and $Z$ is a connected noncompact complex manifold.

Proof. Normal spaces are locally irreducible. The checked the topology of unibranch normalization gives arbitrarily small connected neighborhoods whose complements of $A$ are connected and dense. Consequently every connected open set $G\subset Z$ has connected complement $G\setminus A$: otherwise a separation of that complement extends to a separation of $G$, since each such small neighborhood meets precisely one of its two sides. This argument also proves local connectedness of $Z$.

Take the filled connected compact exhaustion $K_\nu $ of lemma 9.52.5; put $K_\nu =\varnothing $ for negative indices. Define

\[ C_\nu =(K_\nu \setminus \operatorname {int}K_{\nu -1})\setminus V, \qquad G_\nu =\operatorname {int}K_{\nu +1}\setminus K_{\nu -2}. \]

Each $C_\nu $ is compact in $G_\nu \setminus A$. No component $G$ of $G_\nu $ is contained in $K_\nu $. Otherwise it would be open and closed in $Z\setminus K_{\nu -2}$: its closure is in $K_\nu \Subset \operatorname {int}K_{\nu +1}$, and components of $G_\nu $ are closed there. It would then be a component with compact closure, contrary to the filled property. Thus $(G\setminus A)\setminus K_\nu $ is a nonempty open set, using nowhere density of $A$.

Inductively also include finitely many points $F_{\nu -1}$ from the preceding step, initially empty, in $U_\nu $. They lie in $G_\nu \setminus A$. The compact $C_\nu \cup F_{\nu -1}$ meets only finitely many components $G_ s$ of $G_\nu $, because those components are open. The intersection with each $G_ s$ is compact. Inside the connected manifold $G_ s\setminus A$ choose a connected relatively compact open $U_{\nu ,s}$ containing that intersection and with an ordinary smooth boundary point $a_ s$ outside $K_\nu $. For completeness this elementary choice needs no global regular-value theorem: cover the compact intersection by finitely many small balls, join them and a ball in $(G_ s\setminus A)\setminus K_\nu $ by finitely many coordinate arcs and thin open tubes, and retain a free spherical cap of the last ball. The resulting relatively compact connected union has the required smooth boundary near any point of that cap. Set $U_\nu =\bigcup _ s U_{\nu ,s}$ and $F_\nu =\{ a_ s\} _ s$. The cap points lie in $G_{\nu +1}\setminus A$ since they are outside $K_\nu $ and inside $\operatorname {int}K_{\nu +1}$.

The next $U_{\nu +1}$ contains each $a_ s$ as an interior point. Its component there meets $U_{\nu ,s}$ and contains points just outside its closure. The other finitely many $U_{\nu ,t}$ lie in different components of $G_\nu $, so their closures do not meet a neighborhood of $a_ s$ in $G_ s$. Those exterior points are therefore outside the closure of the entire $U_\nu $, as required. Finally $U_\nu \Subset G_\nu $ makes the family of their closures locally finite in $Z$, and $C_\nu \subset U_\nu $ proves the covering property. The compact sets $C_\nu $ have union exactly $Z\setminus V$. $\square$

Corollary 9.52.7.073A6ABDD1C9 Let $Z$ be a noncompact irreducible reduced complex space, let $A$ be a nowhere dense analytic subset containing $Z_{\mathrm{sing}}$, and let $V$ be a neighborhood of $A$. The conclusions about open sets and compact subsets in lemma 9.52.6 hold in $Z\setminus A$, with the closures locally finite in $Z$.

Proof. Take the finite normalization $\nu :\widetilde Z\to Z$ from normalization of reduced complex spaces. The regular locus of $Z$ is connected and dense by global irreducible analytic components; its proof uses only local embedding patches and therefore applies to a reduced complex space. The inverse image of that locus is isomorphic to it and dense in $\widetilde Z$, by bimeromorphy, so $\widetilde Z$ is connected. It is noncompact, since a compact source would have compact image. The analytic set $\widetilde A=\nu ^{-1}(A)$ is nowhere dense by nowhere-dense inverse images under bimeromorphic maps, and the restriction $\widetilde Z\setminus \widetilde A\to Z\setminus A$ is an isomorphism. Apply the preceding lemma with $\nu ^{-1}(V)$.

Transport its open sets and compact targets through this isomorphism. Each open set has compact closure in the regular locus. Its image closure is precisely the image of its closure, because the latter is compact and $Z$ is Hausdorff. Thus the intersection and whole-closure escape assertions are preserved. Given a compact neighborhood $K$ of a point of $Z$, its inverse image is compact by properness and meets only finitely many shell closures. Their images therefore form a locally finite family in $Z$. Surjectivity and $\nu ^{-1}(Z\setminus V)=\widetilde Z\setminus \nu ^{-1}(V)$ give the required covering by compact targets. $\square$

Theorem 9.52.8.89227CC07C7F Every connected noncompact complex manifold of positive dimension has a smooth strictly $\omega $-subharmonic exhaustion for any prescribed smooth Hermitian metric $\omega $. In particular it is strongly $n$-complete in dimension $n$.

Proof. Take any nonnegative smooth exhaustion $f$. Apply lemma 9.52.6 with $A=V=\varnothing $. For each $\nu $, lemma 9.52.1 gives a nonnegative $v_\nu $ with $L_\omega v_\nu >0$ on $U_\nu $, support in $\overline{U_\nu }\cup \overline{U_{\nu +1}}$, and possible negative Laplacian confined to a compact $S_\nu \subset U_{\nu +1}$. Choose $S_\nu $ to contain its entire closed negative locus; it lies outside $\overline{U_\nu }$. Put $S_{-1}=\varnothing $. Choose $c_\nu >0$ successively so that

\[ L_\omega \left(f+\sum _{\mu \leq \nu }c_\mu v_\mu \right)>0 \quad \hbox{on } C_\nu \cup S_{\nu -1}. \]

This is possible because that set is compact inside $U_\nu $, where $L_\omega v_\nu $ has a positive minimum. The sum $\psi =f+\sum _\nu c_\nu v_\nu $ is locally finite and smooth, and is exhaustive since $\psi \geq f$. At any point only finitely many summands act. If a negative Laplacian summand occurs there, its last index is followed by a step that makes the full partial sum strictly positive on its $S_\nu $; all later summands have nonnegative Laplacian there. If none occurs, use a $C_\nu $ containing the point and its defining positivity step. Thus $L_\omega \psi >0$ everywhere. $\square$

Lemma 9.52.9.61FD8A8E66FF If a closed analytic $Y\subset X$ is strongly $q$-complete, there is a nonnegative smooth exhaustion $f$ on $X$ which is strongly $q$-convex on a neighborhood of $Y$.

Proof. If $Y=\varnothing $, take any nonnegative smooth exhaustion of $X$. Assume henceforth that $Y$ is nonempty. Use proposition 9.49.4 to extend its exhaustion approximately, with error less than one, to a strongly $q$-convex $f_0$ on a neighborhood $W$. Shift it so that $f_0\geq 1$ on $Y$. Its restriction to $Y$ still has compact closed sublevels. Let $r$ be a nonnegative smooth exhaustion of $X$. The quantities $\sup _{Y\cap \{ f_0\leq j\} }r$ are finite; choose a continuous increasing function $a:\mathbb R\to (0,\infty )$ majorizing them strictly on the preceding integer bands, assigning zero to an empty supremum. For example prescribe $a(j)>1+ \sup _{Y\cap \{ f_0\leq j+1\} }r$ for $j\geq 0$ and interpolate increasingly. Then $r<a(f_0)$ on $Y$. Shrink $W$ to the open neighborhood where $f_0>0$ and $r<a(f_0)$, and choose a closed neighborhood $D\subset W$ of $Y$, using the locally finite smooth partitions of lemma 9.49.1. On $D$, every closed $f_0$ sublevel is closed in $X$ and lies in a compact $r$ sublevel, so is compact. Take a smooth cutoff $0\leq \eta \leq 1$ equal to one on a smaller neighborhood of $Y$ and supported in the interior of $D$, and set $f=\eta f_0+(1-\eta )r$. It is smooth, nonnegative and agrees with $f_0$ near $Y$. A sublevel $f\leq c$ lies in the union of the compact sets $D\cap \{ f_0\leq 2c\} $ and $\{ r\leq 2c\} $, according as $\eta \geq 1/2$ or $\eta <1/2$. It is closed there, hence compact. $\square$

Theorem 9.52.10.E86D84BDEFC8 Let $X$ be a complex space, possibly nonreduced, of dimension at most an integer $n\geq 0$. It is strongly $(n+1)$-complete. For $n\geq 1$:

  1. If $X$ has no compact irreducible component of dimension $n$, then it is strongly $n$-complete.

  2. If it has only finitely many compact irreducible components of dimension $n$, then it has a smooth exhaustion strongly $n$-convex outside a compact set.

Only compact top-dimensional components are counted in the last hypothesis; there can be infinitely many noncompact ones.

Proof. The geometric statements depend only on the reduction. We prove the first assertion and item1 by dimension induction. A zero-dimensional space is discrete, with a smooth exhaustion; in a zero-dimensional local model the strong1 condition is vacuous. Suppose the assertions below dimension $n$ are known. The dimension filtration of lemma 9.49.3 supplies a closed analytic $Y$ with $\dim Y\leq n-1$ and smooth pure $n$-dimensional complement $N$. If $\dim X<n$, the inductive assertion and monotonicity in $q$ already give the required conclusions. Otherwise $Y$ is strongly $n$-complete by induction. Use lemma 9.52.9 to get a nonnegative exhaustion $f$ strongly $n$-convex near $Y$. On $N$ any smooth function is strongly $(n+1)$-convex, since its dimension is $n$; the embedding-independent convention then proves the first assertion on all $X$.

For item1, the components $N_ j$ of $N$ have closures equal to the $n$-dimensional irreducible components $Z_ j$ of $X$, by global irreducible analytic components. They are noncompact by hypothesis. On a neighborhood of $Y$ the function $f$ has at least one positive Levi eigenvalue along $N$. Apply lemma 9.50.1 there with $q=n$ and $(n-1)\varepsilon <1$ to give its Levi form positive trace. Choose a smooth cutoff on $X$ equal to one on a smaller neighborhood $V$ of $Y$ and supported where this metric is defined on $N$. A convex combination of this inverse metric and an arbitrary smooth inverse metric on $N$ is positive definite and smooth. On $V\cap N$ it is the constructed inverse metric, so $L_\omega f>0$ there.

Apply corollary 9.52.7 on each $Z_ j$, with analytic set $Y\cap Z_ j$ and neighborhood $V\cap Z_ j$. The analytic set is nowhere dense in this irreducible component and contains its singular locus, and its complement is $N_ j$. Apply lemma 9.52.1 to consecutive open sets to obtain $v_{j,\nu }$, supported in $\overline{U_{j,\nu }}\cup \overline{U_{j,\nu +1}}$ and with all negative Laplacian in a compact subset of $U_{j,\nu +1}$. Each compactly supported smooth regular-locus bump extends by zero to a smooth function on $X$: its support is compact and avoids $Y$, and near it $X$ is the smooth manifold $N_ j$. The supports form a locally finite family in $X$. Within $Z_ j$ this is the properness argument in the preceding corollary; a compact set in $X$ meets only finitely many of its locally finite irreducible components.

On each $N_ j$ repeat the coefficient construction of theorem 9.52.8, using the compact targets $C_{j,\nu }$ outside $V$ and the preceding compact negative locus $S_{j,\nu -1}$. Add all these nonnegative bumps to $f$. Their sum $\psi $ is smooth on $X$, exhaustive and unchanged near each point of $Y$, since their supports are locally finite and disjoint from $Y$. On $N$ the last-negative-summand argument gives $L_\omega \psi >0$: outside $V$ use its covered compact target; inside $V$ the initial trace is positive unless a negative summand occurs, whose last occurrence is corrected at the next step. Thus $\psi $ is strongly $n$-convex both near $Y$ and on $N$, proving item1. This also completes the induction.

For item2 let $B$ be the union of $Y$ and all the noncompact $n$-dimensional components of $X$. This is closed analytic by local finiteness; it has no compact irreducible component of dimension $n$. Item1, including its lower-dimensional case, makes $B$ strongly $n$-complete. The preceding proper extension lemma gives an exhaustion strongly $n$-convex on an open neighborhood $O$ of $B$. The closed complement $X\setminus O$ is contained in the finite union of compact top components, so is compact. This proves item2 without the false assertion that $B$ must have dimension less than $n$. $\square$

Lemma 9.52.11.90D581391F17 If $F$ is closed in a locally compact Hausdorff space and $T$ is a compact connected component of $F$, there is a compact neighborhood $Q$ of $T$ with $\partial Q\cap F=\varnothing $.

Proof. Choose a compact neighborhood $D$ of $T$. In the compact Hausdorff space $H=F\cap D$, the component containing $T$ is still $T$: any connected enlargement in $H$ would enlarge it in $F$. We show that $T$ has a clopen neighborhood in $H$ avoiding $F\cap \partial D$. Let $J$ be the intersection of all clopen neighborhoods of $T$ in $H$. If $J$ were separated into two nonempty compact parts $J_1,J_2$, its connected subset $T$ would lie in one, say $J_1$. Choose disjoint open neighborhoods of $J_1,J_2$. Finitely many clopen neighborhoods of $T$ exclude the compact set outside their union. Their intersection is clopen and splits into two clopen parts inside those open neighborhoods. Its first part is a clopen neighborhood of $T$ excluding $J_2$, a contradiction to the definition of $J$. Thus $J$ is connected and equals $T$. Compactness now supplies a finite intersection $E$ of such clopen neighborhoods disjoint from $F\cap \partial D$.

The set $E$ is compact, lies in $\operatorname {int}D$ and is open in $F$, so $F\setminus E$ is closed in the ambient space. By local compactness and regularity, finitely many relatively compact open sets cover $E$ with closures disjoint from $F\setminus E$. Their union $B$ has compact closure, contains $E$, and satisfies $\overline B\cap F\subset E\subset B$. Therefore $\partial \overline B\cap F=\varnothing $, and $Q=\overline B$ is the required compact neighborhood of $T$. $\square$

Proposition 9.52.12.5366BBA720F6 Let $M$ be a connected noncompact complex manifold of dimension $n\geq 1$, and $U\subset M$ open. The following are equivalent:

  1. For every compact $K\subset U$ some smooth strongly $n$-convex exhaustion $\psi $ of $M$ has $K\subset \{ \psi <b\} \Subset U$.

  2. $M\setminus U$ has no compact connected component.

In particular this is precisely the top-degree Runge-complete condition.

Proof. If $T$ is a compact component of the closed complement, use lemma 9.52.11 to choose a compact neighborhood $Q$ of it with $\partial Q\subset U$. A strongly $n$-convex function has no local maximum, because a negative-semidefinite Levi form cannot have a positive eigenvalue. Its maximum on $Q$ must therefore occur on $\partial Q$. A sublevel containing that compact boundary contains $Q$, contradicting its containment in $U$.

Conversely, if $K=\varnothing $, use any strongly $n$-convex exhaustion and a level below its infimum. Suppose now that $K\subset U$ is nonempty and compact, and take a strictly $\omega $-subharmonic exhaustion $f$ supplied by theorem 9.52.8. Choose $b>\max _ K f$, with a positive margin, and put $P=(M\setminus U)\cap \{ f\leq b\} $. It is compact. Every component $T$ of the complement is closed and noncompact, so $f$ is unbounded on it. A point $x\in P\cap T$ and a point $y\in T$ with $f(y)>b+2$ lie in the same component of the open manifold $M\setminus K$: connectedness of $T$ is enough, and the containing open component is path connected. A thin connected relatively compact path neighborhood $D_ x\Subset M\setminus K$ thus connects $x$ to $W=\{ f>b+1\} $. Finitely many such $D_ x$ cover $P$. Their union $D$ has finitely many components and compact closure disjoint from $K$. Every component of $D$ meets $W$.

Each component of $W$ has noncompact closure. Otherwise $f$ would attain a maximum greater than $b+1$ in its interior, since its boundary has value $b+1$, contradicting strict subharmonicity. Thus each component of $W$ meeting $D$ contains a point outside $\overline D$. The whole-open sink lemma gives $v\geq 0$, positive on $P$, with $L_\omega v\geq 0$ off a compact $S\subset W\setminus \overline D$. It vanishes on a neighborhood of $K$, since both $\overline D$ and $\overline W$ avoid $K$. Choose $C$ so that $f+Cv>b+1$ on $P$, a strict margin. Choose a smooth convex increasing $\chi \geq 0$, zero for $t\leq b+1/2$, whose derivative for $t\geq b+1$ is large enough that $\chi '(f)L_\omega f$ dominates $C|L_\omega v|$ on the compact $S$. Such a function is an integral of a smooth nondecreasing step times a sufficiently large constant; $\min _ S L_\omega f>0$ when $S$ is nonempty. If $S=\varnothing $, take $\chi =0$. The chain rule gives

\[ \psi =f+Cv+\chi (f),\qquad L_\omega \psi >0. \]

It is exhaustive since $\psi \geq f$, and $\psi =f$ on $K$. On the complement of $U$, points with $f\leq b$ belong to $P$ and have $\psi >b+1$, and other points have $\psi \geq f>b$. Hence $\{ \psi \leq b\} \subset U$. This is compact, so $K\subset \{ \psi <b\} \Subset U$ as claimed. If $P$ is empty the same conclusion holds with $v=\chi =0$. $\square$

Corollary 9.52.13.0AD84DC1EF06 Let $\mathcal F$ be coherent on a countable-basis complex space $X$, possibly nonreduced, with $\dim X\leq n$ for an integer $n\geq 0$. Then $H^ k(X,\mathcal F)=0$ for $k\geq n+1$. If $X$ has no compact irreducible component of dimension $n$, then $H^ n(X,\mathcal F)=0$. If it has only finitely many such compact components, then $H^ n(X,\mathcal F)$ is Hausdorff and finite dimensional.

Proof. For $n\geq 1$ apply the complete vanishing and compact-exceptional finiteness theorem 9.51.13 to theorem 9.52.10, with $q=n+1$ or $q=n$. If $\dim X<n$, its strongly $(\dim X+1)$-complete exhaustion already gives the degree-$n$ vanishing. For $n=0$ the first assertion uses strong1-completeness. The second says $X$ is empty. In the last, $X$ is a finite set and hence compact; apply theorem 9.51.10 also in degree zero. This retains the full nonreduced coherent module, not just its values on the reduction. $\square$

Corollary 9.52.14.DBA14B44B8CF Let $\mathcal F$ be coherent on a connected noncompact complex $n$-manifold $M$, where $n\geq 1$, and let $U\subset M$ be open. If $M\setminus U$ has no compact connected component, restriction

\[ H^{n-1}(M,\mathcal F)\longrightarrow H^{n-1}(U,\mathcal F) \]

has dense image for the intrinsic quotient cohomology topologies.

Proof. The manifold is strongly $n$-complete by theorem 9.52.8. The preceding Runge-complete proposition gives, for every compact subset of $U$, a globally strongly $n$-convex exhaustion with the required relatively compact sublevel. This satisfies all hypotheses of the degree $q-1$ density assertion in theorem 9.51.13, for $q=n$. Its topology is exactly the intrinsic cohomology topology constructed there, including degree zero when $n=1$. $\square$
