25.10 Singular spaces, traces, and locally conformally Kähler geometryF14EBE94C297

F14EBE94C297

The manifold theory extends in two directions which require different tools: allowing the underlying complex space to be singular, and allowing the metric to be only locally conformal to a Kähler metric. The chapter on complex spaces and modifications supplies the analytic foundations for the first direction. The chapter on Hopf and locally conformally Kähler geometry develops the second through concrete geometric models. Their common use of local potentials should not obscure their different global obstructions.

On a complex space, plurisubharmonicity can be tested through local embeddings and holomorphic discs. The theorem of Fornæss–Narasimhan is a central reference for the disc characterization. Extension and proper-image results make it possible to compare intrinsic functions with ambient analytic data. Resolutions and principalization then replace singular spaces or ideals by smooth models with controlled divisors. Pome treats Hironaka's resolution and principalization as external geometric inputs; the analytic estimates used after passing to a resolution remain separate arguments.

The local geometry of analytic sets, normalization and modifications also provides a common setting for extension and coherent-sheaf methods. Demailly's book joins these geometric constructions to Cartan's cohomology theorems, proper direct images and convexity estimates. Nilpotent structure requires the full quotient structure sheaf; the underlying zero set alone does not determine a complex space. Affine algebraicity also involves growth at infinity, whereas local analytic parametrization concerns a neighborhood of one point.

Bounded strictly plurisubharmonic exhaustions on smoothly bounded pseudoconvex domains are the subject of Diederich–Fornæss. Their exponent and boundary hypotheses accompany the exhaustion estimate. This work is distinct from the later smooth nonpluripolar-curve example. Passing to a smooth model still leaves the analytic extension and descent arguments to establish.

Projective models and Kähler modifications connect meromorphic maps to compact geometry. Demailly's author-hosted Analytic Methods in Algebraic Geometry is the specified reference for resolving a bimeromorphic map from a compact Kähler manifold by smooth-center blowups of its source. This elimination existence theorem remains an explicit external input; reconstructing the surrounding analytic and cohomological descent arguments does not provide a proof of elimination.

Unibranchness and related local hypotheses affect how potentials and measures behave across singularities. A result valid on a resolution does not automatically descend to every singular space, and a restriction to a subvariety may be identically minus infinity. The trace constructions in b-divisors and restriction address this problem by specifying how singularity data is transferred. These questions explain the continued interaction between modifications, multiplier ideals, and non-pluripolar products.

A locally conformally Kähler metric has a closed Lee form and becomes Kähler after a local conformal change. On a suitable covering space, deck transformations act by homotheties, so global potentials may carry an automorphy condition. Hopf manifolds offer concrete examples where this geometry can be studied without assuming a global Kähler form. The work of Ornea–Verbitsky and the related literature on Vaisman and locally conformally Kähler manifolds provides the geometric background for the final geometric chapter.

The Perron constructions used there impose both positivity and the relevant equivariance or boundary condition. Descending a function from the cover, selecting a normalization, and solving the local nonlinear equation are distinct steps. These examples bring the book back to the classical envelope method, but in a setting where topology and conformal geometry change the admissible potentials. They also show why the common organization of Pome follows mathematical subjects rather than treating each source manuscript as a separate theory.
