B2AAD3B5B63F

Proposition 3.9.1.964B6728E795 Let $D$ be a nonempty proper connected open subset of $\mathbb R^2$, and put $d(x)=\operatorname {dist}(x,\mathbb R^2\setminus D)$ for $x\in D$. Then $-d$ is subharmonic on $D$ if and only if $D$ is convex.

Proof. Suppose that $D$ is not convex. Choose $x,y\in D$ with $[x,y]\not\subset D$, and join them by a polygonal path in $D$ having the least possible number of segments. Such paths exist: the set of points reachable from $x$ by polygonal paths is both relatively open and relatively closed in the connected open set $D$. The least number is an integer at least two. If $a,b,c$ are its first three vertices, then $[a,b]\cup [b,c]\subset D$ but $[a,c]\not\subset D$, since otherwise the path could be shortened by one segment. The three vertices are not collinear, for otherwise their first two segments would already contain $[a,c]$.

After a rigid motion, let $[a,c]$ be horizontal and $b$ lie above it. Write $T$ for the closed triangle and $F=\mathbb R^2\setminus D$. The compact set $F\cap T$ is nonempty and avoids the two sides $[a,b],[b,c]$. Let $H$ be its greatest height and choose the rightmost point $p$ of its height-$H$ slice. If $H$ is above the base, $p$ is interior to $T$, so a sufficiently small ball around it lies in $T$. If $H$ is the base height, $F\cap T$ lies on the base and $p$ is interior to that side; on a sufficiently small ball the upper half lies in $T$. In either case, translating $p$ to the origin gives an $r>0$ such that

\[ 0\in F,\qquad F\cap B(0,r)\subset \{ (x_1,x_2):x_2\le 0\} , \qquad (t,0)\notin F\quad (0<t<r). \]

The last assertion follows from the choice of the rightmost point, with $r$ decreased so that the horizontal points in question stay in $T$.

Set $q=(0,r/4)$ and $A=B(q,r/8)\Subset D$. For $z\in A$, every point of $F\cap B(0,r)$ is at distance at least $z_2$ from $z$; every other point of $F$ is at distance at least $r-|z|>5r/8>z_2$. Therefore $d(z)\ge z_2$, while $d(q)=r/4$ because $0\in F$. If also $z_1>0$, equality is impossible: a nearest point exists, and equality would require that point to be $(z_1,0)$, which is absent from $F$. Thus $d(z)>z_2$ on the right half of $A$. Continuity gives

\[ \frac1{|A|}\int _ A d(z)\, dz >\frac1{|A|}\int _ A z_2\, dz=\frac r4=d(q). \]

This contradicts the supermean inequality for $d$ if $-d$ is subharmonic. Hence $D$ must be convex.

Conversely the distance to the boundary of an open convex set is concave: convex combinations of balls $B(x,s)\subset D$ and $B(y,t)\subset D$ contain the ball with the combined center and radius. Let $s\uparrow d(x)$ and $t\uparrow d(y)$ to obtain the concavity inequality. The negative of this function is convex, and hence subharmonic by convex mollification. $\square$

Proposition 3.9.2.59D258340230 Let $F$ be a nonempty proper closed subset of $\mathbb R^2$, and let $G\subset \mathbb R^2$ be a connected open set containing $F$. If $d(x)=\operatorname {dist}(x,F)$ is subharmonic on $G$, then $F$ is convex. Conversely the distance to a nonempty closed convex set is convex, and hence subharmonic, on the whole plane.

Proof. We first prove a regularity fact in $\mathbb R^ m$. On a ball $B\Subset G\setminus F$ there is a constant $C$ such that

\[ D^2d\le CI \]

in the sense of distributions. Indeed, for every $a\in F$ the Hessian of $x\mapsto |x-a|$ is at most $I/\operatorname {dist}(B,F)$ on $B$. Subtracting $C|x|^2/2$ makes each such function concave; their infimum $d-C|x|^2/2$ is concave as well. Concave mollifications give the distributional assertion. Since $d$ is subharmonic, $\Delta d\ge 0$. Mollify on a smaller ball. The eigenvalues of the smooth Hessian are at most $C$ and their sum is nonnegative, so each is at least $-(m-1)C$. Thus the full mollified Hessians are uniformly bounded.

The function $d$ is $1$-Lipschitz. Its mollifications and their gradients are uniformly bounded, and the Hessian bound makes those gradients equi-Lipschitz. Compactness on smaller balls gives a uniformly convergent subsequence of gradients. The segment integral identity identifies its limit with $\nabla d$, and the Lipschitz bound passes to the limit. Consequently $d\in C^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(G\setminus F)$, and its distributional Hessian is a locally bounded symmetric matrix $H$. This also follows from the canonical bounded-Hessian regularity theorem.

Every $x\in G\setminus F$ has a nearest point $a\in F$, since a minimizing sequence stays bounded and $F$ is closed. The smooth function $|\, \cdot -a|$ dominates $d$ with equality at $x$. Differentiability therefore gives

\[ \nabla d(x)=\frac{x-a}{|x-a|},\qquad |\nabla d(x)|=1. \]

In particular the nearest point is unique wherever $d$ is differentiable. Differentiating $|\nabla d|^2=1$ weakly gives

\[ H\nabla d=0\quad \text{almost everywhere on }G\setminus F. \]

For clarity this weak product rule needs no additional differentiability theorem: mollify the locally Lipschitz vector $\nabla d$, whose weak derivatives are bounded. The vectors converge uniformly on compact sets, the derivatives converge distributionally with common bounds, and the ordinary product rule passes to the limit against test functions.

Now take $m=2$. The symmetric matrix $H$ has a zero eigenvalue, because $\nabla d$ is a unit vector, and its other eigenvalue is $\operatorname {tr}H=\Delta d\ge 0$. Hence $H\ge 0$ almost everywhere. Mollification on each interior ball and uniform convergence show that $d$ is locally convex on $G\setminus F$.

The set $F$ is locally convex, including at its boundary. Fix $p\in F$ and a ball $B(p,r)\subset G$. For $x,y\in F\cap B(p,r)$, restrict $d$ to their segment. If it were positive somewhere, a component interval of its positive set would have two zero endpoints. It is locally convex in that interval, hence convex there by the one-dimensional slope inequalities. Continuity at its endpoints would force it to be at most their zero chord, a contradiction. Thus the whole segment lies in $F\cap B(p,r)$.

Define the nearest-point map $P:G\to F$, with $P(x)=x$ on $F$. Off $F$ the preceding formula gives $P(x)=x-d(x)\nabla d(x)$, so $P$ is continuous there. At $p\in F$,

\[ |P(x)-p|\le |P(x)-x|+|x-p|\le 2|x-p|, \]

so it is continuous on all of $G$. Since $P$ fixes $F$, its image is exactly $F$. Connectedness of $G$ therefore makes $F$ connected. The closed connected local-convexity proposition now makes $F$ convex.

Conversely let $F$ be closed and convex. Choose nearest points $a,b$ for $x,y$. For $0\le t\le 1$ the point $(1-t)a+tb$ lies in $F$, and the triangle inequality gives

\[ d((1-t)x+ty)\le (1-t)|x-a|+t|y-b| =(1-t)d(x)+td(y). \]

Thus $d$ is convex. Convex mollifications have nonnegative Laplacian, and their locally uniform limit is subharmonic. $\square$

Proposition 3.9.3.BBEB233BF8A4 Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb R^2$ be nonempty, open and proper, and put $F=\mathbb R^2\setminus \Omega $ and $d(x)=\operatorname {dist}(x,F)$ on the whole plane. If there is a connected open neighborhood $U$ of $\partial \Omega $ on which $d$ is subharmonic, then $F$ is convex. Without connectedness of that neighborhood the conclusion fails, even for a connected $\Omega $ with smooth boundary.

Proof. The open set $G=U\cup \operatorname {int}F$ contains $F$, and $d$ is subharmonic there by locality: it is zero on $\operatorname {int}F$. It is connected. In fact every component $V$ of $\operatorname {int}F$ has nonempty boundary, since $F$ is proper, and $\partial V\subset \partial F\subset U$. Thus $V$ meets $U$. The preceding proposition applies to $G$.

For the counterexample take two disjoint closed unit discs with centers $a_1=(-3,0)$ and $a_2=(3,0)$, and let $F$ be their union. The complement $\Omega $ is connected: any polygonal path can be modified around the two disjoint discs by circular detours of radius slightly greater than $1$. Its boundary is the union of two smooth circles, while $F$ is not convex, since it contains the centers and not their midpoint. On the disjoint open collars

\[ U_ j=\{ x:1/2<|x-a_ j|<3/2\} ,\qquad U=U_1\cup U_2, \]

the global distance to $F$ is $d(x)=\max \{ |x-a_ j|-1,0\} $. Each function is convex and subharmonic on the whole plane, being the maximum of the convex functions $|x-a_ j|-1$ and $0$. Hence $d$ is subharmonic on the open neighborhood $U$ of $\partial \Omega $. This proves the stated failure. $\square$
