9.20 Complex geodesics on bounded convex domains7F25990CD800

7F25990CD800

Write $dm=d\theta /(2\pi )$ on $\mathbb T$, and use the complex bilinear pairing $z\cdot w=\sum z_ jw_ j$. All distances have the normalization $q(0,t)=\operatorname {atanh}t$.

Proposition 9.20.1.659A580EE96C Let $A$ be the disc algebra. If a finite complex measure $\mu $ on $\mathbb T$ annihilates $A$, then $d\mu =H^*dm$, where $H$ is holomorphic, $H(0)=0$, and $\sup _{r<1}\int |H(r\zeta )|dm<\infty $. For every holomorphic function with this last bound, the radial functions converge in $L^1$ to their almost everywhere boundary values. A nonzero such function has nonzero boundary values almost everywhere.

Proof. We give the analytic boundary argument, including the absolute continuity assertion. The positive Fejer kernels are approximate identities, so trigonometric polynomials are dense in $L^2(\mathbb T)$; orthogonality and this density give Parseval on circles. For $H^2$, write the Taylor series. Bounded square means are equivalent to square summability of the coefficients. Their $L^2$ Fourier sum $g^*$ has only nonnegative frequencies; $g(r\zeta )$ is its Poisson integral. The Poisson kernels are approximate identities, so convergence holds in $L^2$ and at every Lebesgue point. The latter assertion follows directly by splitting the integral into dyadic arcs about that point, using $P_ r(\theta )\leq C(1-r)/((1-r)^2+\theta ^2)$.

Now let $F\in H^1$, meaning that its integral means of absolute value are bounded. Remove a possible zero at zero. Jensen's formula follows by factoring the finitely many zeros inside a circle, applying the harmonic mean identity to the zero-free factor, and using $\int \log |r\zeta -a|dm=\log \max (r,|a|)$ for each zero. It gives the Blaschke condition for its remaining zeros $a_ j$: $\sum _ j-\log |a_ j|<\infty $. Indeed the means of $\log |F|$ are bounded above by the logarithm of its bounded absolute means. The factors $b_ a(z)=(|a|/a)(a-z)/(1-\bar a z)$ satisfy $|1-b_ a(z)|\leq C_ K(1-|a|)$ on each compact subdisc. Thus their product $B$ converges there, has precisely the prescribed zeros, and has modulus at most one. Moreover $|B^*|=1$ almost everywhere: $B\in H^2$, and Jensen's formula for the factors gives

\[ \int -\log |B(r\zeta )|dm =\sum _ j-\log \max (r,|a_ j|)\longrightarrow 0. \]

Dominated convergence for the sum and Fatou's lemma prove the claim. An origin factor contributes only $-k\log r$ and causes no change.

For each finite subproduct $B_ N$, $F/B_ N$ is holomorphic and has $H^1$ norm at most that of $F$: let the circle radius tend to one, where $|B_ N|$ tends uniformly to one, and use monotonicity of absolute means. Compact convergence and Fatou give the same bound for $G=F/B$. This function is zero free. Choose a holomorphic square root $g$ of $G$ on the disc. Both $g$ and $h=Bg$ lie in $H^2$, and $F=gh$. Their $L^2$ boundary convergence and Cauchy–Schwarz give $F(r\zeta )\to g^*h^*$ in $L^1$ and almost everywhere. In particular the $H^1$ norm equals the boundary $L^1$ norm, and the Cauchy and Poisson formulas hold with these boundary values.

For a nonzero $F$, its boundary values cannot vanish on a set of positive measure. After removing an origin zero, Jensen gives $\int \log |F(r\zeta )|dm\geq \log |F(0)|$, whereas the positive parts of these logarithms have uniformly bounded integrals. Fatou applied to their negative parts would contradict this inequality if the almost everywhere boundary function vanished on such a set.

Finally, annihilation of $1,\zeta ,\zeta ^2,\ldots $ says that the Poisson integral of $\mu $ has only strictly positive Fourier frequencies. It is therefore a holomorphic $H$ with $H(0)=0$. Convolution with $P_ r$ bounds its absolute means by $\| \mu \| $. The $H^1$ conclusion just proved gives strong $L^1$ convergence to $H^*$. Poisson convolution also converges weakly to $\mu $ as a measure, so $\mu =H^*dm$. $\square$

Proposition 9.20.2.415609592B60 Let $D$ be bounded and convex, and let $f:\Delta \to D$ be an extremal disc for two distinct points. Normalize its nodes to $0,t$, where $0<t<1$. There is a nonzero $H\in H^1(\Delta ,\mathbb C^ n)$ such that

\[ h(\zeta )=\frac{H(\zeta )}{\zeta -t},\qquad \operatorname {Re}(z-f^*(\zeta ))\cdot h^*(\zeta )\leq 0 \quad (z\in \overline D) \]

almost everywhere on $\mathbb T$. The boundary covector $h^*$ is nonzero almost everywhere. This disc is extremal for every pair of its nodes; in particular $\delta _ D(f(\lambda ),f(\eta ))=q(\lambda ,\eta )$.

Proof. Translate the coordinates so that $0\in D$. Let $p$ be its real Minkowski functional; it is sublinear and Lipschitz, and bounded above and below by positive multiples of the Euclidean norm. On $C(\mathbb T,\mathbb C^ n)$ put $P(u)=\max p(u)$, and let $\mathcal L$ consist of the disc-algebra maps with the same values as $f$ at $0,t$. We claim $\inf _{\mathcal L}P=1$. For the upper bound, $f(r\zeta )$ has $P\leq 1$; add $\zeta (f(t)-f(rt))/t$ to restore the second value. The correction tends uniformly to zero. For the lower bound, suppose $g\in \mathcal L$ has $P(g)<1$. Its dilations $g(r\zeta )$ with the same affine correction converge uniformly to $g$, extend past the closed disc, and still have image compactly contained in $D$. A dilation of one of these maps by a factor slightly larger than one retains its image in $D$ and attains the second target at a node smaller than $t$. This contradicts extremality. This argument uses the Lipschitz gauge $p$, without identifying it with a Euclidean norm.

Put $d(\zeta )=\zeta (\zeta -t)$ and $Y=dA^ n$; then $\mathcal L=f_0+Y$ for any interpolation polynomial $f_0$. Define $\ell (sf_0+y)=s$ on the real space $\mathbb R f_0+Y$. If $s>0$, the preceding infimum gives $P(sf_0+y)\geq s$; if $s\leq 0$, the inequality follows from $P\geq 0$. Real Hahn–Banach extends $\ell $ with $\ell \leq P$. The complex functional $L(u)=\ell (u)-i\ell (iu)$ is bounded, annihilates $Y$, has $\operatorname {Re}L(f_0)=1$, and satisfies $\operatorname {Re}L(u)\leq P(u)$. Represent its coordinates by finite complex measures $\mu _ j$ on the circle. To reduce this representation to the canonical positive-functional representation, for a bounded real functional $l$ set $l^+(u)=\sup _{0\leq v\leq u}l(v)$ for $u\geq 0$. This is additive on the positive cone: split any $0\leq v\leq u_1+u_2$ as $\min (v,u_1)+(v-\min (v,u_1))$, and use the reverse inequality by adding two admissible functions. Thus $l^+$ extends positively, as does $l^+-l$, and each is represented by a finite positive measure. For a positive functional on $C(\mathbb T)$, apply the canonical representation on $\mathbb R^2$ to restriction of compactly supported functions; the resulting measure is supported on the circle. Apply this to real and imaginary coordinate functionals. Since $d\mu _ j$ annihilates $A$, Proposition 9.20.1 gives $d\mu _ j=\zeta H_ j^*dm$, with $H_ j\in H^1$. Consequently $d\mu =h^*dm$, where $h=H/(\zeta -t)$.

For a covector $w$ put $p^*(w)=\max _{z\in \overline D}\operatorname {Re}z\cdot w$. The dual bound implies

\[ \int p^*(h^*)dm=1. \]

Here the left side is precisely $\sup _{P(u)\leq 1}\operatorname {Re}\int u\cdot h^*dm$: the upper bound is pointwise; for the lower bound choose maximizers from a countable dense subset of $\overline D$, approximate the resulting finite measurable partition by a continuous partition of unity in $L^1(|h^*|dm)$, and take its convex combination of those points. The reverse inequality $\geq 1$ also follows by applying the functional to an interpolation sequence with $P\downarrow 1$.

The bounded holomorphic map $f$ has radial boundary values by the $H^2$ argument. The corrected dilations used above and dominated convergence against $h^*\in L^1$ give $\operatorname {Re}\int f^*\cdot h^*dm=1$. Since $f^*\in \overline D$ almost everywhere, equality of the integrals forces $\operatorname {Re}f^*\cdot h^*=p^*(h^*)$ almost everywhere. One component of $H$ is nonzero, so the boundary nonvanishing part of Proposition 9.20.1 applies. Thus $f^*\in \partial D$ and $h^*$ is a nonzero supporting covector almost everywhere.

To change the nodes to distinct $\lambda ,\eta \in \Delta $, multiply $h$ by the meromorphic function whose circle values are

\[ \phi (\zeta )= \frac{|\zeta |^2|\zeta -t|^2}{|\zeta -\lambda |^2|\zeta -\eta |^2}>0. \]

Indeed its holomorphic expression is

\[ \phi h= \frac{\zeta H(\zeta )(1-t\zeta )}{(\zeta -\lambda )(\zeta -\eta ) (1-\bar\lambda \zeta )(1-\bar\eta \zeta )}. \]

It annihilates all disc-algebra variations vanishing at the two new nodes, by Cauchy's formula. The same holds for bounded holomorphic variations: dilate them and correct their two values by interpolation polynomials, then pass to the integral by bounded convergence. The supporting covector remains nonzero almost everywhere. If a compact-image map $g:\Delta \to D$ interpolated $f$ at those nodes, then for some $\varepsilon >0$ it would satisfy

\[ \operatorname {Re}\int g^*\cdot \phi h^*dm \leq (1-\varepsilon )\int p^*(\phi h^*)dm <\operatorname {Re}\int f^*\cdot \phi h^*dm, \]

contradicting annihilation of $f-g$. If a disc achieved the new pair at a strictly smaller disc distance, automorphisms would normalize its nodes to $0,s$, and the new nodes to $0,u$, with $s<u$. Precomposition by $(s/u)\zeta $, followed by the latter automorphism, would give exactly such a compact-image interpolant. This proves the asserted extremality for every pair, and also injectivity of $f$. $\square$

Proposition 9.20.3.5DE7AB27F999 On a bounded strongly real convex domain with $C^2$ boundary, an extremal disc $f$ extends to a $C^{1/2}$ map on $\overline\Delta $, with $f(\mathbb T)\subset \partial D$.

Proof. Compactness and positive real boundary curvature give a fixed interior tangent-ball radius $r_0>0$, a fixed exterior tangent-ball radius $R_0$, and a collar in which the nearest boundary point is unique. For completeness, in local boundary coordinates the real Hessian restricted to the tangent space is bounded above and below by positive constants. Taylor's formula gives both tangent-ball inequalities near the contact point; away from it strict convexity and compactness give a uniform strict supporting-plane gap. Decreasing $r_0$ and increasing $R_0$ extend the inequalities to the entire boundary. The interior centers form a compact subset of $D$.

Put $a=f(0)$, and $d(z)=\operatorname {dist}(z,\partial D)$. For $z=\xi -d(z)\nu (\xi )$ in the collar, the interior ball centered at $c=\xi -r_0\nu (\xi )$ gives, using the metric already proved in Theorem 9.18.1,

\[ \delta _ D(a,z)\leq C+ \operatorname {atanh}\frac{r_0-d(z)}{r_0} \leq C'+\tfrac 12\log \frac1{d(z)}. \]

Enlarging $C'$ handles the compact part outside the collar. The preceding proposition gives $\delta _ D(a,f(\zeta ))=\operatorname {atanh}|\zeta |$, so $d(f(\zeta ))\leq C''(1-|\zeta |)$.

For the exterior ball tangent at the nearest point, the elementary ball Schwarz estimate gives

\[ |f'(\zeta )|(1-|\zeta |^2) \leq \sqrt{R_0^2-|f(\zeta )-c|^2} \leq \sqrt{2R_0d(f(\zeta ))}. \]

To check the first inequality, scale to the unit ball and rotate the image point to $(s,0,\ldots ,0)$. Its automorphism

\[ w\longmapsto \left( \frac{s-w_1}{1-sw_1}, \frac{-\sqrt{1-s^2}\, w'}{1-sw_1}\right) \]

sends that point to zero and has differential of length at least $|v|/\sqrt{1-s^2}$. Apply the vector Schwarz lemma after a disc automorphism sends the chosen source point to zero. Thus $|f'(\zeta )|\leq C(1-|\zeta |)^{-1/2}$. Integrating along radii gives boundary limits. For two points at distance $s<1/2$, move each radially to radius at most $1-s$ and join the moved points within that smaller disc. The radial integrals and the joining integral are all at most $C\sqrt{s}$. This proves the claimed closed-disc estimate. The almost everywhere boundary containment proved above and continuity give containment everywhere. $\square$

Proposition 9.20.4.E378BBBF406D Under the hypotheses of Proposition 9.20.3, the disc has a holomorphic continuous lift $\ell $ satisfying

\[ \ell (\zeta )=\zeta p(\zeta )\overline{\nu (f(\zeta ))}, \qquad \zeta \in \mathbb T, \]

where $p$ is continuous and strictly positive. In fact $p$ and $\ell $ are $C^{1/2}$ on the circle and the closed disc, respectively.

Proof. First recall three boundary facts, with their relevant proofs. The circular Hilbert transform is bounded on $C^\alpha $ for $0<\alpha <1$. Subtract the value at the singular point in its cotangent-kernel integral; this bounds the integral by $C\int _0^\pi t^{\alpha -1}dt$. For differences at points distance $s$, split at $2s$ and use cancellation on the short arcs and the kernel derivative bound on the remainder. The resulting bound is

\[ C\left(\int _0^{2s}t^{\alpha -1}dt +s\int _{2s}^\pi t^{\alpha -2}dt\right)\leq C_\alpha s^\alpha . \]

The Poisson extension has the same $C^\alpha $ bound on the closed disc, by the corresponding kernel bounds. Consequently a real $C^\alpha $ function on the circle is the imaginary part of a holomorphic closed-disc $C^\alpha $ function.

Second, an $H^1$ function whose boundary values are real on an open arc extends holomorphically across that arc by reflection. Define the exterior function by conjugation in the circle. The interior and exterior traces converge in $L^1$ and agree on the arc. Integration by parts on circles tending to the boundary therefore shows that the glued locally integrable function has distributional $\bar\partial =0$. The distributional holomorphic-removal theorem gives the extension. This is an $H^1$ reflection statement; an almost everywhere real boundary condition on an arbitrary analytic function alone would not suffice.

Third, if $F\in H^1$ and $|F^*|/|\zeta -\xi |^ k\in L^1$ for $\xi \in \mathbb T$, then $F/(\zeta -\xi )^ k\in H^1$. Indeed the $H^1$ norm of $F/(1-r\bar\xi \zeta )^ k$ equals its boundary norm, which is uniformly bounded for $r\geq 1/2$ by $2^{k/2}\int |F^*|/|\zeta -\xi |^ k dm$, because

\[ |1-r\bar\xi \zeta |^2=(1-r)^2+r|\zeta -\xi |^2 \quad (\zeta \in \mathbb T). \]

Let $r\uparrow 1$ on compact subdiscs and use Fatou on circles.

For the dual in Proposition 9.20.2, put $\ell =(1-t\zeta )H$. On the circle $\ell /\zeta =|\zeta -t|^2h^*$, so $\ell =\zeta p\overline{\nu \circ f}$ almost everywhere, with $p>0$ almost everywhere and $p\in L^1$. Near any circle point, rotate the target coordinates so that $\nu _1\circ f\neq 0$. The function $q(\zeta )=\zeta \overline{\nu _1(f(\zeta ))}$ has a real $C^{1/2}$ argument on a sufficiently short arc. Extend that argument to a real $C^{1/2}$ function on the circle, and choose a holomorphic $C^{1/2}$ function $A$ with that imaginary part. Then $g=\ell _1e^{-A}\in H^1$ has nonnegative real boundary values on the arc. Reflection makes $g$ analytic across the arc, and

\[ p=g\, e^{\operatorname {Re}A}/|\nu _1\circ f| \]

gives a nonnegative $C^{1/2}$ representative there. Overlaps agree almost everywhere and hence everywhere. Thus $p$ is globally $C^{1/2}$, and the boundary relation gives the same regularity for $\ell $. Its Poisson representation supplies that regularity on the closed disc.

It remains essential to rule out zeros of $p$. If $p(\xi )=0$, the nonnegative analytic boundary function $g$ just constructed has an even-order zero, of order at least two. It is not identically zero, since $p>0$ almost everywhere. Therefore $p(\zeta )=O(|\zeta -\xi |^2)$ near $\xi $. Each component of

\[ L(\zeta )=\frac{-\xi \zeta \ell (\zeta )}{(\zeta -\xi )^2} \]

belongs to $H^1$ by the third boundary fact, and $L(0)=0$. Its circle relation is $L=\zeta p'\overline{\nu \circ f}$, where $p'=p/|\zeta -\xi |^2\geq 0$ is integrable and positive almost everywhere. The scalar function

\[ Q(\zeta )=\frac{(f(0)-f(\zeta ))\cdot L(\zeta )}\zeta \]

is holomorphic $H^1$ and has $Q(0)=0$. An interior ball about $f(0)$ and the supporting-plane inequality give $\operatorname {Re}Q^*\leq -\varepsilon p'$ almost everywhere, for a fixed $\varepsilon >0$. Its boundary mean is strictly negative, contrary to $Q(0)=0$. Hence $p$ has no zeros. $\square$

Theorem 9.20.5.7264782B9A80 Let $D\subset \mathbb C^ n$ be bounded and convex, and $a\neq b$ in $D$. There exist holomorphic maps $f:\Delta \to D$ and $P:D\to \Delta $, and $0<t<1$, such that

\[ f(0)=a,\qquad f(t)=b,\qquad P\circ f=\operatorname {id}_\Delta . \]

Consequently

\[ c_ D(a,b)=\delta _ D(a,b)=k_ D(a,b)=\sigma _ D(a,b) =\operatorname {atanh}t,\qquad g_ D(b,a)=\log t. \]

Proof. For $C^2$ strongly real convex $D$, take the extremal disc supplied by Theorem 9.18.1. The preceding three propositions give its continuous positive stationary lift. Proposition 9.18.2 supplies $P$ and all the asserted equalities.

We spell out the smooth exhaustion for a general bounded convex $D$. Given a compact $K\subset D$, include $K$ in a compact convex neighborhood $L\subset D$. Supporting half-spaces and compactness give a bounded open convex polytope

\[ K\subset L\subset Q=\{ x:\ell _ j(x)<0,\ 1\leq j\leq N\} , \qquad \overline Q\subset D. \]

One direct construction separates $L$ from every point of $\partial D$, takes a finite subcover of the compact boundary, and adds a large bounding box. If the resulting polytope met the complement of $D$, a segment from a point of $L$ would meet $\partial D$ inside its closure, contradicting the strict separating inequalities; small inward offsets preserve $L$ and make its closure disjoint from $\partial D$. For small $\varepsilon >0$ and large $M$, the analytic function

\[ \rho (x)=M^{-1}\log \sum _{j=1}^ N e^{M\ell _ j(x)} +\varepsilon |x|^2 \]

is proper and has positive definite real Hessian. It is negative on $L$, by the uniform negative margin of the $\ell _ j$ there, and its negative sublevel lies in $Q$, since $M^{-1}\log \sum e^{M\ell _ j}\geq \max _ j\ell _ j$. The zero level is regular: a critical point of a strictly convex function is its unique minimum, whose value is negative. Thus the negative sublevel is bounded and strongly real convex with analytic boundary. Iterating the construction with compact exhaustions and the previous closure included in $K$ gives $D_ j\Subset D_{j+1}$ and $\bigcup _ jD_ j=D$.

Take the retract discs $f_ j,P_ j$ through $a,b$ in $D_ j$ and their nodes $t_ j$. A fixed compact chain of inner balls joining $a$ to $b$ lies in all sufficiently large $D_ j$. The local affine-disc bounds and the triangle inequality give $\operatorname {atanh}t_ j\leq C$, uniformly. Coordinate Schwarz bounds give $t_ j\geq c>0$. Pass to $t_ j\to t\in (0,1)$ and normal-family limits $f_ j\to f$ and $P_ j\to P$ on their respective domains, using a diagonal sequence for $P_ j$ on the exhaustion. The supporting-hyperplane maximum principle puts $f(\Delta )$ in $D$, because $f(0)=a\in D$. The maximum principle puts $P(D)$ in $\Delta $, because $P(a)=0$. Compact-uniform convergence and $P_ jf_ j=\operatorname {id}$ give $Pf=\operatorname {id}$.

Holomorphic contraction and the disc identities now bound all four distances below and above by $q(0,t)$. For the Green function, $\log |P|$ is an admissible negative plurisubharmonic function with a logarithmic pole at $a$, so $g_ D(b,a)\geq \log t$. Pullback to $f$ and the disc pole comparison give the reverse inequality. The case $a=b$ follows with distance zero and Green value $-\infty $. $\square$

Example 9.20.6.8E0727128F9C Smooth convex boundary alone does not make every complex geodesic continuous on the closed disc. Choose a smooth convex nondecreasing proper function $\chi :[0,\infty )\to [0,\infty )$ which is zero on $[0,1]$ and has $\chi '>0$ on $(1,\infty )$. For example take

\[ \chi (s)=\int _1^ s(s-u)e^{-1/(u-1)}du\quad (s>1), \qquad \chi (s)=0\quad (s\leq 1). \]

The bounded domain $D=\{ |z_1|^2+\chi (|z_2|^2)<1\} $ is convex with smooth boundary; its defining gradient is nonzero on that boundary. The map

\[ f(\zeta )=\left(\zeta , \tfrac 12\exp \left(-\frac{1+\zeta }{1-\zeta }\right)\right) \]

has left inverse $P(z)=z_1$ and hence is a complex geodesic. Its second component has no limit at $1$: along a radius it tends to zero, whereas the imaginary part of $(1+\zeta )/(1-\zeta )$ can diverge with bounded positive real part along interior paths. Taking that imaginary part through multiples of $2\pi $ or odd multiples of $\pi $ gives distinct nonzero limiting values.
