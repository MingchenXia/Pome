5.58 Radial positive currentsF07D34B4B294

F07D34B4B294

Throughout this section $n\geq 1$, $X=\mathbb C^ n\setminus \{ 0\} $, $t=\log |z|^2$, and

\[ \theta =d^ ct,\qquad \beta =dd^ ct,\qquad \gamma =dt\wedge \theta ,\qquad \beta _0=dd^ c|z|^2. \]

We use $d^ c=i(\bar\partial -\partial )/(4\pi )$, so $dd^ c=i\partial \bar\partial /(2\pi )$. In polar coordinates $X=\mathbb R\times S^{2n-1}$, the forms $\theta ,\beta $ are pulled back from the sphere, and

9EB38C83DB56 5.58.1
\begin{equation} d\theta =\beta ,\quad \beta ^ n=0,\quad \beta _0=e^ t(\beta +\gamma ),\quad \beta _0^ n=n e^{nt}\, dt\wedge \sigma , \qquad \sigma =\theta \wedge \beta ^{n-1}. \end{equation}

The sphere has its boundary orientation. The positive volume form $\sigma $ has integral one. Indeed, $\beta _0^ n=(n!/\pi ^ n)dV$ and the Euclidean ball of radius $R$ has volume $\pi ^ nR^{2n}/n!$; integration of the last identity over an annulus gives $\int \sigma =1$. Thus $\sigma $ is also the invariant sphere probability of invariant probability on unitary homogeneous spaces.

For a Radon measure $\nu $ on $\mathbb R$, the notation $\nu (dt)\wedge \theta \wedge \beta ^{p-1}$ means the current obtained by taking the product of the degree-one measure current $\nu (dt)$ on the first factor with that smooth form on the second factor. For $\nu =v(t)dt$ it is $v(t)\gamma \wedge \beta ^{p-1}$. This notation never multiplies two singular measures.

Lemma 5.58.2.EE2F097FF26A Let $T$ be a distributional differential form on $X$ invariant under $\mathrm U(n)$. Put $h_ s(z)=e^{s/2}z$. Choose a nonnegative smooth compactly supported function $\rho $ on $\mathbb R$ of integral one. Then

\[ T_\varepsilon =\int _{\mathbb R}\varepsilon ^{-1} \rho (s/\varepsilon )h_ s^*T\, ds \]

is smooth on $X$ and converges weakly to $T$. It is invariant under $\mathrm U(n)$; closedness and positivity are preserved.

Proof. The integral is a weak integral: on any compact set all transformed test forms have support in one compact set. Pullback is the pullback of the entire differential form or current, including differential factors. It commutes with $d$ and preserves positivity here. Continuity of test-form pullback as $s\to 0$ proves weak convergence, with the normalizing factor $\varepsilon ^{-1}$.

Here is why smoothing in only one parameter suffices. The group $G=\mathbb R\times \mathrm U(n)$ acts transitively on $X$. The invariant probability on $\mathrm U(n)$ is a smooth density: the inner product $-\operatorname {Re}\operatorname {tr}(AB)$ on its skew-Hermitian tangent matrices gives a bi-invariant Riemannian metric, whose normalized volume equals that probability by its uniqueness. Unitary invariance allows us to insert integration over this density in the displayed formula.

The map $(g,x)\mapsto (x,gx)$ from $G\times X$ to $X\times X$ is a submersion. Its differential in $g$ spans the tangent of the orbit: the dilation supplies the real radial direction, and skew- Hermitian matrices applied to a unit vector supply every tangent direction of the sphere. In submersion coordinates, integration of a smooth density of compact support in the group variable is integration over some coordinate variables. A partition of unity therefore gives a smooth kernel in $(x,y)$, with compact $y$ support locally uniformly in $x$. The same assertion holds for forms, since the matrix factors of the smooth group action on the exterior bundles are smooth. Pairing this kernel in $y$ with the distribution $T$ proves smoothness in $x$. The group support is compact, so this argument applies on every compact subset of $X$. $\square$

Theorem 5.58.3.FB41A7CE1CEE Let $1\leq p<n$. The closed positive $\mathrm U(n)$-invariant currents of bidegree $(p,p)$ on $X$ are exactly

2A9B47FEBEC7 5.58.4
\begin{equation} T=u(t)\beta ^ p+(Du)(dt)\wedge \theta \wedge \beta ^{p-1}, \end{equation}

where $u$ is a nonnegative nondecreasing locally bounded function on $\mathbb R$ and $Du$ is its distributional derivative measure. There is a unique such $u$ when its right-continuous representative is chosen. Equivalently,

\[ T=(dd^ c\chi (t))^ p, \]

where $\chi $ is a finite convex nondecreasing function on $\mathbb R$. This potential is unique up to an additive constant. The product is the locally bounded Bedford–Taylor product on $X$.

Proof. First suppose $T$ is smooth. At a point on the last coordinate axis the stabilizer is $\mathrm U(n-1)$. Its diagonal phases annihilate the coefficients $dz_ I\wedge d\bar z_ J$ with unequal index sets; permutations of the first $n-1$ coordinates leave only two coefficients, according as the radial index occurs or does not. Consequently

\[ T=u(t)\beta ^ p+v(t)\gamma \wedge \beta ^{p-1}. \]

Both tensors are nonzero and independent for $1\leq p<n$. Testing a tangential complex $p$-plane and a plane containing the radial direction shows that positivity is equivalent to $u,v\geq 0$. These conditions are also sufficient, since both tensors are positive. As $d\gamma =-dt\wedge \beta $, closedness is exactly

\[ (u'-v)dt\wedge \beta ^ p=0, \]

and $dt\wedge \beta ^ p\ne 0$ in this range. Thus $v=u'$.

For a general $T$, apply lemma 5.58.2. On each compact polar interval the two smooth tensors above have smooth dual coefficient extractors. In particular distributional convergence of $T_\varepsilon $ extracts distributions $U,V$ on that interval from $u_\varepsilon (t),v_\varepsilon (t)$. One may construct the extractors by inverting their positive definite Gram matrix on the sphere and integrating there. Positivity makes $U,V$ positive Radon measures: alternatively their masses are bounded by a fixed multiple of the trace mass of $T_\varepsilon $ on the interval, as in coefficient measures and compactness of positive currents. Thus $DU=V$. These extractions agree on overlapping intervals.

An elementary one-dimensional distribution argument now identifies $U$. On an interval choose a cumulative function $g$ for $V$; Fubini applied to compactly supported tests gives $Dg=V$. Hence $D(U-g)=0$. A distribution with derivative zero is constant: every compactly supported smooth function of integral zero is the derivative of a compactly supported smooth function. It follows that $U=(g+c)dt$. The positivity of $U$ makes $u=g+c$ nonnegative almost everywhere, and $Du=V\geq 0$ makes it nondecreasing. Its right-continuous representative is finite and locally bounded; the interval descriptions agree. This proves the asserted formula and uniqueness, including its measure-valued second coefficient.

Put $w=u^{1/p}$ and choose an antiderivative $\chi $ of $w$. It is finite, convex, nondecreasing and locally Lipschitz. Smooth one-dimensional convolutions $\chi _ j$ are convex and nondecreasing and converge locally uniformly to $\chi $. For $w_ j=\chi _ j'$ the functions $w_ j$ are nonnegative and locally uniformly bounded, and $w_ j^ p\to u$ in $L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}$, by almost- everywhere convergence of convolutions at continuity points of $w$. The smooth radial Hessian has nonnegative tangential and radial eigenvalues, so $\chi _ j(t)$ is plurisubharmonic, and

DB4651A869BB 5.58.5
\begin{equation} (dd^ c\chi _ j(t))^ p =w_ j^ p\beta ^ p+D(w_ j^ p)(dt)\wedge \theta \wedge \beta ^{p-1}. \end{equation}

The right side converges distributionally to (5.58.4). Uniform convergence on compact annuli and uniform convergence of bounded Bedford–Taylor products identify the left limit with $(dd^ c\chi (t))^ p$; they also prove that $\chi (t)$ is plurisubharmonic. This proves existence and the converse. The coefficient $u$ fixes $\chi '=u^{1/p}$ almost everywhere, which proves uniqueness up to a constant. At a jump the measure $Du$ has mass $u(t+)-u(t-)$; no value of a product $p w^{p-1}Dw$ at that jump is being asserted. $\square$

Proposition 5.58.6.56D537355910 In bidegree $(0,0)$ the closed positive currents on $X$ are the nonnegative constants. In bidegree $(n,n)$ the positive invariant currents are exactly

7B165B21807E 5.58.7
\begin{equation} T=\nu (dt)\wedge \sigma \end{equation}

for arbitrary positive Radon measures $\nu $ on $\mathbb R$; closedness imposes no further condition. Such a current is $(dd^ c\chi (t))^ n$ for a finite convex nondecreasing $\chi $ on $\mathbb R$ if and only if

02850E385188 5.58.8
\begin{equation} \nu (( -\infty ,b])<\infty \quad \hbox{for one, equivalently every, }b\in \mathbb R. \end{equation}

When $n=1$, every $\nu $ nevertheless has a finite convex potential $\chi $ on $\mathbb R$ with $dd^ c\chi (t)=T$; this $\chi $ need not be nondecreasing.

Proof. A distribution with all first derivatives zero is locally constant (convolve in coordinate balls, then pass to distributions). The connectedness of $X$ gives the assertion in degree zero.

A positive top-degree current is a positive measure. Push it forward by $t$ to get $\nu $; compact polar intervals have compact inverse images, so this is Radon. Average any compactly supported continuous test over $\mathrm U(n)$. The uniqueness of its sphere probability shows that the averaged test at radius $t$ is its $\sigma $ integral. Invariance and Fubini now give (5.58.7). This proves both existence and uniqueness without any regularity assumption on $\nu $. Differentiating a top-degree current is zero.

For a convex nondecreasing $\chi $, let $w=\chi '\geq 0$ be its nondecreasing derivative representative. The same smooth approximation and Bedford–Taylor argument as in (5.58.5), with $\beta ^ n=0$, give

\[ (dd^ c\chi (t))^ n=D(w^ n)(dt)\wedge \sigma . \]

Since $w$ is finite on compact intervals, $D(w^ n)$ has a finite left tail. Conversely, under (5.58.8), choose any $c\geq 0$, set $w(t)=(c+\nu (( -\infty ,t]))^{1/n}$, and integrate $w$ to obtain $\chi $. The formula just proved gives its required top power. The free constant $c$ shows why the top-degree potential is not unique up to an additive constant.

For $n=1$, a signed cumulative function $w$ with $Dw=\nu $ always exists on the whole real line: use $w(t)=\nu ((0,t])$ for $t>0$, $w(t)=-\nu ((t,0])$ for $t<0$, with the value at zero adjusted to a one-sided representative. It is nondecreasing and locally bounded. Its integral is a finite convex $\chi $. Since $\beta =0$ when $n=1$, smoothing gives $dd^ c\chi (t)=\nu (dt)\wedge \theta $ even if $w$ takes negative values. For example $\nu =dt$ admits $\chi (t)=t^2/2$, whose derivative changes sign. $\square$

Corollary 5.58.9.4F0954956743 For $0\leq p<n$, a closed positive invariant current on $X$ is also invariant under positive dilations if and only if it is $c\beta ^ p$ for a constant $c\geq 0$. In top degree the dilation- invariant currents on $X$ are instead $c\, dt\wedge \sigma $.

Every middle-degree current in theorem 5.58.3 has a unique closed order-zero extension to $\mathbb C^ n$; it is positive and is its trivial extension. For a top-degree current such an extension exists exactly under (5.58.8), and all positive extensions are its trivial extension plus $a\delta _0$, $a\geq 0$. Thus global dilation-invariant positive top-degree currents are precisely $a\delta _0$. Degree-zero currents extend as the same constants.

Proof. If $T$ is dilation-invariant, its normalized dilation smoothing equals $T$, so it is smooth on $X$. For $p<n$, lemma 6.41.4 therefore gives $c\beta ^ p$ (and includes degree zero). Conversely these forms are invariant. In top degree, homogeneity is translation invariance of $\nu $ on $\mathbb R$. A locally finite translation-invariant positive measure is $c\, dt$: its mass on an interval depends only on its length, is additive, and is continuous as the length decreases to zero. Rational subdivision and this continuity give $c$ times the length, and intervals determine the measure.

For $1\leq p<n$ put $m=n-p>0$. Wedge the middle-degree formula with $\beta _0^ m$ to obtain the trace measure

\[ T\wedge \beta _0^ m =e^{mt}\bigl (m u(t)dt+(Du)(dt)\bigr )\wedge \sigma . \]

Since $u$ is bounded on each left half-line, the product rule for a BV function yields, for every finite $b$,

\[ \int _{t<b}T\wedge \beta _0^ m=e^{mb}u(b-)<\infty . \]

Indeed it is the integral of $D(e^{mt}u)$, whose limit at $-\infty $ is zero. The positive-current trace estimate gives locally finite coefficient masses at the origin. The origin is complete pluripolar, with potential $\log |z|$, so theorem 5.60.3 proves closedness and positivity of the trivial extension. The difference of two closed order-zero extensions is a normal current supported at a point, of real dimension $2(n-p)>0$; it vanishes by theorem 5.56.3.

In top degree local finiteness at the origin is exactly finite left-tail mass. The missing measure can only be an arbitrary nonnegative atom there; all these extensions are closed. The nonzero measure $c\, dt\wedge \sigma $ has infinite left-tail mass, so no such punctured dilation-invariant measure extends positively. An atom at zero is invariant, proving the global top-degree assertion. $\square$

The distinction of degrees is necessary. For example $dt\wedge \sigma =\beta _0^ n/(n|z|^{2n})$ is a nonzero smooth positive closed top-degree form on $X$ invariant under all positive dilations, whereas $\beta ^ n=0$. In degree zero $(dd^ c\chi )^0=1$ does not represent an arbitrary constant. All potential identities above are asserted on the punctured space, where the potentials are locally bounded; no unbounded product at the origin is implicit.
