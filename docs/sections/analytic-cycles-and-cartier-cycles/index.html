20.6 Analytic cycles and Cartier cyclesBBC701E1E172

20.6.1 Analytic cycle sheaves and Cartier cycles0F9AFEB5C512

Definition 20.6.2.BE0C86A8DD65 For $\Lambda =\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q$ or $\mathbb R$ and $q\geq 0$, a $q$-dimensional analytic $\Lambda $-cycle on a reduced complex space is a formal sum $\sum _ A c_ A A$, where the $A$ are distinct irreducible closed analytic subsets of pure dimension $q$, $c_ A\in \Lambda $, and the family with nonzero coefficient is locally finite. Its support is their union. It is effective when every coefficient is nonnegative. On a pure-dimensional space of dimension $n$, the cycles of dimension $n-1$ are called Weil divisors. In negative dimensions set the cycle module equal to zero.

Proposition 20.6.3.FD8966432506 Restrictions, with each restricted irreducible set decomposed into its analytic components carrying the same coefficient, make these cycles a sheaf of $\Lambda $-modules. Its stalk at $x$ is the free $\Lambda $-module on irreducible $q$-dimensional analytic germs at $x$. The coefficientwise maximum and minimum are sheaf operations and give its natural lattice order.

Proof. The local decomposition and global component theorem global irreducible analytic components give locally finite irreducible restrictions. Distinct irreducible global sets cannot acquire the same local branch: a common branch gives a regular open overlap, and the identity principle on the connected regular locus identifies the global sets. A locally finite cycle thus restricts unambiguously, and restrictions compose by uniqueness of analytic component decomposition. Equality can be checked locally, proving the separated sheaf axiom.

For gluing, suppose cycles agree on an open cover. Their supports glue to a closed analytic set $S$: around each point a single cover member describes the same support on all overlaps, so closedness and analytic equations hold locally. It is pure of dimension $q$ where nonempty. Its global irreducible components are locally finite. On the connected regular locus of each component, outside its intersections with other components, the local coefficients agree and are locally constant. They therefore have one constant value on that component; connectedness and density follow from global irreducible analytic components and removing proper analytic subsets. These constants give a global cycle with the prescribed restrictions. Germwise equality shows uniqueness.

Only finitely many local branches occur at a point, so a stalk element gives a finite formal combination of irreducible germs. Conversely finitely many such germs have representatives on one neighborhood, so every finite combination occurs. A zero germ forces all its coefficients to vanish by uniqueness of branch decomposition, proving the free-module assertion. For two cycles use the locally finite union of their component families and put missing coefficients equal to zero. Their coefficientwise maximum and minimum remain locally finite, and restrictions preserve these operations because every restricted branch retains its coefficient. This proves the stated lattice and its compatibility with the sheaf structure. $\square$

Proposition 20.6.4.AA96C35C8290 Let $X$ be reduced and pure of dimension $n$, with singular locus of dimension at most $n-2$. A holomorphic function $f$ nonzero on every irreducible component defines an effective Weil divisor $\operatorname {div}(f)$ by its generic vanishing orders on $X_{\rm reg}$. These orders are well defined and

\[ \operatorname {div}(fg)=\operatorname {div}(f)+\operatorname {div}(g). \]

Cycles locally given by integer combinations of these divisors form the subsheaf of Cartier cycles; locally each is $\operatorname {div}(g)-\operatorname {div}(h)$. On a complex manifold every integral Weil divisor is Cartier, and every effective integral Weil divisor is locally the divisor of one holomorphic function.

Proof. The zero set of $f$ is pure of dimension $n-1$ by purity of a nontrivial holomorphic zero set, and its components are locally finite. No such component lies in $X_{\rm sing}$. On the smooth locus of a zero component, away from the other zero components and from $X_{\rm sing}$, choose its local coordinate equation $t=0$. The factorial hypersurface result local equations and currents of irreducible hypersurfaces and its factorization give $f=t^ m v$ with $v$ a unit and $m\geq 1$. The integer $m$ is locally constant on this locus. It is connected and dense in the irreducible zero component, by the global component theorem and removal of proper analytic subsets. Thus it has one value, which defines the coefficient. At a regular point of a component in the union of the zero divisors of $f,g$, their powers of the same coordinate multiply, proving additivity of the orders.

A finite integer combination of principal holomorphic divisors is the difference of the divisors of the products with positive and negative exponents. Functions nonzero on every component remain so under those products. This proves the asserted local description and its sheaf compatibility. On a manifold take local prime equations for the finitely many hypersurface branches, as in local equations and currents of irreducible hypersurfaces. Their product with the prescribed nonnegative powers has the required effective divisor. Taking a quotient treats arbitrary integral coefficients. Dimension zero has no nonempty hypersurface divisors, so its assertion is immediate. $\square$

Example 20.6.5.0C462992AB3A On the three-dimensional quadric

\[ X=\{ x_1x_2+x_3x_4=0\} \subset \mathbb C^4, \qquad A=\{ x_1=x_3=0\} , \]

the effective Weil divisor $A$ is not a Cartier cycle at zero.

Proof. The rank-one matrix parametrization

\[ F:\mathbb C^4\longrightarrow X,\qquad (a,b,c,d)\longmapsto (ac,bd,ad,-bc) \]

is surjective. It is surjective as a germ at zero as well: every sufficiently small rank-one matrix factors as a column times a row with both factors small, by scaling the factors to equal norm. Thus the germ of $X$ is irreducible, since an analytic decomposition would pull back to one of the irreducible smooth germ $\mathbb C^4$. The quadric is pure of dimension three. Its nonzero points are regular, by its gradient, and its origin is singular: its lowest quadratic form has rank four, so cannot factor into two linear forms, and its reduced principal ideal has no linear term. Its tangent dimension there is four. In particular the singular set is the point zero.

Suppose $A$ were Cartier as a cycle. Its local description in the proposition would give a meromorphic function $f=g/h$ with $\operatorname {div}(f)=A$, where $g,h$ are holomorphic germs nonzero on $X$. Pullback by $F$ is defined: germwise surjectivity makes $h\circ F$ nonzero. Outside

\[ F^{-1}(0)=\{ a=b=0\} \cup \{ c=d=0\} , \]

the map is a submersion onto $X_{\rm reg}$. Indeed, for nonzero column $u=(a,b)$ and row $v=(c,d)$, the kernel of $(\dot u,\dot v)\mapsto \dot u v+u\dot v$ consists exactly of $(t u,-t v)$ and has dimension one. There the pullback of $A$ is the reduced hypersurface $a=0$. For example where $b\ne 0$ its normal coordinate is $a/b$, and where $a=0$ one has $b\ne 0$ off the displayed exceptional set. Pullback of divisors by this submersion therefore gives $\operatorname {div}(f\circ F)=[a=0]$ off that set. The exceptional set has codimension two, so it cannot support any additional hypersurface in a meromorphic divisor. The required smooth local factorization follows from the already proved Noetherianity of convergent power-series modules, local equations and currents of irreducible hypersurfaces: factorization into nonunits terminates by the ascending chain condition on principal ideals; each irreducible factor is associated to the prime equation of its hypersurface branch. Such factors are therefore prime. Applying this to the numerator and denominator of $f\circ F$ cancels all factors except one $a$, giving $f\circ F=a e$ for a holomorphic unit $e$ at zero.

For $t\in \mathbb C^*$ the transformation $(a,b,c,d)\mapsto (ta,tb,t^{-1}c,t^{-1}d)$ preserves $F$. For each fixed $t$ close to one, the equality of meromorphic germs $a e=t a\, e(ta,tb,t^{-1}c,t^{-1}d)$ permits cancellation of $a$. Evaluating at zero gives $e(0)=t e(0)$, impossible for $t\ne 1$ because $e$ is a unit. This proves non-Cartierness as a cycle, without confusing it with nonprincipality of the reduced ideal alone. $\square$
