2.12 Open maps, compact perturbations and finite cohomologyAB0CD215F15E

AB0CD215F15E

All locally convex spaces here are Hausdorff, over $\mathbb C$. A Fréchet space is complete and metrizable. A continuous linear map $h:E\to F$ is compact if $\overline{h(U)}$ is compact for some neighborhood $U$ of zero. This is compactness on one neighborhood, not merely compactness on bounded sets.

We use the dominated-linear-extension argument already proved in the proof of Lamari's positivity criterion. It extends a real linear functional dominated by a finite sublinear function on any real vector space; no geometric hypothesis enters that extension argument. Applied to continuous seminorms it implies that continuous linear functionals separate points of a Hausdorff locally convex space. For complex functionals, extend the real part and use $\ell (x)=a(x)-i a(ix)$. A finite-dimensional subspace $L$ is closed and has a continuous linear projection: on its Euclidean unit sphere finitely many continuous seminorms have a maximum bounded below by a positive number. This maximum controls every coordinate functional of $L$; dominated extension gives continuous coordinate functionals on the whole space and hence the projection. The same seminorm bound shows that $L$ has its usual finite-dimensional topology and is closed. These facts also show that a closed finite-codimensional subspace has a finite-dimensional continuous complement, by passing to its Hausdorff finite-dimensional quotient.

Proposition 2.12.1.71B16A3EF23E A quotient of a Fréchet space by a closed linear subspace is Fréchet. Every continuous surjective linear map between Fréchet spaces is open. A linear map between Fréchet spaces whose graph is closed is continuous.

Proof. Let $(p_ j)$ be increasing defining seminorms on $E$ and let $N\subset E$ be closed. The seminorms $\bar p_ j([x])=\inf _{n\in N}p_ j(x+n)$ define its Hausdorff quotient topology. Indeed their open balls are exactly the images of the corresponding open seminorm balls, and $\bar p_ j([x])=0$ for all $j$ implies $x\in \overline N=N$. For a Cauchy sequence in the quotient choose a subsequence whose successive differences have representatives $v_ j$ satisfying $p_ j(v_ j)<2^{-j}$. Starting from one representative and adding these $v_ j$ gives a Cauchy sequence in $E$, because for every fixed $r$ its tail has $p_ r$-size at most a geometric series. Completeness gives a quotient limit for the subsequence and then for the original Cauchy sequence. This proves the assertion.

Let $A:E\to F$ be continuous and surjective, and let $U$ be a zero neighborhood in $E$. Choose a closed convex balanced neighborhood $B\subset U$. Choose convex balanced neighborhoods $V_ j\subset 2^{-j-1}B$, $j\geq 0$, so small that $d_ E(v,0)<2^{-j}$ for $v\in V_ j$, using a complete translation-invariant defining metric. Since $F=\bigcup _{n\geq 1}A(nV_ j)$, the Baire property makes $\overline{A(V_ j)}$ a neighborhood of zero. Here Baire is the complete-metric nested-ball argument already supplied in proposition 2.10.3; convexity and balance move a nonempty interior to zero. Choose open neighborhoods $W_ j\subset \overline{A(V_ j)}$ with $d_ F(w,0)<2^{-j}$ for $w\in W_ j$.

For $y_0\in W_0$, successively choose $x_ j\in V_ j$ so that $y_{j+1}=y_ j-Ax_ j\in W_{j+1}$. Density in the indicated closure permits each choice. The series $\sum _ jx_ j$ is Cauchy for the complete metric, and every partial sum belongs to $B$ by convexity and the sum of the coefficients $2^{-j-1}$. Its limit $x$ belongs to $B\subset U$. The residuals tend to zero, so $Ax=y_0$. Thus $W_0\subset A(U)$, proving openness. Finally, a closed graph is a closed Fréchet subspace of $E\times F$; its projection to $E$ is a continuous linear bijection, whose inverse is continuous by openness. Compose with the other projection. $\square$

For a continuous linear $g:E\to F$, call $g$ a closed embedding if it is a topological isomorphism onto a closed subspace. Call it a quasi-monomorphism if $\ker g$ is finite dimensional and the induced map $E/\ker g\to F$ is a closed embedding. Call it a quasi-epimorphism if it is open onto a closed finite-codimensional image. A quasi-isomorphism has both properties.

Corollary 2.12.2.181D2DF97074 For continuous linear maps between Fréchet spaces, a closed embedding is the same as an injection with closed image, and a quasi-monomorphism is the same as a map with finite-dimensional kernel and closed image. A finite-codimensional image is automatically closed, and the map onto it is open. Thus quasi-epimorphism is equivalent to finite codimension of the image.

Proof. For closed image, apply the preceding proposition to $E/\ker g\to g(E)$. If the image is only known to have finite algebraic codimension, choose a finite-dimensional algebraic complement $S\subset F$. The map

\[ (E/\ker g)\oplus S\longrightarrow F,\qquad ([x],s)\longmapsto gx+s \]

is a continuous linear bijection of Fréchet spaces. It is a homeomorphism by the proposition; hence its image of the closed subspace $(E/\ker g)\oplus \{ 0\} $ is closed. Openness onto this image follows in the same way. This assertion concerns images of continuous Fréchet maps, not arbitrary algebraic subspaces of finite codimension. $\square$

Theorem 2.12.3.E27FE1F7DE85 Let $g,h:E\to F$ be continuous linear maps, with $h$ compact. If $g$ is a quasi-monomorphism, then $g+h$ is a quasi-monomorphism. This assertion holds for Hausdorff locally convex spaces. If $E,F$ are Fréchet and $g$ is a quasi-epimorphism, then $g+h$ is a quasi-epimorphism. Consequently compact perturbations preserve quasi-isomorphisms between Fréchet spaces.

Proof. For the first assertion remove $\ker g$ by its continuous finite-dimensional complement. On that complement identify $E$ with the closed subspace $g(E)\subset F$, so $g(x)=x$. Choose a convex balanced open $U\subset E$ with $K=\overline{h(U)}$ compact, and a convex balanced open $V\subset F$ such that $V\cap E\subset U$. Continuous functionals separate every nonzero point. A finite subcover of the compact set $K\setminus \tfrac 14V$ by sets where one such functional is nonzero produces a closed finite-codimensional $F_0\subset F$ with $K\cap F_0\subset \tfrac 14V$. Replace $E$ by the closed finite-codimensional subspace $E_0=h^{-1}(F_0)$ and $U$ by $U_0=U\cap E_0$. Then $K_0=\overline{h(U_0)}$ is compact and $K_0\subset \tfrac 14V$, while $V\cap E_0\subset U_0$.

Let $q$ be the Minkowski seminorm of $V$, and put $f=g+h$. For $x\in E_0$ and $t>q(x)$ we have $x\in tU_0$, so $h(x)\in tK_0$ and $q(hx)\leq t/4$. Letting $t\downarrow q(x)$ gives

A0294A1D9E73 2.12.4
\begin{equation} q(hx)\leq \tfrac 14q(x),\qquad q(x)\leq \tfrac 43q(fx). \end{equation}

For any prescribed neighborhood $\Omega $ in $E_0$, choose a balanced neighborhood $W$ in $F$ with $(W+W)\cap E_0\subset \Omega $. Compactness makes $K_0$ bounded, so choose $t>0$ with $tK_0\subset W$. If $fx\in W$ and $q(fx)<3t/4$, then $x\in tU_0$ and $hx\in W$; thus $x=fx-hx\in \Omega $. This proves inverse continuity at zero and also injectivity, by intersecting all $\Omega $.

We must also prove closedness of the image. If a net $f(x_ i)$, $x_ i\in E_0$, converges to $y\in F$, (2.12.4) bounds $q(x_ i)$ on a tail. Thus $x_ i\in tU_0$ for one fixed $t$, and $h(x_ i)\in tK_0$. A compact subnet converges to some $z$. Along it, $x_ i=fx_ i-hx_ i\to y-z$. Since $E_0$ is closed in $F$, $x=y-z$ belongs to $E_0$. Its induced topology and continuity of $h$ imply $hx=z$, hence $fx=y$. This proves closed image without a completeness assumption on the original spaces.

Return now to the finite-dimensional subspaces removed above. A finite-dimensional extension of a closed subspace is closed, as seen in the Hausdorff quotient. The kernel of $f$ injects into the finite-dimensional quotient by the closed subspace on which $f$ is injective, so it is finite dimensional. To check the quotient topology as well, choose vectors mapping to a basis of $f(E)/f(E_0)$. The resulting finite-dimensional complement to $f(E_0)$ is continuous, and its basis lifts, together with the continuous inverse on $f(E_0)$, give a continuous map into $E/\ker f$ inverse to the induced map. This proves the full quasi-monomorphism assertion.

For the second assertion first quotient $F$ by a finite-dimensional complement to $g(E)$. It suffices to show that after a further finite-dimensional quotient the perturbed map is surjective: the finite-codimensional image criterion above will then give closed image and openness. We may therefore assume $g$ surjective and open. Choose convex balanced open $U$ with $K=\overline{h(U)}$ compact, and put $V=g(U)$. Cover $K$ by finitely many translates $b_ j+\tfrac 14V$ and quotient $F$ by $S=\operatorname {span}\{ b_ j\} $. The compact image of $K$ is the closure of the image of $h(U)$ and is contained in $\tfrac 14$ of the image of $V$. Finite-dimensional quotients remain Fréchet. Renaming the induced data, we have

\[ g(E)=F,\qquad K\subset \tfrac 12V. \]

Let $y_0\in V$. If $y_ n$ is a residual, choose $x_ n$ with $gx_ n=y_ n$, and put $y_{n+1}=y_ n-fx_ n=-hx_ n$. Choosing $x_ n\in 2^{-n}U$ gives $y_{n+1}\in 2^{-n}K\subset 2^{-n-1}V$; this is possible inductively, but convergence in all source seminorms requires a further choice. Take a convex balanced neighborhood basis $U_ p$ in $E$ with $U_{p+1}\subset \tfrac 12U_ p$. For each $p$, the increasing open sets

\[ g(2^ N U_ p\cap \tfrac 12U),\qquad N\geq 0, \]

cover $g(\tfrac 12U)$ and hence $K$. Compactness gives strictly increasing integers $N_ p$ tending to infinity with

\[ K\subset g(2^{N_ p}U_ p\cap \tfrac 12U). \]

For $N_ p<n\leq N_{p+1}$ the residual $y_ n\in 2^{1-n}K$ therefore has a lift satisfying

\[ x_ n\in 2^{N_ p+1-n}U_ p\cap 2^{-n}U. \]

Use these choices and arbitrary allowed lifts for the finitely many initial indices. Each block sum belongs to $2U_ p$; tails of successive blocks are bounded by $4U_ p$, because the basis shrinks geometrically. Partial blocks obey the same bound. The partial sums are thus Cauchy in every defining neighborhood, and completeness gives $x=\sum _ nx_ n\in E$. The residuals tend to zero, since they lie in scalar multiples of the bounded compact set $K$. Hence $fx=y_0$. The image contains $V$ and is all of $F$ by linearity. Undoing the finite-dimensional quotients gives the claimed quasi-epimorphism. $\square$

Theorem 2.12.5 (Cartan–Serre).D8E2BCEBC75D Let $(E^\bullet ,d)$ and $(F^\bullet ,\delta )$ be complexes of Fréchet spaces with continuous differentials, and let $\rho ^\bullet :E^\bullet \to F^\bullet $ be a continuous chain map. Give cohomology its quotient topology on cycles modulo boundaries. If $\rho ^ q$ is compact and $H^ q(\rho )$ is surjective, then $H^ q(F^\bullet )$ is Hausdorff and finite dimensional. More generally, without compactness, every surjective $H^ q(\rho )$ is open. A bijective cohomology map of this kind is a topological isomorphism, even if its spaces are not Hausdorff.

Proof. Cycle spaces are closed Fréchet subspaces. The map

\[ G:Z^ q(E)\oplus F^{q-1}\longrightarrow Z^ q(F),\qquad G(x,y)=\rho ^ q x+\delta y, \]

is continuous and surjective by the cohomology assumption, hence open. If $\rho ^ q$ is compact, so is $H(x,y)=-\rho ^ qx$: restrict a compactness neighborhood to $Z^ q(E)$ and take its product with the whole space $F^{q-1}$. The closures lie in $Z^ q(F)$, which is closed. The perturbed map $G+H=(x,y)\mapsto \delta y$ is a quasi-epimorphism by theorem 2.12.3. Its image $B^ q(F)$ is therefore closed and finite codimensional in $Z^ q(F)$, proving the first assertion.

For openness, let $O\subset H^ q(E)$ be open and let $U\subset Z^ q(E)$ be its inverse image. The inverse image in $Z^ q(F)$ of $H^ q(\rho )(O)$ is $\rho ^ q(U)+B^ q(F)=G(U\times F^{q-1})$, which is open. The quotient topology therefore makes $H^ q(\rho )(O)$ open. No closed-boundary hypothesis was used in this last argument. $\square$
