Lemma 2.20.1 (Positive fourth-order modes).CB1875F07408 Let $z\geq 2$, $0<a<1$, and $\lambda _ z(t)=z^3-z^4t^4\geq 0$ for $0\leq t\leq a$. There is a positive nondecreasing solution $w$ of

\[ (\partial _ t-z)^4w=\lambda _ z(t)w \]

with any prescribed positive value at any $s\in [0,a]$. For every integer $m\geq 0$ there are constants $C_ m,A_ m$ independent of $z,a,s$ and that prescribed value such that

9F264158A115 2.20.2
\begin{equation} |w^{(m)}(t)|\leq C_ m z^{A_ m}w(t)\qquad (0\leq t\leq a). \end{equation}

There are absolute constants $c,C>0$ such that, whenever $0\leq T\leq t\leq a$ and $0\leq l\leq \lambda _ z\leq L$ on $[T,t]$,

F83983317719 2.20.3
\begin{equation} c z^{-3}e^{(z+l^{1/4})(t-T)} \leq \frac{w(t)}{w(T)} \leq C z^3e^{(z+L^{1/4})(t-T)}. \end{equation}

Proof. Solve $y^{(4)}=\lambda _ z y$ with $y^{(j)}(0)=1$ for $0\leq j\leq 3$. Here existence and the needed comparison principle have an elementary integral proof. The first-order state $Y=(y,y',y'',y''')$ satisfies $Y'=M(t)Y$, where $M$ has three superdiagonal entries equal to one and its bottom-left entry equal to $\lambda _ z(t)$, all other entries being zero. Successive substitution in $Y=Y(0)+\int _0^ t M(v)Y(v)\, dv$ converges uniformly on $[0,a]$: its $j$th iterated-integral term is bounded by $\| Y(0)\| (a\sup \| M\| )^ j/j!$. Differentiating the integral equation gives a smooth solution. The same series on $[T,t]$, including a nonnegative inhomogeneous term, proves componentwise comparison, since every matrix entry is nonnegative. In particular all four state components are at least one and are nondecreasing.

For $0\leq j\leq 2$, integration gives $y^{(j)}\leq 1+a y^{(j+1)}\leq 2y^{(j+1)}$. Also $y'''\leq 1+a z^3y\leq 2z^3y$. Thus

\[ 2^{-j}y\leq y^{(j)}\leq 8z^3y\qquad (0\leq j\leq 3). \]

All derivatives of $\lambda _ z$ are bounded by a fixed power of $z$ on this interval, with constants depending only on their order. Differentiating $y^{(4)}=\lambda _ z y$ and inducting therefore bounds every $|y^{(m)}|$ by $C_ m z^{A_ m}y$. Multiplication by $e^{zt}$, and a positive constant to set $w(s)$, proves the equation, positivity, monotonicity and (2.20.2).

For the upper growth bound compare $y$ on $[T,t]$ with

\[ q(v)=8z^3y(T)e^{(L^{1/4}+1)(v-T)}. \]

Its first four state values dominate those of $y$ at $T$, and $q^{(4)}\geq \lambda _ z q$. For the lower bound use

\[ p(v)=\frac{y(T)}{8(1+l)}e^{l^{1/4}(v-T)}. \]

Its state at $T$ is dominated by that of $y$, because $l^{j/4}/(1+l)\leq 1$ for $0\leq j\leq 3$; also $p^{(4)}\leq \lambda _ zp$. The componentwise comparison just proved applies to both. Since $t-T<1$ and $l\leq z^3$, their prefactors give the stated bounds after multiplication by $e^{z(t-T)}$. $\square$

Theorem 2.20.4 (A smooth fourth-order weak-continuation counterexample).2D8A7E49E474 For some $a\in (0,1)$ there are $f,u\in C^\infty ((-a,a)\times \mathbb R,\mathbb C)$ such that

A5DFFD00C673 2.20.5
\begin{equation} \big ((\partial _ t-i\partial _ x)^4+t^4\partial _ x^4 +i\partial _ x^3\big )u=f u. \end{equation}

Both functions vanish for $t\leq 0$, whereas $u$ is not identically zero in any neighborhood of any point $(0,x)$.

Proof. The construction uses only the preceding positive modes and two separated cutoff transitions. Write $\mathcal L$ for the operator on the left of (2.20.5). Set

\[ \begin{aligned} z_ n& =n^{11/4},& a_ n& =n^{-11/16}=z_ n^{-1/4},& \Delta _ n& =a_ n-a_{n+1},\\ r_ n& =\Delta _ n/10^4,& b_ n& =a_ n-4r_ n,& s_ n& =a_ n-2r_ n. \end{aligned} \]

The functions $\lambda _ n(t)=z_ n^3-z_ n^4t^4$ are nonnegative on $[0,a_ n]$. Define also

\[ G_ n=z_ na_ n^{3/4}\Delta _ n^{1/4}. \]

The mean value theorem for powers of $n$ gives

D652D0FDF2A4 2.20.6
\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \Delta _ n& \sim \tfrac {11}{16}n^{-27/16},& G_ n& \sim (\tfrac {11}{16})^{1/4}n^{29/16},\\ r_ nG_ n& \sim 10^{-4}(\tfrac {11}{16})^{5/4}n^{1/8},& z_ n-z_{n-1}& \sim \tfrac {11}{4}n^{28/16}. \end{aligned} \end{equation}

In particular the last difference is $o(G_ n)$. On $[b_ n,a_ n]$, factoring $a_ n^4-t^4$ gives

\[ 0\leq \lambda _ n(t) \leq 16z_ n^4a_ n^3r_ n=(16/10^4)G_ n^4. \]

Put $l_ n=\lambda _{n-1}(a_ n)$. Since $\lambda _{n-1}$ decreases, it is at least $l_ n$ on this interval, and

\[ \frac{l_ n}{G_ n^4} =\frac{z_{n-1}^4(a_{n-1}^4-a_ n^4)}{z_ n^4a_ n^3\Delta _ n}\longrightarrow 4. \]

Here $(a_{n-1}-a_ n)/\Delta _ n\to 1$ and the factored cubic sum, divided by $a_ n^3$, tends to four. Thus for sufficiently large $n$ one has $l_ n^{1/4}\geq G_ n$, whereas the upper fourth root for $\lambda _ n$ is at most $G_ n/5$. Increasing the starting integer if necessary gives the uniform growth-rate gap

55589EE4444A 2.20.7
\begin{equation} (z_{n-1}+l_ n^{1/4})-(z_ n+G_ n/5)\geq G_ n/2. \end{equation}

Fix such an integer $N\geq 2$. Use the preceding lemma to choose $w_ N$ on $[0,a_ N]$ with $w_ N(s_ N)=1$, and successively $w_ n$ on $[0,a_ n]$, $n>N$, with

8296A62C8BD3 2.20.8
\begin{equation} w_ n(s_ n)=w_{n-1}(s_ n). \end{equation}

The arguments $s_ n$ belong to both intervals. All these functions are positive and nondecreasing, and their relative derivatives have polynomial bounds in $n$, independent of their normalization. Their complex modes

\[ u_ n(t,x)=w_ n(t)e^{-iz_ nx} \]

satisfy $\mathcal L u_ n=0$: indeed $(\partial _ t-i\partial _ x)$ becomes $(\partial _ t-z_ n)$, $\partial _ x^4$ becomes $z_ n^4$, and $i\partial _ x^3$ becomes $-z_ n^3$. Every mixed derivative of $u_ n$ is bounded in absolute value by $C_ m n^{A_ m}w_ n(t)$, for total order at most $m$.

Compare the two modes about their equal value at $s_ n$. For $s_ n\leq t\leq a_ n$, the upper growth bound for $w_ n$ and the lower one for $w_{n-1}$, together with (2.20.7), give

\[ \frac{w_ n(t)}{w_{n-1}(t)} \leq C n^ A e^{-G_ n(t-s_ n)/2}. \]

For $b_ n\leq t\leq s_ n$, apply the same two growth bounds on $[t,s_ n]$ and invert the lower bound. This gives

\[ \frac{w_{n-1}(t)}{w_ n(t)} \leq C n^ A e^{-G_ n(s_ n-t)/2}. \]

By (2.20.6), after increasing $N$ there is $c>0$ such that, for $n>N$, $\epsilon _ n=e^{-c n^{1/8}}<1/2$ and

4B3C0F159C1D 2.20.9
\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} |u_ n/u_{n-1}|& \leq \epsilon _ n& & (a_ n-r_ n\leq t\leq a_ n),\\ |u_{n-1}/u_ n|& \leq \epsilon _ n& & (b_ n\leq t\leq b_ n+r_ n). \end{aligned} \end{equation}

The polynomial prefactor is absorbed because $n^{1/8}/\log n\to \infty $. All comparisons are uniform in $x$.

Choose a smooth $\rho :\mathbb R\to [0,1]$ equal to zero for $v\leq 1/4$ and to one for $v\geq 3/4$, with bounded derivatives of each order. For example normalize the integral of $e^{-1/(v(1-v))}$ on $(0,1)$, extend it constantly, and compose with $v\mapsto 2v-1/2$. These constant collars will make the adjacent strip definitions agree on neighborhoods of their endpoints. On $I_ n=[a_{n+1},a_ n]$, $n>N$, put

\[ A_ n(t)=\rho ((t-b_ n)/r_ n),\qquad B_ n(t)=1-\rho ((t-a_ n+r_ n)/r_ n),\qquad u=A_ nu_{n-1}+B_ nu_ n. \]

On the lower transition $[b_ n,b_ n+r_ n]$ one has $B_ n=1$ and $|u|\geq (1-\epsilon _ n)|u_ n|$. On the upper transition $[a_ n-r_ n,a_ n]$ one has $A_ n=1$ and $|u|\geq (1-\epsilon _ n)|u_{n-1}|$. Between them both cutoffs are one; outside them in $I_ n$ they are constant as well. Consequently $\mathcal L u=0$ outside the two transition bands, even if the two modes cancel in the middle. Define $f=\mathcal L u/u$ on the bands and $f=0$ elsewhere. The displayed lower bounds give nonvanishing neighborhoods of every band endpoint. There the residual is identically zero on the adjacent constant-cutoff side. Thus this definition is smooth at every endpoint; it never divides by a possible middle zero.

For clarity we check all derivatives of this quotient. On the upper band, $\mathcal L u=[\mathcal L,B_ n]u_ n$. Each derivative of this commutator is bounded by $C_ m n^{A_ m}|u_ n|$: it is a finite sum of derivatives of the cutoffs, the polynomial coefficients of $\mathcal L$, and derivatives of $u_ n$. Here $r_ n^{-1}$ is bounded by a power of $n$. On the same band, every derivative of $u$ is bounded by a power of $n$ times $|u_{n-1}|$, and $|u|\geq |u_{n-1}|/2$. Repeated differentiation of $u^{-1}$, using the product rule on $u u^{-1}=1$, therefore bounds its derivatives by a power of $n$ times $|u_{n-1}|^{-1}$. The quotient rule and (2.20.9) give

8B718C586209 2.20.10
\begin{equation} \sup _{I_ n\times \mathbb R}|D^\alpha f| \leq C_ m n^{A_ m}\epsilon _ n, \qquad |\alpha |\leq m. \end{equation}

On the lower band use $[\mathcal L,A_ n]u_{n-1}$ and the dominant mode $u_ n$ instead. Outside the bands the derivatives of $f$ are zero, completing the proof of this estimate.

Near the upper endpoint of $I_ n$ the resulting $u$ is $u_{n-1}$; near its lower endpoint it is $u_ n$. These formulas agree with the neighboring strip, and $f=0$ near every $a_ n$. On $a_{N+1}<t<a_ N$ put $u=u_ N$ and $f=0$. Thus both functions are smooth on $(0,a_ N)\times \mathbb R$ and satisfy the equation there.

Finally these functions extend smoothly and flatly by zero to $t\leq 0$. To verify this rather than assuming it, note $s_ n<s_{n-1}$ and (2.20.8); monotonicity gives $w_ n(s_ n)\leq w_{n-1}(s_{n-1})\leq 1$. Since $a_ n<s_{n-1}$, the upper-band estimate at $a_ n$ also gives

\[ w_ n(t)\leq w_ n(a_ n) \leq \epsilon _ n w_{n-1}(a_ n)\leq \epsilon _ n \qquad (0\leq t\leq a_ n). \]

For $n\geq N+2$, both modes used on $I_ n$ therefore have size at most $\epsilon _{n-1}$, after decreasing $c$ if needed. The relative derivative and cutoff estimates imply

\[ \sup _{I_ n\times \mathbb R}|D^\alpha u| \leq C_ m n^{A_ m}\epsilon _{n-1},\qquad |\alpha |\leq m. \]

Together with (2.20.10), these bounds decay faster than any power of $t$, since $t$ on $I_ n$ is comparable to $n^{-11/16}$. Every derivative thus tends uniformly to zero as $t\downarrow 0$. The zero extensions are smooth: induct on derivative order, using the zero boundary values for tangential derivatives and the fundamental theorem of calculus for the transverse derivative. All their derivatives vanish on $t=0$, so the differential equation also holds there and on $t<0$.

Take $a=a_ N$. At each $t=a_ n$, $n>N$, the constructed function is $u_{n-1}(a_ n,x)$, which is nonzero for every $x$. As $a_ n\downarrow 0$, this proves the required nontriviality in every neighborhood of each $(0,x)$, and completes the construction. $\square$

Proposition 2.20.11 (An ordinary second-order prolongation).CE8EC0706B26 Let $\Omega $ be an open subset of $\{ (t,x)\in \mathbb R^2:|t|<1\} $, and let $f\in C^\infty (\Omega ,\mathbb C)$. Put

\[ D=\partial _ t-i\partial _ x,\qquad A=D^2+it^2\partial _ x^2,\qquad B=D^2-it^2\partial _ x^2, \qquad P_ f=D^4+t^4\partial _ x^4+i\partial _ x^3-f. \]

There is a smooth second-order elliptic differential operator $\mathcal P_ f$ on the trivial complex bundle $\Omega \times \mathbb C^3$ such that every smooth solution $u$ of $P_ fu=0$ gives a solution

\[ U=(u,\partial _ xu,Bu),\qquad \mathcal P_ fU=0. \]

The section $U$ is zero on every open set on which $u$ is zero, and $U$ is nonzero whenever $u$ is nonzero. The realification of $\mathcal P_ f$ is also an ordinary second-order elliptic operator on a trivial real bundle of rank six.

Proof. The coefficients $t^2$ commute with $\partial _ x$, and $D(t^2)=2t$, $D^2(t^2)=2$. Applying the product rule to a test function gives the exact differential-operator identity

\[ AB=D^4+t^4\partial _ x^4-4it\partial _ x^2D-2i\partial _ x^2. \]

Consequently

\[ P_ f=AB+Q\partial _ x-f,\qquad Q=4it\partial _ t\partial _ x+(4t+i)\partial _ x^2+2i\partial _ x. \]

Here the term $4t\partial _ x^3$ comes from $4it\partial _ x^2(-i\partial _ x)$; in particular it has positive sign. Define, for arbitrary smooth triples $(u,w,v)$,

\[ \mathcal P_ f \begin{pmatrix} u \\ w \\ v \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} Bu-v \\ Bw-\partial _ xv \\ Av+Qw-fu \end{pmatrix}. \]

This is a differential operator of order two in every component. Since $B$ commutes with $\partial _ x$, substitution of $w=\partial _ xu$, $v=Bu$ makes its first two components zero, and the third component is exactly $P_ fu$. This proves the asserted solution map. Derivatives of a function that is identically zero on an open set are identically zero there; the first component also proves the nontriviality assertion.

For ellipticity, take the principal-symbol convention that replaces $\partial _ t,\partial _ x$ by real variables $\tau ,\xi $; the alternative convention with $i\tau ,i\xi $ changes the entire second-order matrix by a nonzero scalar and gives the same invertibility test. The principal matrix is

\[ \sigma _2(\mathcal P_ f)(t,x;\tau ,\xi )= \begin{pmatrix} b & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & b & 0 \\ 0 & q & a \end{pmatrix}, \quad \begin{aligned} a& =(\tau -i\xi )^2+it^2\xi ^2,\\ b& =(\tau -i\xi )^2-it^2\xi ^2,\\ q& =4it\tau \xi +(4t+i)\xi ^2. \end{aligned} \]

Its determinant is $b^2a$. If $\xi =0$ and $(\tau ,\xi )\ne 0$, then $a=b=\tau ^2\ne 0$. If $\xi \ne 0$, the vanishing of either $a$ or $b$ would require, on putting $r=\tau /\xi \in \mathbb R$,

\[ r^2-1=0,\qquad -2r\pm t^2=0. \]

The first equation gives $r=1$ or $r=-1$, whereas the second is impossible for $|t|<1$. Thus the symbol is invertible at every nonzero real covector. This proves ordinary ellipticity, without a Douglis–Nirenberg weighting or an unasserted positivity of the symbol.

Write complex coefficients and components in real and imaginary parts. The real principal matrix is the realification of the displayed complex matrix. Its determinant is $|b^2a|^2$, so it is invertible as a real matrix at the same covectors. The real and imaginary parts of $U$ give the corresponding real solution and preserve its zero set and nontriviality. $\square$

Corollary 2.20.12 (Failure of unrestricted elliptic-system continuation).1ADDDDB90B14 There is a connected smooth real surface carrying a smooth ordinary second-order elliptic operator on a rank-three complex vector bundle with a nonzero smooth kernel section vanishing on a nonempty open set. The same assertion holds for a rank-six real vector bundle.

Proof. Take $\Omega =(-a,a)\times \mathbb R$ and the functions from Theorem 2.20.4, with $a<1$. Apply Proposition 2.20.11. Its section $U$ solves the homogeneous second-order elliptic system, is nonzero because its first component is $u$, and vanishes on $(-a,0)\times \mathbb R$. The realification has the same properties. $\square$

Remark 2.20.13 (Scalar and matrix principal symbols).ED67DD231FDF The preceding corollary disproves weak continuation for arbitrary smooth matrix-principal elliptic systems under ordinary symbol invertibility. For harmonic forms the relevant hypothesis is more restrictive: a single real elliptic second-order principal operator acts on every component, with smooth coupled lower-order terms. The weak-continuation theorem 2.16.7 applies to that class. The constructed system instead has distinct principal symbols $a,b$ and the second-order off-diagonal term $q$, so it does not belong to that class.
