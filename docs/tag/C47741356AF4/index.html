9.61.1 Linear growth and affine algebraicityC47741356AF4

An algebraic subset of $\mathbb C^ n$ is a common zero set of polynomials. Finite equations suffice by theorem 9.58.2. Algebraic irreducibility refers to decompositions into algebraic subsets; analytic irreducibility refers to closed analytic subsets.

Lemma 9.61.2.D8FA405BDFAC Suppose a nonempty pure $p$-dimensional analytic subset $A\subset \mathbb C^ p\times \mathbb C^{n-p}$ satisfies

D6D620F9E57D 9.61.3
\begin{equation} |z''|\leq C(1+|z'|)\quad \text{on }A. \end{equation}

Projection to $z'$ is proper and finite. If $p>0$, it is onto, and outside a proper analytic subset of $\mathbb C^ p$ it is a covering with a constant finite positive number of sheets. The global analytic irreducible components of $A$ are finite in number. For $p=0$, $A$ is a finite set.

Proof. The inverse image of a compact base set is a closed subset of a bounded product, and hence compact. A fiber is a compact analytic subset of a Euclidean space, so it is finite by the maximum principle on analytic branches. For $p=0$ this already proves the assertion. For $p>0$, the finite-open-map theorem proper finite representatives of analytic map germs makes projection open at every point of the pure-dimensional source. Its nonempty image is also closed by properness, and therefore is all of $\mathbb C^ p$.

Here are the precise exceptional-set and covering steps. The union of the singular locus with the rank-deficient regular locus is analytic: in local ambient equations, the maximal minors formed from their differentials and the $p$ base coordinate rows test surjectivity on the regular tangent space and vanish on the singular locus. This is the same analytic critical-set construction in lemma 6.38.1. It contains no irreducible component. Otherwise on a regular open subset of that component the maximum differential rank would be less than $p$, and the constant-rank theorem would give a positive-dimensional fiber. Its dimension is thus at most $p-1$. Its proper image $E$ is analytic by Remmert's proper mapping theorem, and has dimension at most $p-1$ by dimension bounds for holomorphic images. Outside $E$ projection is a proper local biholomorphism. Finitely many inverse charts at one fiber and properness exclude extra inverse points over a smaller base neighborhood, proving that it is a covering. The complement of $E$ in $\mathbb C^ p$ is connected by bounded holomorphic removal and connected complements, so its sheet number is constant.

Each global irreducible component $B$ is pure of dimension $p$, by purity and dimension of irreducible analytic sets. Projection on $B$ is again proper, finite and open, and hence onto. It meets the finite fiber over zero. There are only finitely many local branches of $A$ at the points of that fiber. Every such global component contains one of those branches, and distinct components cannot contain the same branch: it would give a nonempty regular open overlap, and the identity principle on the connected regular locus would identify the components. Thus there are only finitely many global components. $\square$

Theorem 9.61.4.D15F5F84A74A Every pure $p$-dimensional algebraic subset of $\mathbb C^ n$ admits linear coordinates $(z',z'')$, with $z'\in \mathbb C^ p$, in which (9.61.3) holds. Conversely, a pure $p$-dimensional analytic subset satisfying that bound is algebraic.

Proof. First suppose $A$ is algebraic and nonempty. Homogenize a finite set of its defining equations and let $B$ be their common zero set in $\mathbb P^ n$. Its part outside the hyperplane at infinity $H_\infty $ is exactly $A$. By analytic closure after removing an analytic subset, the ordinary closure $\overline A$ in $\mathbb P^ n$ is analytic: it is the union of the components of $B$ not contained in $H_\infty $. Only finitely many occur, since $B$ is compact and its analytic components are locally finite. Every such component meets the affine chart in a pure $p$-dimensional open subset, so it is pure of dimension $p$. Its intersection with $H_\infty $ has dimension at most $p-1$ by purity of a nontrivial holomorphic zero set. Thus $D=\overline A\cap H_\infty $ has dimension at most $p-1$. If $p=0$, the closure is a finite set of points, none in $H_\infty $, and $A$ is finite and bounded. The assertion follows.

Choose a rank-$p$ linear map $L:\mathbb C^ n\to \mathbb C^ p$ whose projective kernel misses $D$. Such maps exist by the following explicit dimension argument. Stratify $D$ into countably many complex manifold charts of dimension at most $p-1$, using analytic singular strata and Hausdorff dimension. On a projective chart with one coordinate equal to one, the equation $Lz=0$ solves for one coefficient in each of the $p$ rows of $L$. Its incidence set over such a stratum is therefore parametrized by that stratum and $np-p$ free complex coefficients, of total dimension at most $np-1$. Restrict to countably many compact coordinate boxes. The incidence projection to the space of $L$ is Lipschitz on each box, so its image has Lebesgue measure zero by the elementary dimension estimate of analytic singular strata and Hausdorff dimension. The rank-deficient matrices form another null set, since they are contained in the zero set of a nonzero maximal minor. Choose $L$ outside these sets and extend its rows to a coordinate system, writing $z'=Lz$.

If the required bound failed, there would be $z_\nu \in A$ with $|z''_\nu |/(1+|z'_\nu |)\to \infty $. A subsequence of their projective points would converge to $[0:0:\zeta '']\in D$, with $\zeta ''\ne 0$. This is a point of the projective kernel of $L$, a contradiction. This proves the linear bound. The finite proper projection conclusions now follow from the lemma. When $p=n$ its kernel is zero and the same argument applies; the bound on the empty tuple $z''$ is automatic.

For the converse, the case $p=0$ is a finite set by the lemma, and a finite union of points is algebraic. Suppose $p>0$. Let $d$ be the covering degree and $E$ the exceptional set from the lemma. For a complex linear form $\ell $ in the vertical coordinates form the monic polynomial whose roots, with the covering multiplicities, are its values on a fiber:

\[ P_\ell (z',T)=\prod _{a\in \pi ^{-1}(z')} (T-\ell (a'')) =T^ d+\sum _{j=1}^ d c_{\ell ,j}(z')T^{d-j} \quad (z'\notin E). \]

The coefficients are holomorphic by local inverse sheets and symmetry. The cone bound gives

\[ |c_{\ell ,j}(z')|\leq \binom dj (C\| \ell \| )^ j(1+|z'|)^ j. \]

They are locally bounded across $E$, and extend to entire functions by bounded holomorphic removal and connected complements. The same bound holds after extension. On a polydisc of radius $R$, the Cauchy coefficient estimate bounds a Taylor coefficient of multi-degree $\alpha $ by a constant times $(1+\sqrt p R)^ jR^{-|\alpha |}$. For $|\alpha |>j$ let $R\to \infty $. That coefficient is zero. Hence $c_{\ell ,j}$ is a polynomial of total degree at most $j$.

The polynomial $P_\ell (z',\ell (z''))$ vanishes on $A$. This is first true outside $\pi ^{-1}(E)$, and then everywhere by density, since projection is locally open on each branch. These polynomials, for all $\ell $, define exactly $A$. Indeed fix $b=(b',b'')\notin A$. Choose $\ell $ whose value at $b''$ differs from its values at every point of the finite fiber $\pi ^{-1}(b')$; only finitely many proper linear hyperplanes of choices are excluded. All roots of $P_\ell (b',T)$ are values of $\ell $ on that fiber. To verify this even over $E$, take $z'_\nu \notin E$ tending to $b'$, enumerate the $d$ fiber points and pass to a subsequence on which all converge, using properness. Their limits belong to the fiber over $b'$. Passing to the product of the $d$ linear factors gives the claimed root description. It follows that $P_\ell (b',\ell (b''))\ne 0$.

The ideal generated by this family of polynomials is finitely generated by theorem 9.58.2. Its generators involve only finitely many family members, whose common zero set is consequently $A$. This proves algebraicity. $\square$

Corollary 9.61.5.A30D3E21C6D2 Every algebraic subset of $\mathbb C^ n$, without a purity assumption, has finitely many analytic irreducible components, and each is algebraic. It is irreducible algebraically if and only if it is irreducible analytically.

Proof. For a pure-dimensional algebraic set, choose the preceding linear growth coordinates. The lemma gives finitely many analytic components. Each is pure of the same dimension and inherits the cone bound, so the converse theorem makes it algebraic.

For a general algebraic set choose finite equations $P_1=\cdots =P_ N=0$ and induct on their number. Start with $\mathbb C^ n$, with its one irreducible component. Suppose the partial zero set already has finitely many analytic irreducible components, all algebraic. For one such component $B$, either $P_ j$ vanishes identically on $B$, in which case it is retained, or its zero set on $B$ is empty or pure of dimension $\dim B-1$ by purity of a nontrivial holomorphic zero set. This zero set is algebraic, as an intersection of algebraic sets. Its analytic components are finite and algebraic by the pure case just proved. Thus the next partial zero set is a finite union of irreducible analytic algebraic sets. Its components are the maximal members of that finite family, so the induction continues. Zero-dimensional components either persist or disappear; no negative dimension is introduced. The empty set causes no difficulty.

An algebraic decomposition is an analytic decomposition. Conversely, if an algebraic set is analytically reducible, its finite analytic components give a nontrivial algebraic decomposition by what was just proved. This proves both directions. $\square$

Proposition 9.61.6.05AC0FB85B88 For a nonzero polynomial $P$ on $\mathbb C^ n$, the irreducible analytic components of $\{ P=0\} $ correspond bijectively to the distinct nonconstant irreducible polynomial factors of $P$. In particular an irreducible polynomial defines an irreducible global analytic hypersurface.

Proof. We spell out the algebra used here. Polynomial rings over a field are factorial. Inductively, if $R$ is factorial and $K$ is its fraction field, call a polynomial in $R[T]$ primitive when its coefficients have no common prime factor. A product of primitive polynomials is primitive: reduction modulo any prime of $R$ gives a product of two nonzero polynomials over the domain $R/(\varpi )$, which is nonzero. Consequently contents multiply, up to units. Clearing denominators in a factorization in $K[T]$ and dividing out the contents therefore gives a factorization of a primitive polynomial in $R[T]$. In particular a primitive polynomial irreducible over $R$ is irreducible over $K$, hence prime over $K[T]$ by Euclidean division. The same content calculation descends its divisibilities to $R[T]$, making it prime there. Primes of $R$ remain prime because their quotient polynomial rings are domains. Factoring the content in $R$ and the primitive part in $K[T]$ gives existence of factorization; the primality just proved gives uniqueness. This proves the induction, starting from the field $\mathbb C$.

Write $P=c\prod _ j Q_ j^{m_ j}$ with distinct irreducible $Q_ j$. The strong algebraic Nullstellensatz theorem 9.58.2 gives $I(V(Q_ j))=\sqrt{(Q_ j)}=(Q_ j)$, a prime ideal. Its zero set is algebraically irreducible: if it were the union of two proper algebraic subsets, choose a polynomial vanishing on each subset but not on all of $V(Q_ j)$. Their product would belong to $(Q_ j)$ while neither factor did, a contradiction. It is therefore analytically irreducible by the preceding corollary. The zero sets for distinct $Q_ j$ are distinct and none contains another, since containment and the same ideal identity would give divisibility between the irreducible factors. They are exactly the components of their finite union $V(P)$. If $P$ is constant the zero set and factor family are both empty. $\square$
