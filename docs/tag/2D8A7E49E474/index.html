Theorem 2.20.4 (A smooth fourth-order weak-continuation counterexample)2D8A7E49E474

Theorem 2.20.4 (A smooth fourth-order weak-continuation counterexample).2D8A7E49E474 For some $a\in (0,1)$ there are $f,u\in C^\infty ((-a,a)\times \mathbb R,\mathbb C)$ such that

A5DFFD00C673 2.20.5
\begin{equation} \big ((\partial _ t-i\partial _ x)^4+t^4\partial _ x^4 +i\partial _ x^3\big )u=f u. \end{equation}

Both functions vanish for $t\leq 0$, whereas $u$ is not identically zero in any neighborhood of any point $(0,x)$.

Proof. The construction uses only the preceding positive modes and two separated cutoff transitions. Write $\mathcal L$ for the operator on the left of (2.20.5). Set

\[ \begin{aligned} z_ n& =n^{11/4},& a_ n& =n^{-11/16}=z_ n^{-1/4},& \Delta _ n& =a_ n-a_{n+1},\\ r_ n& =\Delta _ n/10^4,& b_ n& =a_ n-4r_ n,& s_ n& =a_ n-2r_ n. \end{aligned} \]

The functions $\lambda _ n(t)=z_ n^3-z_ n^4t^4$ are nonnegative on $[0,a_ n]$. Define also

\[ G_ n=z_ na_ n^{3/4}\Delta _ n^{1/4}. \]

The mean value theorem for powers of $n$ gives

D652D0FDF2A4 2.20.6
\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \Delta _ n& \sim \tfrac {11}{16}n^{-27/16},& G_ n& \sim (\tfrac {11}{16})^{1/4}n^{29/16},\\ r_ nG_ n& \sim 10^{-4}(\tfrac {11}{16})^{5/4}n^{1/8},& z_ n-z_{n-1}& \sim \tfrac {11}{4}n^{28/16}. \end{aligned} \end{equation}

In particular the last difference is $o(G_ n)$. On $[b_ n,a_ n]$, factoring $a_ n^4-t^4$ gives

\[ 0\leq \lambda _ n(t) \leq 16z_ n^4a_ n^3r_ n=(16/10^4)G_ n^4. \]

Put $l_ n=\lambda _{n-1}(a_ n)$. Since $\lambda _{n-1}$ decreases, it is at least $l_ n$ on this interval, and

\[ \frac{l_ n}{G_ n^4} =\frac{z_{n-1}^4(a_{n-1}^4-a_ n^4)}{z_ n^4a_ n^3\Delta _ n}\longrightarrow 4. \]

Here $(a_{n-1}-a_ n)/\Delta _ n\to 1$ and the factored cubic sum, divided by $a_ n^3$, tends to four. Thus for sufficiently large $n$ one has $l_ n^{1/4}\geq G_ n$, whereas the upper fourth root for $\lambda _ n$ is at most $G_ n/5$. Increasing the starting integer if necessary gives the uniform growth-rate gap

55589EE4444A 2.20.7
\begin{equation} (z_{n-1}+l_ n^{1/4})-(z_ n+G_ n/5)\geq G_ n/2. \end{equation}

Fix such an integer $N\geq 2$. Use the preceding lemma to choose $w_ N$ on $[0,a_ N]$ with $w_ N(s_ N)=1$, and successively $w_ n$ on $[0,a_ n]$, $n>N$, with

8296A62C8BD3 2.20.8
\begin{equation} w_ n(s_ n)=w_{n-1}(s_ n). \end{equation}

The arguments $s_ n$ belong to both intervals. All these functions are positive and nondecreasing, and their relative derivatives have polynomial bounds in $n$, independent of their normalization. Their complex modes

\[ u_ n(t,x)=w_ n(t)e^{-iz_ nx} \]

satisfy $\mathcal L u_ n=0$: indeed $(\partial _ t-i\partial _ x)$ becomes $(\partial _ t-z_ n)$, $\partial _ x^4$ becomes $z_ n^4$, and $i\partial _ x^3$ becomes $-z_ n^3$. Every mixed derivative of $u_ n$ is bounded in absolute value by $C_ m n^{A_ m}w_ n(t)$, for total order at most $m$.

Compare the two modes about their equal value at $s_ n$. For $s_ n\leq t\leq a_ n$, the upper growth bound for $w_ n$ and the lower one for $w_{n-1}$, together with (2.20.7), give

\[ \frac{w_ n(t)}{w_{n-1}(t)} \leq C n^ A e^{-G_ n(t-s_ n)/2}. \]

For $b_ n\leq t\leq s_ n$, apply the same two growth bounds on $[t,s_ n]$ and invert the lower bound. This gives

\[ \frac{w_{n-1}(t)}{w_ n(t)} \leq C n^ A e^{-G_ n(s_ n-t)/2}. \]

By (2.20.6), after increasing $N$ there is $c>0$ such that, for $n>N$, $\epsilon _ n=e^{-c n^{1/8}}<1/2$ and

4B3C0F159C1D 2.20.9
\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} |u_ n/u_{n-1}|& \leq \epsilon _ n& & (a_ n-r_ n\leq t\leq a_ n),\\ |u_{n-1}/u_ n|& \leq \epsilon _ n& & (b_ n\leq t\leq b_ n+r_ n). \end{aligned} \end{equation}

The polynomial prefactor is absorbed because $n^{1/8}/\log n\to \infty $. All comparisons are uniform in $x$.

Choose a smooth $\rho :\mathbb R\to [0,1]$ equal to zero for $v\leq 1/4$ and to one for $v\geq 3/4$, with bounded derivatives of each order. For example normalize the integral of $e^{-1/(v(1-v))}$ on $(0,1)$, extend it constantly, and compose with $v\mapsto 2v-1/2$. These constant collars will make the adjacent strip definitions agree on neighborhoods of their endpoints. On $I_ n=[a_{n+1},a_ n]$, $n>N$, put

\[ A_ n(t)=\rho ((t-b_ n)/r_ n),\qquad B_ n(t)=1-\rho ((t-a_ n+r_ n)/r_ n),\qquad u=A_ nu_{n-1}+B_ nu_ n. \]

On the lower transition $[b_ n,b_ n+r_ n]$ one has $B_ n=1$ and $|u|\geq (1-\epsilon _ n)|u_ n|$. On the upper transition $[a_ n-r_ n,a_ n]$ one has $A_ n=1$ and $|u|\geq (1-\epsilon _ n)|u_{n-1}|$. Between them both cutoffs are one; outside them in $I_ n$ they are constant as well. Consequently $\mathcal L u=0$ outside the two transition bands, even if the two modes cancel in the middle. Define $f=\mathcal L u/u$ on the bands and $f=0$ elsewhere. The displayed lower bounds give nonvanishing neighborhoods of every band endpoint. There the residual is identically zero on the adjacent constant-cutoff side. Thus this definition is smooth at every endpoint; it never divides by a possible middle zero.

For clarity we check all derivatives of this quotient. On the upper band, $\mathcal L u=[\mathcal L,B_ n]u_ n$. Each derivative of this commutator is bounded by $C_ m n^{A_ m}|u_ n|$: it is a finite sum of derivatives of the cutoffs, the polynomial coefficients of $\mathcal L$, and derivatives of $u_ n$. Here $r_ n^{-1}$ is bounded by a power of $n$. On the same band, every derivative of $u$ is bounded by a power of $n$ times $|u_{n-1}|$, and $|u|\geq |u_{n-1}|/2$. Repeated differentiation of $u^{-1}$, using the product rule on $u u^{-1}=1$, therefore bounds its derivatives by a power of $n$ times $|u_{n-1}|^{-1}$. The quotient rule and (2.20.9) give

8B718C586209 2.20.10
\begin{equation} \sup _{I_ n\times \mathbb R}|D^\alpha f| \leq C_ m n^{A_ m}\epsilon _ n, \qquad |\alpha |\leq m. \end{equation}

On the lower band use $[\mathcal L,A_ n]u_{n-1}$ and the dominant mode $u_ n$ instead. Outside the bands the derivatives of $f$ are zero, completing the proof of this estimate.

Near the upper endpoint of $I_ n$ the resulting $u$ is $u_{n-1}$; near its lower endpoint it is $u_ n$. These formulas agree with the neighboring strip, and $f=0$ near every $a_ n$. On $a_{N+1}<t<a_ N$ put $u=u_ N$ and $f=0$. Thus both functions are smooth on $(0,a_ N)\times \mathbb R$ and satisfy the equation there.

Finally these functions extend smoothly and flatly by zero to $t\leq 0$. To verify this rather than assuming it, note $s_ n<s_{n-1}$ and (2.20.8); monotonicity gives $w_ n(s_ n)\leq w_{n-1}(s_{n-1})\leq 1$. Since $a_ n<s_{n-1}$, the upper-band estimate at $a_ n$ also gives

\[ w_ n(t)\leq w_ n(a_ n) \leq \epsilon _ n w_{n-1}(a_ n)\leq \epsilon _ n \qquad (0\leq t\leq a_ n). \]

For $n\geq N+2$, both modes used on $I_ n$ therefore have size at most $\epsilon _{n-1}$, after decreasing $c$ if needed. The relative derivative and cutoff estimates imply

\[ \sup _{I_ n\times \mathbb R}|D^\alpha u| \leq C_ m n^{A_ m}\epsilon _{n-1},\qquad |\alpha |\leq m. \]

Together with (2.20.10), these bounds decay faster than any power of $t$, since $t$ on $I_ n$ is comparable to $n^{-11/16}$. Every derivative thus tends uniformly to zero as $t\downarrow 0$. The zero extensions are smooth: induct on derivative order, using the zero boundary values for tangential derivatives and the fundamental theorem of calculus for the transverse derivative. All their derivatives vanish on $t=0$, so the differential equation also holds there and on $t<0$.

Take $a=a_ N$. At each $t=a_ n$, $n>N$, the constructed function is $u_{n-1}(a_ n,x)$, which is nonzero for every $x$. As $a_ n\downarrow 0$, this proves the required nontriviality in every neighborhood of each $(0,x)$, and completes the construction. $\square$
