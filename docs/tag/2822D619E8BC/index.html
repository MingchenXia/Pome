Proposition 9.16.22822D619E8BC

Proposition 9.16.2.2822D619E8BC Let $D$ be a bounded connected domain in $\mathbb R^ m$ with a $C^2$ defining function $q$ on a neighborhood of $\overline D$: $D=\{ q<0\} $ there and $\mathrm dq\ne 0$ on $\partial D$. If $D^2q$ is positive definite on every real tangent space to $\partial D$, then $D$ is convex and every point of its boundary is an extreme point of $\overline D$.

Proof. Near a boundary point replace $q$ by $\widetilde q=q+M q^2$. At the boundary its real Hessian is

\[ D^2\widetilde q=D^2q+2M\, \mathrm dq\otimes \mathrm dq. \]

Write a vector as a tangential part plus a normal part. The tangential quadratic form is positive definite, mixed terms are bounded, and the last displayed term is a positive multiple of $M$ times the squared normal component. The inequality $2uv\leq \varepsilon u^2+\varepsilon ^{-1}v^2$ therefore makes the full Hessian positive definite for large enough $M$. It remains so on a smaller neighborhood by continuity, where $q$ and $q+Mq^2$ have the same sign. Thus $\overline D$ is locally convex at the boundary, being the nonpositive sublevel of a strictly convex function on a small ball. It is locally convex at its interior points as well. It is closed and connected, so the preceding proposition makes it convex. The regular defining function also gives $D=\operatorname {int}\overline D$: at a boundary point it has both signs locally, so that point cannot be in the interior of the closure. It follows that $D$ is convex.

If a boundary point were interior to a nontrivial segment in $\overline D$, take two nearby points of that segment in the small strictly convex defining-function neighborhood. Their values of $\widetilde q$ are nonpositive; strict convexity makes its value at the intermediate boundary point negative, a contradiction. This is exactly the claimed extreme-point property. $\square$
