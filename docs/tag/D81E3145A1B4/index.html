Theorem 23.3.3D81E3145A1B4

Theorem 23.3.3.D81E3145A1B4 Let $F:\mathbb C^2\to \mathbb C^2$ be either

\[ F(z_1,z_2)=(\lambda _1z_1,\lambda _2z_2), \qquad 0<|\lambda _1|\leq |\lambda _2|<1, \]

or

\[ F(z_1,z_2)=(\lambda z_1,\lambda z_2+z_1^ p), \qquad 0<|\lambda |<1,\quad p\in \mathbb Z_{\geq 1}. \]

Its integer iterates act freely and properly discontinuously on $U=\mathbb C^2\setminus \{ 0\} $. The quotient $X_ F=U/\langle F\rangle $ is a connected compact complex surface with

\[ \pi _1(X_ F)\simeq \mathbb Z,\qquad H^1_{\rm dR}(X_ F;\mathbb R)\simeq \mathbb R. \]

Consequently $X_ F$ admits no Kähler metric.

Proof. Both maps are polynomial automorphisms fixing zero. In the triangular case the inverse is obtained first from $z_1=\lambda ^{-1}w_1$ and then from $z_2=\lambda ^{-1}(w_2-z_1^ p)$. For $k\geq 1$ direct iteration gives

436562DE848D 23.3.4
\begin{equation} F^ k(z)=\left(\lambda ^ kz_1, \lambda ^ kz_2+\lambda ^{k-1}z_1^ p \sum _{j=0}^{k-1}\lambda ^{(p-1)j}\right). \end{equation}

For $p=1$ the sum is $k$; for $p>1$ it is bounded uniformly in $k$. Thus $F^ k\to 0$ uniformly on compact subsets of $\mathbb C^2$. The same assertion for the diagonal map follows immediately from its coordinate formula. If $F^ kz=z$ for $k>0$, the first coordinate forces $z_1=0$, and the second then forces $z_2=0$. A negative fixed iterate gives a positive one by applying its inverse. This proves freeness on $U$.

There is a bounded neighborhood $B$ of zero with $F(\overline B)\subset B$. For the diagonal map take an ordinary Euclidean ball. For the triangular map with $p>1$, its derivative at zero is $\lambda I$, so a sufficiently small Euclidean ball has this property. For $p=1$ use the norm

\[ \| (z_1,z_2)\| _ a=a|z_1|+|z_2|, \qquad a>(1-|\lambda |)^{-1}. \]

Indeed $\| Fz\| _ a\leq (|\lambda |+a^{-1})\| z\| _ a$. The closed balls for each of these norms are compact.

For compact $K\subset U$, uniform convergence to zero shows that $F^ k(K)\cap K=\varnothing $ for all sufficiently large positive $k$. For negative $k$ the same follows by applying $F^{-k}$ to an intersection. Thus only finitely many translates of any compact set meet it. Freeness and this property provide, about each point, a neighborhood whose distinct translates are disjoint: start with a relatively compact neighborhood, exclude its finitely many possible nontrivial intersecting translates by shrinking about the point, and use freeness for each such translate. These neighborhoods give quotient complex charts. The same compact-neighborhood argument separates two distinct orbits, so the quotient is Hausdorff; a countable basis upstairs projects to one downstairs. The quotient map is therefore a holomorphic covering map and $X_ F$ is a connected complex surface.

The compact set $K_0=\overline B\setminus F(B)$ is disjoint from zero. Every orbit meets $K_0$. In fact its forward iterates eventually belong to $B$, whereas its backward iterates cannot all belong to $B$: otherwise, writing $z=F^ k(F^{-k}z)$ with $F^{-k}z\in \overline B$ would force $z=0$ by uniform convergence on $\overline B$. The integers $j$ for which $F^ jz\in B$ consequently have a least member, since $F(B)\subset B$. The corresponding iterate belongs to $B\setminus F(B)$. Thus $K_0$ surjects onto $X_ F$, proving compactness.

Radial deformation retracts $U$ onto $S^3$, which is simply connected. Here this last assertion needs only an elementary loop argument: subdivide a continuous loop into arcs in coordinate balls and replace them, by straight-coordinate homotopies fixing endpoints, by piecewise smooth arcs. A finite union of such arcs misses some point of $S^3$; for example its three-dimensional measure is zero, as follows by covering each bounded-derivative arc with $O(\epsilon ^{-1})$ balls of radius $O(\epsilon )$. Stereographic projection identifies the complement of that point with $\mathbb R^3$, where the loop contracts by linear homotopy. Hence $U$ is simply connected.

Lift a based loop of $X_ F$ to $U$. Its endpoint is a unique $F^ kz_0$; concatenation adds $k$. Every integer occurs by projecting a path from $z_0$ to $F^ kz_0$. A loop with $k=0$ lifts to a closed loop, contracts upstairs and hence contracts downstairs. Path and homotopy lifting follow by finite subdivisions into the quotient charts just constructed. This proves $\pi _1(X_ F)=\mathbb Z$. The exact period comparison theorem 10.79.2 now gives $H^1_{\rm dR}(X_ F;\mathbb R)=\operatorname {Hom}(\mathbb Z,\mathbb R)$. For a compact Kähler manifold, conjugation exchanges the two summands $H^{1,0}$ and $H^{0,1}$ in theorem 10.47.13, so its first real Betti number is even. The value one just obtained is a contradiction. $\square$
