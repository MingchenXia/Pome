The second part constructs, from a smooth lattice polytope $P$, a projective toric manifold $X_ P$ with a very ample line bundle $L$. 25.1 Historical and bibliographical notes65E16F3944C7

These notes give the sources of the results in this book. For each chapter we indicate where the main results were first proved or where they can be found in the literature, compare our statements, proofs and normalizations with those of the sources, and point to alternative presentations. The sections follow the order of the book: each concerns one chapter, except that the notes on section 17.1 occupy three sections, and the last section concerns section 9.1. A few ideas recur throughout the book: a function is recovered from its distributional derivatives, exceptional sets are controlled by capacities, nonlinear measures are constructed by approximation with bounded potentials, and singular potentials are compared through their envelopes. These ideas have different origins, and their formulation has changed as the theory moved from domains in Euclidean space to compact complex manifolds.

When the book is compared with another source, the conventions of that source matter as much as its hypotheses. We use $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}=\mathrm i\partial \bar\partial /(2\pi )$; Lelong numbers are normalized so that $\log |z|^2$ has Lelong number $1$ at the origin, and multiplier ideals are defined with the weight $\mathrm e^{-\varphi }$. Several of the sources cited below use the operator $\mathrm{dd^ c}=\mathrm i\partial \bar\partial /\pi $, measure Lelong numbers against $\log |z|$ and use the weight $\mathrm e^{-2\varphi }$. A source with the last two conventions assigns to $\varphi =u/2$ the Lelong numbers and multiplier ideals that we assign to $u$. If the operator is multiplied by a constant $c>0$, then at fixed potentials a product of $p$ factors of the form $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u$ is multiplied by $c^ p$. We record these conversions where they matter.

Theorem and page numbers refer to the cited version of a work. When this version is a preprint rather than the published article, whose numbering or scope may differ, we say so. Two existence theorems are used without proof, namely the resolution and principalization theorems (theorem 9.6.6, theorem 9.6.7); their source is given in section 25.9.

25.1 Measures and distributions65E16F3944C7

The representation of positive linear functionals by measures, theorem 2.3.3, is the Riesz representation theorem. For its formulation on locally compact, $\sigma $-compact spaces compare [Theorem 24, Tao09Radon]; on open subsets of Euclidean space our proof constructs the measure from a metric outer measure and identifies the functional by slicing functions into level sets. The regularity and density statements in proposition 2.2.2, proposition 2.3.5 correspond to [Theorem 21, Exercise 22 and Proposition 10, Tao09Radon]. For the measurability part of the outer-measure construction compare [Theorem 5, Tao10Products]. The assertions on product measures and the interchange of integrals in proposition 2.2.7 form the Tonelli–Fubini theorem for $\sigma $-finite measures; compare [Theorem 36 and its proof, Tao10Products], where the monotone class lemma (Lemma 35 there) is applied to the Boolean algebra of finite disjoint unions of rectangles. We instead apply Dynkin's $\pi $–$\lambda $ theorem, lemma 2.2.6, to the rectangles themselves.

For the local order bound, regularization by mollifiers and the fact that positive distributions are measures (definition 2.4.1, proposition 2.5.3, theorem 2.5.6) compare [Section 1, Exercises 4–5; Section 2, Definition 1, Exercise 15 and Lemma 2, Tao09Distributions]. That source writes the test function first and gives the space of distributions the conjugate complex structure. Our pairing $\langle u,\chi \rangle =\int u\chi \, \mathrm d x$ is complex linear in both variables, the convention used for currents later in the book; with this pairing the derivative of a distribution is defined with the same sign as in that source.

The selection theorem for sequences of measures, theorem 2.6.5, is proved by a diagonal argument over countably many test functions. The compactness theorem for nets, theorem 2.7.3, rests instead on Tychonoff's theorem for a product of intervals, which we prove with ultrafilters (lemma 2.7.2); this is where the axiom of choice enters. Subnets are Willard subnets throughout the book, and proposition 2.7.1 characterizes compactness in these terms. The Arzelà–Ascoli theorem, proposition 2.7.4, is proved in the usual way, by showing that an equicontinuous and pointwise bounded family is uniformly bounded and totally bounded for the uniform metric.

For the differentiation of integrals compare [Section 2, Theorems 32 and 36, Exercise 38 and Lemma 39, Tao10Differentiation]. In theorem 2.8.3 we use cubes instead of balls. The covering argument is the same: a finite disjoint subfamily of the cubes covers the original cubes after enlargement by the factor three, and the constant in the maximal inequality becomes $3^ d$. The Poincaré-type inequality lemma 2.8.4 follows by integrating the fundamental theorem of calculus along segments parallel to the coordinate axes.

For the Radon–Nikodym theorem compare [Corollary 1, following Theorem 2, Tao09RN]. The book uses only the case of a Borel measure that is dominated, at least locally, by a constant multiple of a Radon measure, lemma 2.8.1. We prove it by an $L^2$ argument on half-open dyadic cubes, so that no assumption on the measure of cube boundaries is needed. This lemma provides, for instance, the densities of the coefficient measures in lemma 7.14.3.

25.1 Subharmonic functions and polar sets65E16F3944C7

The characterizations of subharmonic functions by comparison with harmonic functions and by mean values, their local integrability, the Riesz decomposition and the distributional characterization are developed in [Theorem 3.2.3, Corollary 3.2.8 and Theorems 3.2.9–3.2.11, pp. 143–148, Hor07]; see theorem 3.4.5, proposition 3.4.8, theorem 3.4.14, theorem 3.4.12. Since a subharmonic function is the limit of its means at every point, the canonical representative of a distribution with positive Laplacian is determined by the distribution at every point, not only almost everywhere. Our Riesz measure is $\Delta u$, without a factor $1/(2\pi )$; thus a logarithmic pole in the plane has Riesz mass $2\pi $.

For the compactness theorem and Hartogs' lemma (theorem 3.4.19, theorem 3.4.20) compare [Theorems 3.2.12–3.2.13, pp. 149–151, Hor07]. Theorem 3.2.13 there also contains convergence statements in Sobolev spaces and against measures; the latter are discussed in the next section. Choquet's lemma and the regularized upper envelope (lemma 3.4.22, theorem 3.4.23) correspond to [Proposition 3.4.4, pp. 205–206, Hor07]; theorem 3.4.23 also asserts that the pointwise supremum of an arbitrary family is Lebesgue measurable. For the local definition of polar sets and the removable singularity theorem (definition 3.4.26, theorem 3.4.27) compare [Definition 3.4.1 and Theorem 3.4.3, pp. 203–205, Hor07]. Our proof works directly with the local subharmonic functions of the definition, and the connectedness of the complement of a closed polar set in a domain follows from the maximum principle.

25.1 Energy, capacity and exceptional sets65E16F3944C7

The continuity principle and the restriction of a measure to compact sets on which its potential is continuous (theorem 4.1.10) follow the argument of [Theorems 3.4.6–3.4.7, pp. 207–208, Hor07]; the approximation lemma of Lusin type used there is lemma 4.1.9. The criterion for polarity of compact sets is [Theorem 3.4.5, pp. 206–208, Hor07]. Our relative capacity of a compact set is the total mass of the Riesz measure $\Delta u$ of its regularized relative extremal function $u$, and our Green functions are positive with $-\Delta G=\delta $; the numerical values of the capacity depend on these normalizations.

Strong subadditivity, outer regularity and capacitability are developed in [Section 3.4, pp. 209–216, especially Theorems 3.4.9 and 3.4.12 and Proposition 3.4.13, Hor07]. Choquet's capacitability theorem, theorem 4.1.23, which underlies the capacitability assertion of that section, is proved here by approximating continuous images of countable intersections of countable unions of compact sets from inside by compact sets. The convergence of integrals against measures with continuous potentials in lemma 4.1.25 is part of [Theorem 3.2.13, pp. 150–151, Hor07], and the fact that the exceptional sets of the limit theorems are polar, theorem 4.1.26, corresponds to [Theorem 3.4.14, pp. 216–217, Hor07]. This polarity strengthens the equality almost everywhere of an upper envelope and its regularization in theorem 3.4.23.

For the identity between Green energy and Dirichlet energy and for quasi-continuity compare [Propositions 3.4.19 and 3.4.21, pp. 221–223, Hor07]. In proposition 4.1.29 we work with the explicit, symmetric Green function of a ball. The positivity of the Newtonian energy and of the logarithmic energy (proposition 4.1.5) is proved by expressing the kernels as integrals of Gaussians. For the logarithmic kernel, positivity and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality are proved, and used, only for signed measures of total mass zero. The equilibrium measure is obtained by minimizing the energy, and proposition 4.1.27 describes its potential (Frostman's theorem) with our normalization of the kernels.

The example [Example 6.1.3, XiaSampleBook] uses an atom-free measure on a polar Cantor set and refers to [p. 31, Car83] for the Cantor set. Such a measure $\mu $ is constructed explicitly in proposition 4.1.12: the Cantor set $K$ is built from a rapidly decreasing sequence of lengths, so that the subharmonic function $p(z)=\int _ K\log |z-a|^2\, \mathrm d\mu (a)$ equals $-\infty $ at every point of $K$. The remaining ingredients of that example, namely the potential on the projective line, its integrability and the computation of its mass in degree one, are proposition 13.4.10, corollary 6.9.3, example 13.4.11.

25.1 Fine topology and balayage65E16F3944C7

Fuglede attributes the classical fine topology to Cartan and the quasi-Lindelöf principle to Doob in [Introduction, p. 227, Fug71Fine]. His preliminaries [Section 1, pp. 228–230, Fug71Fine] are formulated in an axiomatic harmonic space satisfying the domination axiom and admitting a positive potential, and they assume the general duality theory of sweeping before fine connectedness is studied. In the Euclidean setting of this book the quasi-Lindelöf principle, theorem 4.2.22, follows directly from Choquet's lemma and the polarity of the exceptional set of an upper envelope. By example 4.2.8, the fine topology is not Lindelöf, so a countable subfamily can only be expected to cover the union of a family of finely open sets up to a polar set.

Reduction and balayage (theorem 4.2.14, theorem 4.2.16, theorem 4.2.18) are treated only on a ball, where the Green function is explicit and symmetric and Green energy is a Dirichlet integral by proposition 4.2.11. In this setting the swept measure is determined by an orthogonality relation for the energy, which also gives additivity, and the general duality of sweeping recalled by Fuglede is not required. Example 4.2.20 expresses the swept potential of a Dirac measure through harmonic measure, for a compact set whose complement in the ball is a regular domain. Proposition 4.2.25 is proved directly from the definitions; it shows that for $n\geq 2$ the classical fine topology of $\mathbb C^ n=\mathbb R^{2n}$ differs from the topology generated by the upper semicontinuous functions that are subharmonic or identically $-\infty $ on complex lines, which is the plurifine topology of section 5.47 (example 5.47.17).

The theorem that a function which is subharmonic or identically $-\infty $ on complex lines is automatically upper semicontinuous is proved in the unsigned manuscript [Theorem 1.1 and its proof, pp. 1–6, PSHD], and our proof of theorem 5.8.4 completes the argument given there. In that manuscript, Lemma 1.2, pp. 1–2, proves measurability by induction on the dimension, using means over bounded fibers; Proposition 2.1, pp. 4–5, identifies a finite function that is subharmonic on lines with the canonical representative; and the final proof, p. 6, uses truncation. The incidence results needed for this argument are proved in section 2.9, and proposition 5.4.4 describes the restrictions of the canonical representative to complex lines without using the theorem. We keep the usual definition of plurisubharmonic functions (definition 5.8.1), which requires upper semicontinuity; theorem 5.8.4, proved shortly afterwards, shows that this condition is redundant.

The distributional characterization, upper envelopes and compactness of plurisubharmonic functions (theorem 5.9.1, theorem 5.10.2, theorem 5.10.4) are deduced from the corresponding results for subharmonic functions in section 3.4, section 4.1. At this stage the set where an upper envelope differs from its regularization is only known to be real polar, that is, polar as a subset of $\mathbb R^{2n}$ in local charts; its pluripolarity is proved in section 5.47. The extension theorem (theorem 5.11.3) gives both local boundedness and extension across a relatively closed set that is locally $\mathcal H^{2n-3}$-null; to apply it to analytic sets of codimension at least two (corollary 9.3.18) one needs the local estimate for the Hausdorff measure of analytic sets in terms of their dimension.

For $L^ p$ integrability and convergence in $L^ p_{\mathrm{loc}}$ compare [Theorem 1.48, parts 1–2 and proof Steps 1–2, pp. 30–32, GZ17]. As there, we pass from discs to polydiscs (lemma 5.10.5, proposition 5.10.8, theorem 5.10.10), but we use the Riesz decomposition with a compactly supported measure instead of the Poisson–Jensen formula on the boundary. Since our Riesz measure is $\Delta v$, its logarithmic potential carries the factor $1/(2\pi )$. The bound (5.10.9) gives uniform estimates on smaller polydiscs, and convergence in $L^ p_{\mathrm{loc}}$ then follows from Hölder's inequality. The identity with the factor two in the truncation display of [p. 32, GZ17] is incorrect, as is also noted in an unofficial list of errata for that book.

The local exponential integrability estimates (lemma 5.10.11, proposition 5.10.12) are proved with the same compactly supported Riesz decomposition and Jensen's inequality (lemma 2.2.4), with an exponent that depends only on a local $L^1$ bound; a finite covering by charts then gives the uniform estimate for normalized quasi-plurisubharmonic functions on a compact manifold, corollary 10.3.3.

The partial Legendre transform is due to Kiselman [Kis78]. For his minimum principle and the regularization argument in its proof compare [Chapter I, Section 7.B, Theorem (7.5) and its proof, p. 57, DemBook]. In theorem 5.12.1 the existence of an invariant exhaustion is a hypothesis, and the smooth case is proved with a Schur complement. Such an exhaustion is constructed for product tubes over open convex sets in corollary 5.12.2; the case of general pseudoconvex tubes, corollary 6.3.6, uses in addition the plurisubharmonicity of $-\log $ of the boundary distance.

For the Jensen–Lelong formula compare [Chapter III, (6.1)–(6.11), pp. 162–166, DemBook]. The book uses only its form on Euclidean balls, theorem 6.7.6. For smooth functions the formula follows from the derivative of spherical means, proposition 3.1.5, and the monotonicity of the density from the convexity of circle means on complex lines through the center; regularization, with lower bounds for spherical means given by the Poisson kernel, gives the general case. Demailly's operator there is twice ours, and he measures growth against $\log |z|$; the density in (6.7.5) is written with our $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}$ and $\log |z|^2$, so that $\nu (\log |z|^2,0)=1$.

The coherence of multiplier ideals, theorem 6.8.9, and its proof by generation from a weighted Hilbert space of holomorphic functions are presented in [Proposition 5.7, pp. 36–37, DemAnalyticOnline]; page numbers refer to the manuscript on Demailly's web page. The weight there is $\mathrm e^{-2\varphi }$, which becomes our $\mathrm e^{-\varphi }$ after doubling the potential. For the strong Noetherian property and the Artin–Rees lemma used in the proof compare [Chapter II, (3.22), p. 90, and Chapter IX, (1.1)–(1.3), pp. 403–404, DemBook]. The Weierstrass division theorem and the Noetherian property of the ring of germs of holomorphic functions (proposition 9.2.2, proposition 9.2.4) follow [Chapter II, (2.3)–(2.7), pp. 80–81, DemBook].
