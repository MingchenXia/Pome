An envelope is a maximal admissible potential lying below an obstacle. This construction already appears in Perron's solution of the classical Dirichlet problem. In complex geometry it also compares singularities, defines weak geodesics, and identifies where nonlinear measures can concentrate. The chapter on singularity types, envelopes, and models develops these uses together. A pointwise supremum generally needs upper semicontinuous regularization, and the discrepancy between the two is controlled by pluripolarity and capacity.

Rooftop envelopes impose two upper obstacles simultaneously. Singularity envelopes instead retain an asymptotic bound on the singularity while removing inessential bounded features. The $P$-envelopes of Ross–Witt Nyström, building on ideas of Rashkovskii–Sigurdsson, helped make this language systematic. In the relative theory of Darvas–Di Nezza–Lu, model potentials provide reference singularities for full-mass and finite-energy classes. Their importance comes from the interaction between envelope contact sets and Monge–Ampère mass, rather than from a choice of normalization alone.

Several distinctions are needed when using these constructions. Order up to a bounded additive term describes ordinary singularity type; literal pointwise order describes particular representatives. A rooftop envelope may be identically minus infinity unless its admissible family is nonempty. Equality of total masses does not automatically imply equality of singularity types, and a positive-mass argument may fail when the reference mass is zero. These are structural issues in the development of the theory, and explain the careful separation of envelope identities, comparison results, and zero-mass cases in Pome.

Multiplier ideals give another equivalence relation. Two potentials may have the same multiplier ideals at every positive scale while differing in ordinary singularity type. The $\mathcal I$-envelope retains this integrability data. Darvas–Xia formulated the associated model theory in connection with earlier work of Dano Kim and Boucksom–Favre–Jonsson; related formulations appear in subsequent work, including Trusiani's. The chapter on multiplier-ideal models and partial equilibrium studies this additional structure. Ordinary models and multiplier-ideal models answer different questions, so neither terminology should silently replace the other.

The envelope viewpoint reconnects the equation theory with algebraic approximation. A singularity constraint can be imposed analytically through an obstacle or algebraically through an ideal. The extent to which these constraints have the same mass, section growth, or energy is a theorem to be established. This is the conceptual reason that envelopes appear both before the metric theory and before the chapters on sections and non-Archimedean potentials.

Integral-normalized envelopes occur in Demailly's analytic treatment and in the supercanonical construction of Berman and Demailly. The reconstruction here fixes a rational line bundle, a denominator and a strictly positive reference metric with analytic singularities. A point can have an extremal admissible weight even when the entire pointwise envelope is not itself admissible for the same unit integral bound. Uniform point extension supplies single-section approximation in all sufficiently large allowed degrees and an explicit order-$1/m$ comparison of finite-degree envelopes. The intrinsic density calculation uses one tensor root and retains the complete section norm. No reduction from an arbitrary real cohomology class to this rational setting is used. This integral normalization is distinct from invariance under a flat twist of an adjoint section envelope.

On the projective line, the normalized chordal Green kernel gives an exact integral-normalized envelope for every nonnegative real curvature parameter. A weighted two-pole example shows that taking only single Green poles can give a strictly smaller envelope. The antipodal identity is an identity for the positive Fubini–Study measure; no invariance of its differential form under an antiholomorphic map is asserted. The discussion in [DemAnalyticOnline] uses its stated local operator normalization, and is not assigned a normalization defect.
