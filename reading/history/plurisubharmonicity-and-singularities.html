The passage to several complex variables begins with plurisubharmonic functions, introduced independently in the work of Oka and Lelong. Subharmonicity along complex lines provides a function-theoretic definition; positivity of the distributional complex Hessian provides its current-theoretic form. These two descriptions explain the joint treatment of plurisubharmonic functions and positive currents. Unlike real subharmonicity alone, plurisubharmonicity is adapted to holomorphic maps and complex analytic sets. Bremermann's historical account discusses the early development.

Demailly's analytic exposition supplies the current-theoretic background for the homotopy formulas, complete Hartogs domains, and relative smoothing developed here. Richberg's method underlies smoothing with a variable strict-positivity bound; Klimek attributes the global decreasing smooth approximation on pseudoconvex domains to Fornæss–Narasimhan. Exceptional-set geometry has its own subtleties: Diederich–Fornæss constructed a smooth curve in complex dimension two that is not pluripolar. The construction in Pome treats every complex dimension at least two.

The Green-function theory connects pseudoconvexity with invariant distances and spaces of holomorphic functions. Lempert's work establishes complex geodesics and holomorphic retractions on convex domains; stationary discs also lead to the smooth Green construction on smooth strongly convex domains. The dual extremal method is developed further by Royden–Wong–Krantz. The relation between hyperconvexity and the Fréchet space of holomorphic functions is treated by Aytuna, using the power-series-space methods of Vogt. Pome proves the bounded-domain characterization and states its hypotheses explicitly.

Currents allow differential forms with singular coefficients to participate in geometric operations. The Poincaré–Lelong formula identifies the complex Hessian of a logarithm with the current of a divisor, linking zeros of holomorphic functions to positivity. Lelong numbers then quantify concentration at a point. Siu's analyticity theorem shows that suitable upper level sets of these numbers are analytic, making a numerical measure of singularity detect complex subvarieties. The notions of positivity for currents were developed by Lelong and Harvey–Knapp; they have a history distinct from later regularization techniques.

Demailly's Complex Analytic and Differential Geometry develops real and complex currents alongside plurisubharmonic functions. Regularization and homotopy relate currents to smooth differential forms, while positivity provides measure coefficients and local mass bounds. Extension across complete pluripolar sets and integration over analytic sets connect this calculus to complex geometry. Slicing, generic multiplicity and Siu decomposition relate local densities to analytic cycles.

The generalized Lelong–Jensen theory uses plurisubharmonic weights to measure concentration and construct boundary measures. Demailly's memoir on Monge–Ampère measures and affine algebraicity and his work on Monge–Ampère and pluriharmonic measures are central sources for this development. The admissibility of a weight and properness on the relevant current support remain part of each comparison statement.

Another way to measure a singularity is to test which holomorphic germs remain square integrable against its exponential weight. Nadel's multiplier ideal sheaves turn this integrability condition into coherent algebraic data. The weighted estimates of Hörmander and the extension theorem of Ohsawa–Takegoshi supply the analytic mechanisms relating holomorphic functions to singular weights. The chapter on Lelong numbers, multiplier ideals, and regularization brings these descriptions together. Our convention is $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm c}=\mathrm i\partial\bar\partial/(2\pi)$ and the multiplier weight is $\exp(-\varphi)$; changing either convention changes numerical thresholds.

The openness problem asks how stable such integrability is under a small increase in the weight. Demailly–Kollár formulated and studied the openness conjecture; Berndtsson established an openness theorem, and Guan–Zhou proved strong openness. These results are distinct from approximation statements. Bergman kernels and regularization provide controlled models of singular weights, whereas openness concerns the ideal of a fixed weight. Historical attribution to these methods does not by itself supply the hypotheses or estimates required in a particular approximation argument.

The exceptional sets also have a specifically complex history. Josefson's local-to-global theorem identifies locally pluripolar sets in complex Euclidean space with global pole sets; global variants on compact manifolds require their own hypotheses. The plurifine topology is associated with Fuglede's work in the Lelong seminar. Bedford–Taylor developed its relation to nonlinear products, and El Marzguioui–Wiegerinck proved the local-connectivity result used in the theory. Keeping these contributions separate clarifies why pluripolarity, plurifine locality, and ordinary almost-everywhere identities have different roles.

The diagonal multiplier-ideal examples in Demailly's exposition connect local integrability with explicit monomial conditions. The complete real-exponent germ criterion already supplies the ideal itself; the additional finite-generator calculation records a concrete rectangular bound for the generators. Its radial change of variables retains the exact Jacobian, while the alternative metric convention changes the weight only by a bounded term.

Singular vanishing on a manifold with a plurisubharmonic exhaustion uses regularization on relatively compact neighborhoods, complete sublevel estimates, weighted Runge approximation and a diagonal limit. A fixed positive curvature constant gives a global square-integrable estimate; a variable positive lower bound yields the corresponding cohomological vanishing. The hypotheses include a Kähler metric and a smooth plurisubharmonic exhaustion. Local vanishing for multiplier ideals under a given sequence of smooth blowups then leads to cohomological descent. Existence of a log resolution remains the separately identified principalization input.

The current-theoretic arguments in [DemAnalyticOnline] lead to two distinct extensions. Restricting a closed positive current to a locally complete pluripolar set uses bounded plurisubharmonic cutoffs and trace-measure convergence even when the set is not closed. Real submersion slicing instead begins with a locally flat current, uses one null exceptional set for a countable family of tests, and retains the orientation sign in the differential. The real statement requires neither positivity nor closedness; its flat-convergence assertion concerns the specified subsequence.

For a finite group acting on a linearized line bundle, the product over the full group orbit turns a section into an invariant section in degree multiplied by the group order. This gives the precise comparison of algebraic threshold infima, with the empty-system convention retained. Equality with an analytic invariant is a separate conditional consequence of the stated approximation interface; equivariance of a Monge–Ampère equation alone does not supply that interface.

Generic sums of powers can be chosen with one coefficient tuple working in every degree. Gaussian small-ball estimates give full-sequence convergence, while a first-jet incidence argument controls vanishing orders at every point. The corresponding global construction in [DemAnalyticOnline] uses actual section spaces, a finite-dimensional Gaussian selection in each degree and a fixed auxiliary twist. It yields divisor approximation with uniform Lelong error in all required integer degrees; strict positivity removes the twist in all sufficiently large degrees. The smooth and real Néron–Severi consequences retain their separate hypotheses.
