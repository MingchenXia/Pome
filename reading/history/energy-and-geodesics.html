Energy measures more than the total mass of a potential: it records how the potential interacts with its own mixed Monge–Ampère measures. Cegrell's local classes and the global finite-energy theory of Guedj–Zeriahi made it possible to work with singular functions while retaining integration by parts and comparison. Relative energy uses a chosen reference singularity, allowing the same questions to be asked in a prescribed type. This is the starting point of energy, geodesics, rays, and test curves.

The geometric interpretation comes from the space of Kähler metrics. Mabuchi, Semmes, and Donaldson connected its formal Riemannian geometry with a homogeneous complex Monge–Ampère equation on a product. Chen established weak geodesics for smooth endpoint data. Perron envelopes supply a pluripotential construction of these paths: boundary values become obstacles, and maximality becomes a nonlinear equation. Endpoint convergence is part of this construction and needs to be established in the topology relevant to the endpoints.

Darvas's work on metric geometry and finite-energy completions gives singular potentials an intrinsic metric interpretation. The extension to big classes and relative settings was developed further by Darvas–Di Nezza–Lu and others. Completeness, geodesic existence, and energy continuity rely on the full-mass or finite-energy assumptions appropriate to the metric. A path defined by an envelope need not automatically have every metric or endpoint property of a smooth geodesic.

Geodesic rays lead from paths between two potentials to asymptotic geometry. Ross–Witt Nyström's analytic test configurations relate rays and test curves through a Legendre transform. The convex parameter on the ray side becomes a singularity cutoff on the curve side. Relative energy and mass profiles then describe slopes and asymptotic invariants. This duality helps explain the appearance of convex analysis in a theory initially formulated with complex Hessians.

The Hermitian contribution investigates how much of this metric picture survives when the background is not closed. The local envelope construction remains meaningful, but torsion can obstruct energy identities that in the Kähler case follow from Stokes' theorem. For this reason the book distinguishes metric completeness under its stated hypotheses from the existence of a pointwise envelope, and ordinary singularity control from multiplier-ideal control. These distinctions also matter when comparing analytic rays with the algebraic and non-Archimedean objects discussed next.
