Local positive currents become global geometric objects through their cohomology classes. On a compact Kähler manifold, the $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm c}$-lemma identifies closed currents in a fixed real $(1,1)$-class with quasi-plurisubharmonic potentials, up to a constant on each connected component. This is the bridge from the local theory to cohomology, positivity, and geometric loci. Nefness, bigness, and pseudo-effectivity describe different kinds of positivity; their geometric loci record where positive representatives can or cannot be made regular.

Hermitian linear algebra, Chern connections and elliptic estimates support the Hodge and vanishing theory in Demailly's book. The Frölicher spectral sequence compares Dolbeault and de Rham cohomology on general complex manifolds. Bott–Chern and Aeppli groups retain further information, while the Kähler identities impose stronger relations. The Bochner–Kodaira identity and its torsion terms distinguish the Hermitian calculation from its Kähler specialization.

The $L^2$ method of Hörmander, holomorphic division and compact vanishing theorems connect curvature to global sections and jets. Kodaira’s embedding theorem and its integral-curvature formulation then relate positivity to projectivity. Unique continuation is a separate analytic ingredient: Aronszajn's theorem is a classical source for scalar-principal second-order equations and coupled lower-order inequalities. Its hypotheses do not assert unrestricted unique continuation for every matrix-principal elliptic system.

Singular Hermitian metrics and multiplier ideals connect these estimates to coherent cohomology. Nadel's vanishing theorem and Skoda's division theorem are central instances, developed together in Demailly's Analytic Methods in Algebraic Geometry. The compact singular-metric argument combines a local weighted Dolbeault resolution with decreasing regularization, estimates in the original Kähler metric, analytic removability and weak limits. Its compact Kähler conclusion leaves vanishing on a general noncompact or singular space as a separate question.

Quotient-bundle curvature also gives division estimates for vector-valued forms. The Griffiths semipositive variant uses one copy of the ambient determinant bundle to control the trace term in the kernel curvature; the earlier lifting theorem assumes stronger ambient rank positivity. Projective division with a singular metric instead uses the scalar quotient theorem and decreasing regularization. Its quantitative conclusions retain both the integrability exponent and the dimension of the chosen finite system.

Pliś's smooth elliptic counterexample provides a concrete obstruction. Its fourth-order two-variable construction can be prolonged to an ordinary second-order elliptic system with a nonzero smooth solution vanishing on an open set. This separates general matrix-principal ellipticity from the scalar-principal hypotheses used for harmonic forms.

The spectral proof of holomorphic Morse inequalities uses a different asymptotic from the transcendental subtraction of nef classes. Demailly's magnetic-field method counts low Dolbeault eigenvalues and gives weak and strong line-power cohomology inequalities with a fixed coefficient bundle on compact Hermitian manifolds. Its semipositive specialization yields the leading holomorphic-section growth without assuming a Kähler base.

The order of spectral limits matters. The discussion in Analytic Methods in Algebraic Geometry also considers varying unitary connections. In the example on $\mathbb P^1\times\mathbb P^1$, arbitrarily small nonintegrable perturbations remove the holomorphic kernel. Choosing the threshold below the first positive eigenvalue then produces a different joint limit. The example does not change the fixed-threshold asymptotic for holomorphic tensor powers.

Siu's work on semipositive line bundles over non-Kähler manifolds includes the one-point positivity variant in an added-in-proof announcement. Its geometric application joins section growth to Kähler modifications and coefficient-valued cohomological descent. The existence input for eliminating the meromorphic map is stated separately from the analytic and descent proofs.

Hopf quotients illustrate the topological restrictions imposed by the Kähler condition. The normal-coordinate curvature expansion and Wirtinger’s inequality connect the local Hermitian metric to curvature and complex-volume geometry in Demailly's exposition.

Relative canonical positivity and algebraicity of foliations connect these analytic tools with birational geometry. Cao and Păun develop this connection on compact Kähler manifolds, drawing on Ou's restricted-singularity concentration and movable-slope arguments. The smooth-center Lelong comparison, flat-twist section envelope, anisotropic order estimate, compact-center potential and uniform submanifold mean-value bound support the local deductions. The relative canonical theorem retains its rational Hodge and fiber-vanishing hypotheses; the positive-foliation criterion combines conormal positivity with separate quotient and rational-connectedness inputs.

The variation theory originates in Berndtsson and Păun's Bergman kernels and Păun and Takayama's relative pluricanonical metrics. The latter input uses the precise formulation of arXiv:1409.5504v1. Hacon, Popa and Schnell, in arXiv:1611.08768v2, provide the multiplier-ideal direct-image formulation needed for singular tautological metrics. The projective-fiber section argument is related to Campana, Cao and Păun's work on direct images; the singular-metric vanishing and diagonal multiplier-ideal calculations use Demailly's analytic framework. Local projectivity also uses Grauert's analytic base-change and Euler-characteristic theorems, read with his correction: coherence of a direct image alone does not give the required fiberwise base-change isomorphism.

Campana and Păun's positive-slope arguments relate destabilizing tangent sheaves to rationally connected leaves. Toma's appendix to Campana and Peternell supplies the projective semistability context; Ou's compact-Kähler tensor theorem provides the movable-class interface used here, including boundary classes. Its reduction and boundedness inputs remain separate from the finite Harder–Narasimhan, Hom-vanishing and tensor-slope deductions. Exterior-power slope multiplication is used for semistable factors, with its hypothesis retained.

Campana's cycle-space constructions supply geometric flattening and relative rational quotients. The local leaf-relation deduction uses the two projections distinctly: fiber cycles from the first are pushed forward by the second. The underlying cycle-family existence, pushforward and compactness theorems remain explicit inputs. The Graber–Harris–Starr section theorem is applied after a Kähler-to-projective reduction, while Boucksom–Demailly–Păun–Peternell's projective canonical uniruledness implication is distinguished from cone duality. Fujiki's deformation theorem gives the family application and also supplies the passage from very general to all fibers on the required smooth connected base open. The rational Hodge choice and the comparison of saturated differential images account for the local reductions.

These attributions distinguish locally developed deductions from their stated analytic and geometric theorem inputs. The general relative-canonical pseudo-effectivity conjecture and the numerical-dimension question for a maximal rationally connected quotient remain open statements. Exact consulted preprints and author manuscripts are distinguished from later published bibliographic identities; no equivalence with an unread edition is asserted.

Boucksom's divisorial Zariski decomposition distinguishes the divisorial negative part of a pseudoeffective class from its movable positivity. Orthogonality and the derivative of volume express this structure numerically. The differentiability formula of Boucksom–Favre–Jonsson, and its transcendental extension through Witt Nyström and Boucksom, identify the gradient with the positive product of degree one less than the dimension. Cao–Tosatti establish the sharp local $C^{1,1}$ regularity once this formula is available. Their elementary cone estimate uses homogeneity and monotonicity, so the local Lipschitz bound for the gradient need not be deduced from a concavity theorem. The volume extends by zero across the non-big locus; its regularity there is a separate assertion from differentiability inside the big cone.

Exceptional divisors distinguish numerical negativity from a pointwise curvature condition. A negative definite intersection matrix does not force an arbitrary effective combination of its components to have negative degree on every component. The twice-blown-up projective-plane example exhibits this obstruction while admitting ample approximations with explicit volumes. Its first-cohomology calculation also retains both mixed terms in the square of a divisor class. These calculations isolate two steps in the surface-volume arguments of Demailly's exposition; they do not refute the separate asymptotic cohomology theorem.

For nef transcendental classes on compact Kähler manifolds, Tosatti's sharp Morse inequality supplies the optimal subtraction bound for volume. Its current-concentration and separation argument extends the projective numerical picture to the full real $(1,1)$-space. Together with simultaneous analytic approximation, the inequality yields volume differentiation, orthogonality, and duality of the pseudoeffective and movable cones. The separating classes may already be taken as pushforwards of single Kähler powers from modifications. The spectral holomorphic Morse inequalities discussed earlier count a different cohomological asymptotic.

Restricted volumes record positivity along a specified analytic subvariety. Collins–Tosatti develop their numerical analytic formulation and its relation to non-Kähler loci. Witt Nyström's deformation to the normal bundle and Vu's work on divisorial derivatives connect these invariants with the first variation of volume. The formulation must retain the distinction between ordinary restriction outside the non-Kähler locus and the numerical extension to a subvariety in that locus. Birational reduction and the contribution of divisorial negative parts are part of that comparison.

Balanced metrics provide a smooth representative for another face of the same duality. Toma establishes the relevant projective cone comparison, and Fu–Xiao develop the compact Kähler version. Their separation arguments test closed strictly positive $(n-1,n-1)$-forms against positive $\partial\bar\partial$-closed currents. The behavior of these currents under modifications is studied by Alessandrini–Bassanelli's work on balanced manifolds and their transforms of currents. Lifting these currents under a modification requires an additional theorem: $\partial\bar\partial$-closedness alone does not provide a local plurisubharmonic potential for pullback. This analytic input enters the identification of the closure of the balanced cone with the movable cone.

Hermitian geometry brings a different cohomological structure. Bott–Chern and Aeppli classes retain information lost when one uses only de Rham cohomology, and their pairing is natural for positivity questions. Gauduchon metrics satisfy $\partial\bar\partial\omega^{n-1}=0$, a weaker condition than the Kähler condition $\mathrm d\omega=0$. Gauduchon's existence theorem makes this condition available within a conformal class. Lamari's positivity criterion uses dual testing against Gauduchon forms; see Lamari. These ideas organize Hermitian geometry, torsion, and Gauduchon duality.

The absence of closedness changes the meaning of a volume. In the Kähler setting, Stokes' theorem fixes the mass of bounded potentials in a class. In the Hermitian setting, upper mass bounds and positive lower mass bounds are separate properties of a background. Work of Guedj–Lu and subsequent developments studies these bounds and their role in equations and singularity volumes. The distinction between a bound for each individual potential and a uniform bound over a family is essential. It is also why a theorem for a closed big class cannot be transferred verbatim to an arbitrary positive Hermitian form.

The chapter on Gauduchon variational volume develops an additional variational viewpoint using Gauduchon data and duality. It should be read alongside, rather than identified with, the cohomological volume of a closed class or the mass attached to an ordinary singularity type. The historical strands meet in their use of positivity and nonlinear measures, but their admissible objects and normalizations differ. Several results in the Hermitian contribution belong to a working research exposition; original references and precise source revisions remain under Sources.

The first gentle trace separates an intrinsic scalar mass from a cohomology-valued construction. Its resolved degree-one bridge identifies the first mobile class with the divisorial projection, while the scalar comparison uses strict gentle approximations of minimal potentials. Zero-trace rigidity, finite divisorial comparison and countability retain their individual hypotheses. The negative part of any pseudoeffective class has finite, linearly independent support; the resulting projection is idempotent. No general higher-degree cohomology product is inferred from these assertions.

Morse index infima are invariant under modifications by bounded-potential tail control and descent away from the exceptional set. In the projective setting, rational Picard decompositions and constant-trace metrics identify section volume, non-pluripolar class volume and the degree-zero Morse infimum. The qualitative orthogonality argument and the nonbig limiting case are retained. This does not supply uniform control for every jet-dependent sequence of decompositions.

The nef higher-cohomology conclusion is already supplied by the canonical growth theorem, including its stronger projective estimate. The complementary all-index determinant bound and finite truncated polynomial identity give every algebraic Morse inequality for a difference of nef line bundles, with the rank of a fixed auxiliary vector bundle retained. One first lets the tensor power tend to infinity for a fixed metric, then removes the nef perturbation. This repairs the signed-integral and full-sum shortcuts in [DemAnalyticOnline] without assuming a remainder uniform over varying metrics.

The moving-product construction in [Section 19, DemAnalyticOnline] uses resolved gentle currents, simultaneous weakly positive tests and bounded-mass compactness to give cohomology-valued products in every degree. Principalization of the individual ideals and their sum makes the filtering operation precise. Existence of the required resolutions and principalizations remains the declared external input. The scalar trace identity connects these classes with the canonical positive intersection numbers and identifies the two numerical dimensions.

Restriction to a general member of a covering analytic cycle family, followed by constancy of its homology class, gives the moving-cycle pairing. The sharp nef Morse inequality gives the explicit factor twenty in the orthogonality error; finite divisorial support and decreasing boundary products give boundary orthogonality. These latter conclusions are short consequences of the existing positivity theory. A separate global primitive proves the limit of every power of one regularized divisor current, including singular divisors and rational coefficients. It makes no claim about the signatures of the approximating forms.

The numerical Kähler characterization of Demailly and Păun [DP04], presented in [Section 18, DemAnalyticOnline], follows here from an induction giving smooth ambient positivity near analytic subsets. Strict currents, logarithmic poles and patching of the lower-dimensional bad set precede one shared normal-Hessian correction. The reconstruction retains the existing resolution/principalization existence boundary. Cone topology gives the connected-component statement; proper hyperplane cuts give the projective specialization; the positive polynomial kernel gives the linear nef tests and dual cones.

The surface curve-neighborhood argument has the broader ambient hypothesis of a compact complex surface. Normalization, the curve Poisson equation and logarithmic poles give its direct construction, followed by the same normal-correction lemma. The ample-plus-effective nef theorem is retained as an elementary alternative to a consequence of the general criterion. It gives an acyclic route to a finite nef decomposition and the numerical surface jet theorem of [Exercise 7.14, DemAnalyticOnline], with a strict metric and the required local multiplier-ideal zero set. Curve nefness in this argument is proved to supply the needed smooth metrics; it is not silently replaced by metric nefness.
