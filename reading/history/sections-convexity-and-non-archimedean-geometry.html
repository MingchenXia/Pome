Singular metrics impose integrability conditions on holomorphic sections. Counting sections satisfying those conditions gives an algebraic measure of positivity, while partial Bergman kernels give an analytic measure of their distribution. The chapters on sections, graded series, and Okounkov bodies and multiplier-ideal models and partial equilibrium study how these quantities compare with nonlinear masses and envelopes. Multiplier ideals are the interface: they turn the singular weight into an ideal defining the allowed sections.

Uniform generation of multiplier ideals appears in Siu's work on plurigenera and in Demailly's Analytic Methods in Algebraic Geometry. Pome's proof passes from compact Nadel vanishing to an analytic Castelnuovo–Mumford criterion. A Koszul complex of hyperplanes through a point and its two hypercohomology spectral sequences establish the criterion for coherent analytic sheaves on projective complex spaces, including nonreduced ones. The resulting twist is independent of the singular metric. It also supplies the uniform-generation step in analytifying a complex plurisubharmonic potential.

The comparison between Kodaira and numerical dimensions of a nef line bundle is treated on a compact Kähler manifold. Principalizing the base ideal gives a movable semipositive class, and a mixed-intersection inequality compares it with the nef class. This argument requires no ample divisor on the original manifold. Smooth-center principalization remains an explicit geometric input; preservation of the Kähler property and the intersection comparison enter separately.

For canonical rings, division gives a quantitative sufficient condition involving the singularity-approximation coefficient and the size of the chosen finite pluricanonical system. A double-cover surface and its blow-up show why those factors matter: the canonical ring can require a degree-four generator even when the complete degree-one system has minimal singularities and finite integrability threshold. This example concerns the proposed numerical generation bound in Demailly's exposition, while finite generation itself remains intact.

Weighted Jensen formulas also lead to Schwarz-type bounds for entire functions vanishing on a finite set. Demailly's exposition relates polynomial degree, singular degree and weighted multiplicity in these inequalities. Waldschmidt's account develops the connection with transcendence and algebraic groups, including the analytic method behind Bombieri's arithmetic zero-set theorem. A coarse degree bound, the optimal asymptotic Schwarz coefficient and an exceptional-locus refinement are different assertions. Star configurations of hyperplanes realize the proposed degree bound with equality in every dimension. This special case leaves the general historical conjectures separate from the proved estimates.

Okounkov's construction associates convex sets to asymptotic orders of vanishing. The volume formulas of Lazarsfeld–Mustaţă and Kaveh–Khovanskii relate Euclidean volumes of these bodies to growth of graded linear series and semigroups. Passing from an algebraic series to sections constrained by analytic singularities requires control of the relevant ideals and asymptotic growth. Pome develops the semigroup and convex arguments alongside these applications. The lattice normalization, including the factor $n!$ in the volume formula, is part of the statement.

Toric geometry provides a particularly explicit bridge. The work of Atiyah, Delzant, Guillemin, Abreu, and Donaldson connects toric Kähler geometry with moment polytopes and convex functions. Pluripotential formulations were developed by Berman–Berndtsson and Coman–Guedj–Şahin–Zeriahi. In toric and convex geometry, Legendre transforms turn invariant potentials into convex data, and complex Monge–Ampère measures into real Monge–Ampère measures. This dictionary makes singularity order, rooftops, and mass calculations concrete while keeping track of their domains and boundary behavior.

B-divisors and restriction organize information over different birational models. Instead of selecting one resolution permanently, a b-divisor records compatible data on models. Divisorial valuations then detect orders of singularity that a fixed coordinate chart or fixed model may miss. Restricting a singular potential is a separate operation: a trace must account for poles and for the positivity available on the subvariety. These constructions connect the analytic theory of singularities with intersection-theoretic data.

Polynomial approximation gives a local and affine counterpart of these constructions. Siciak and Zaharjuta relate polynomial growth to pluripotential extremals; Siciak's product theorem and the real-body formulas of Lundin and Baran are presented in Klimek. Pleśniak's survey develops extremal functions and geometric regularity, while Kołodziej studies logarithmic capacity and Robin-function comparisons. Pome's open-semianalytic regularity proof uses real analytic principalization in the form supplied by Bierstone–Milman; this geometric theorem remains an explicit external input.

Bedford–Taylor's study of symmetric real convex bodies connects their extremal functions with explicit equilibrium densities and boundary mass. Pome develops the real Monge–Ampère calculation and the convex foliation alongside these formulas. For arbitrary rough symmetric convex bodies, the boundary-mass proof uses a projective dilation and the bounded energy cocycle; it does not infer absence of boundary mass from a limit of interior densities. This connects the affine formulas with the compact energy calculus.

Non-Archimedean pluripotential theory places related information on valuation spaces and Berkovich spaces. Work of Boucksom–Favre–Jonsson, their later Monge–Ampère theory, and Boucksom–Jonsson develops positivity, envelopes, and energy in this setting. Model functions and test configurations supply algebraic approximants, while analytic rays provide an asymptotic comparison. These correspondences require precise hypotheses, including those governing mass and homogeneity; they should not be inferred merely from the repeated use of the words “potential” or “model.”

Effective adjoint generation in [DemAnalyticOnline] combines coherent Euler polynomials, numerical large-value bounds and singular Nadel vanishing. The construction keeps the entire nonreduced quotient during induction and uses all monomials of the base-point-free embedding in the metric. These choices give the stated simultaneous-jet bounds, including the empty prescribed-point list, without replacing finite-length conditions by their reduced supports.

The Kodaira-dimension inequality in [DemAnalyticOnline] extends beyond nef line bundles when numerical dimension is defined by the canonical scalar positive intersections. A finite section system gives a positive singular current with positive mixed mass at its differential rank. The section-growth jet estimate then relates this rank to algebraic independence of section ratios. The scalar definition is reused from the existing numerical-dimension theory; no unrestricted higher-degree cohomology-valued positive product is presumed.

The point-divisor products used in [Remark 10.3, DemAnalyticOnline] already have a section in degree one. A positive-power comparison uses different degree-one bundles: finite Abel-image avoidance chooses a bundle whose first effective positive power is the genus. Curve Riemann–Roch, the Picard-period torus and the Abel map are reused from the existing curve theory. Their products give the exact first exponent and a coefficient obstruction of one half the dimension when the additive term is fixed. Explicit double covers supply unbounded genera. This corrected example concerns positive powers; it does not establish sharpness of a corollary that allows the zeroth power.

The family attributed to Xiao in [pp. 8–9, FdB94Matsusaka] uses a double cover of a Hirzebruch surface. An even parameter gives the integral square root of its branch class. The construction reuses the existing ruled-surface calculation and the local double-cover algebra; projectivity, ampleness, canonical intersections and the restricted curve sections are checked explicitly. The least very-ample exponent is bounded below by one half the parameter and above by nine quarters of it plus a bounded term. These bounds have the same growth order as the canonical-intersection ratio. This is an order comparison, not an assertion that an upper-bound coefficient is optimal. The cited source is the exact 1994 preprint version.

The effective argument in [Section 10, DemAnalyticOnline] uses the locally square-integrable canonical sheaf of a reduced pure-dimensional projective variety. Its degree-zero resolution formula, finite birational comparison, and adjunction quotient are proved explicitly. The cutting recursion gives the stated very-ampleness bound in dimension at least two. It neither identifies this sheaf with an arbitrary dualizing sheaf nor extends the dimension-two-and-higher formula to curves.
