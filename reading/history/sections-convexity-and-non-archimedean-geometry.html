Singular metrics impose integrability conditions on holomorphic sections. Counting sections satisfying those conditions gives an algebraic measure of positivity, while partial Bergman kernels give an analytic measure of their distribution. The chapters on sections, graded series, and Okounkov bodies and multiplier-ideal models and partial equilibrium study how these quantities compare with nonlinear masses and envelopes. Multiplier ideals are the interface: they turn the singular weight into an ideal defining the allowed sections.

Okounkov's construction associates convex sets to asymptotic orders of vanishing. The volume formulas of Lazarsfeld–Mustaţă and Kaveh–Khovanskii relate Euclidean volumes of these bodies to growth of graded linear series and semigroups. Passing from an algebraic series to sections constrained by analytic singularities requires control of the relevant ideals and asymptotic growth. Pome develops the semigroup and convex arguments alongside these applications. The lattice normalization, including the factor $n!$ in the volume formula, is part of the statement.

Toric geometry provides a particularly explicit bridge. The work of Atiyah, Delzant, Guillemin, Abreu, and Donaldson connects toric Kähler geometry with moment polytopes and convex functions. Pluripotential formulations were developed by Berman–Berndtsson and Coman–Guedj–Şahin–Zeriahi. In toric and convex geometry, Legendre transforms turn invariant potentials into convex data, and complex Monge–Ampère measures into real Monge–Ampère measures. This dictionary makes singularity order, rooftops, and mass calculations concrete while keeping track of their domains and boundary behavior.

B-divisors and restriction organize information over different birational models. Instead of selecting one resolution permanently, a b-divisor records compatible data on models. Divisorial valuations then detect orders of singularity that a fixed coordinate chart or fixed model may miss. Restricting a singular potential is a separate operation: a trace must account for poles and for the positivity available on the subvariety. These constructions connect the analytic theory of singularities with intersection-theoretic data.

Polynomial approximation gives a local and affine counterpart of these constructions. Siciak and Zaharjuta relate polynomial growth to pluripotential extremals; Siciak's product theorem and the real-body formulas of Lundin and Baran are presented in Klimek. Pleśniak's survey develops extremal functions and geometric regularity, while Kołodziej studies logarithmic capacity and Robin-function comparisons. Pome's open-semianalytic regularity proof uses real analytic principalization in the form supplied by Bierstone–Milman; this geometric theorem remains an explicit external input.

Bedford–Taylor's study of symmetric real convex bodies connects their extremal functions with explicit equilibrium densities and boundary mass. Pome develops the real Monge–Ampère calculation and the convex foliation alongside these formulas. For arbitrary rough symmetric convex bodies, the boundary-mass proof uses a projective dilation and the bounded energy cocycle; it does not infer absence of boundary mass from a limit of interior densities. This connects the affine formulas with the compact energy calculus.

Non-Archimedean pluripotential theory places related information on valuation spaces and Berkovich spaces. Work of Boucksom–Favre–Jonsson, their later Monge–Ampère theory, and Boucksom–Jonsson develops positivity, envelopes, and energy in this setting. Model functions and test configurations supply algebraic approximants, while analytic rays provide an asymptotic comparison. These correspondences require precise hypotheses, including those governing mass and homogeneity; they should not be inferred merely from the repeated use of the words “potential” or “model.”
